ABSTRACT 
Justus Haataja: The capabilities and limitations of AI agents in software development

The rapid advancement of Large Language Models (LLMs) has initiated a paradigm shift in 
software engineering, transitioning prompt-based assistants to autonomous AI agents . The new 
AI models like GPT-5.4 and Claude Opus 4.6 are capable of agentic coding. Traditional manual 
programming is labor -intensive and time -consuming, whereas autonomous agents offer dele-
gated autonomy by independently interpreting high-level goals and executing complex, multi-step 
development workflows. As these systems evolve from research prototypes to industrial tools, a 
comprehensive evaluation of their practical effectiveness and associated risks is necessary. 
 
This thesis conducts a literature review of the current landscape of autonomous AI agents 
within the field of software engineering. The analysis investigates the conceptual foundations, 
technical architectures, and empirical performance data associated with prominent agentic frame-
works. The review specifically traces the technological evolution of AI assistance, moving from 
the intuitive human -AI co-creation found in so called vibe coding to advanced agentic systems. 
These contemporary systems employ specialized planners, executors, and AI toolchain modules 
to manage tasks across the entire software development life cycle. 
 
The results indicate that autonomous agents possess significant capabilities, including th e 
ability to perform task decomposition, orchestrate development tools like compilers and version 
control systems, and conduct self-reflective error correction [7][17]. Empirical evidence suggests 
that, in certain research settings, agent-based AI tools have increased developer productivity by 
up to 50 % in routine programming tasks, and in some controlled settings accelerate the imple-
mentation of features from hours to minutes [7][18]. However, the generalizability of these findings 
to real-world software development environments remains partially uncertain, as the observed 
effects are strongly dependent on the research setup, task type, and development environment 
used. Beyond these uncertainties, operational limitations are also evident. Agents are prone to 
hallucinations, often generating non-existent library references or skipping critical project goals. 
Their success rates drop sharply as task complexity increases, and their autonomous actions 
introduce new security vectors such as secret leakage and dependency confusion. Furthermore, 
the opaque nature of their decision-making processes poses challenges for architectural integrity 
and human oversight. [11] 
 
The thesis concludes that while AI agents are revolutionary tools for automating repetitive 
engineering tasks and rapid prototyping, they currently function best as intelligent collaborators 
rather than total replacements for human engineers. Strategic human supervision remains essen-
tial to mitigate reliability issues and ensure that agent -generated code aligns with long-term sys-
tem maintainability and security standards. 
 
Keywords: AI Agents, Artificial Intelligence, Software development

KEY CONCEPTS 
Artificial Intelligence, AI 
- A field within computer science that aims to develop intelligent systems capable 
of carrying out tasks that normally require human intelligence, including natural 
language understanding, pattern recognition, and decision-making. [1][2] 
 
Agentic AI 
- An intelligent computational entity designed to achieve specific goals by inde-
pendently interpreting instructions, planning multi-step strategies, and executing 
actions within a dynamic environment. [6] Agents are proactive, meaning they 
can generate their own pro mpts and manage complex workflows with minimal 
human oversight [19]. 
 
Generative Artificial Intelligence, GenAI 
- A category of AI models and algorithms designed to generate original content. 
The content can be e.g. text, code, images, and video, that matches learned pat-
terns from existing datasets like human created content. [3][17] 
 
Large Language Model, LLM 
- A class of generative AI systems trained on large-scale textual datasets and con-
sisting of billions of parameters. These models produce natural language output 
by estimating the probability distribution of word sequences given a particular 
context. [18] Prominent examples include models like GPT, Claude, and Gemini, 
which are used as the cognitive cores for autonomous coding tasks. 
 
LLM-Based Multi-Agent, LMA 
- Systems that integrate LLMs at the core of multiple autonomous agents to lever-
age synergistic collaboration. These systems allow specialized agents to work 
together toward a common shared goal, such as through debate or cross-exam-
ination, to improve robustness, factuality, and reasoning beyond the limits of a 
single model. [6] 
 
Multi-Agent System, MAS 
- A computational framework where multiple interacting intelligent agents com-
municate and coordinate to solve complex problems that are beyond the capa-
bility of any single agent. These systems are inherently scalable and adaptive, 
making them ideal for managing large volumes of code or complex software re-
quirements. [16] 
 
Software Development Life Cycle, SDLC 
- A structured framework providing the phases for managing software projects, 
typically including requirement engineering, system design, coding, testing, and 
deployment. [21] LMA systems are frequently designed to mirror or automate 
these life cycles using established models like Waterfall or Agile [6].

1 INTRODUCTION 
The rapid development of Large Language Models (LLMs) has initiated a fundamental 
transformation in the field of software engineering. Current flagship models such as GPT-
5.4, and Claude Opus 4.6 have moved beyond simple text prediction to become sophis-
ticated tools capable of understanding and generating complex code structures [21]. This 
evolution is significant for both academic research and practical engineering, as it prom-
ises to redefine how software is designed, implemented, and maintained. By abstracting 
the linguistic burden of programming, these technologies allow developers to focus on 
high-level architectural vision and intent rather than low-level syntactic details, potentially 
addressing the growing global demand for software and the corresponding shortage of 
skilled personnel [21]. 
 However, a significant shift is currently underway, moving from traditional reactive AI 
coding assistants toward autonomous AI agents. While early tools operated primarily in 
an autocomplete or co -pilot role requiring constant human prompting for every line of 
code, modern autonomous agents are designed for delegated autonomy. These systems 
are capable of independ ently interpreting high-level goals, decomposing them into ac-
tionable sub-tasks, and executing multi-step development workflows with minimal human 
oversight [21]. Evaluating these systems is critically important because, despite their 
rapid adoption, there remains a research gap regarding their practical reliability in com-
plex, real-world software development life cycles. The integration of autonomous agents 
introduces practical challenges, particularly in maintaining architectural integrity and ad-
dressing the limited transparency of agentic decision -making, which together require a 
comprehensive evaluation of such systems. 
 Existing research spans three main stran ds: LLM -based code generation, agentic 
workflows, and multi -agent systems. Initial studies demonstrated that models like 
OpenAI Codex could solve isolated algorithmic problems with high accuracy [21]. This 
laid the groundwork for agentic workflows, such as Auto-GPT, which connect LLM-gen-
erated reasoning process to autonomously accomplish assigned tasks such as script 
execution and debugging [21]. More recently, research has pivoted toward LLM -based 
multi-agent systems (LMA s), where specialized agents acting as architects, program-
mers, and testers, collaborate to solve more complex challenges. These collaborative 
frameworks, such as ChatDev and MetaGPT, have shown that agents can monitor each 
other's outputs through iterative inter -agent debate and structured review process,

thereby improving robustness and reducing errors compared to singular agent models 
[21]. 
 Despite this progress, the literature remains fragmented across architectures, evalua-
tion methodologies, and application contexts, limiting a consoli dated understanding of 
real-world capabilities and constraints. This thesis addresses this gap by systematically 
synthesizing empirical findings on autonomous software engineering agents, organizing 
them by capabilities, limitations, and evaluation context s. The objective is to identify 
where evidence is robust, where findings are consistent, and where further validation is 
required. 
 The methodology used in this thesis is a literature review, which is used to analyze 
and synthesize the current state of AI agents in the field of software engineering. This 
approach is particularly suitable for this research problem because the field of agentic 
coding is evolving at an unprecedented pace. A literature review enables the structured 
collection and critical evalu ation of recent empirical data, framework descriptions, and 
case studies, providing a n overview of a rapidly evolving technological landscape that 
may be difficult to capture through traditional experimental methods in real time. 
 The primary objective of this research is to identify the practical boundaries of AI au-
tonomy in engineering. Consequently, this thesis seeks to answer the following research 
question: What are the core capabilities and inherent limitations of autonomous AI agents 
when applied to the software development lifecycle? 
 This thesis provides an overview of current research on autonomous AI agents in soft-
ware engineering, focusing on their reported capabilities and limitations in the literature. 
The analysis covers areas such as task aut omation, code generation, debugging, and 
multi-agent collaboration, as well as commonly reported challenges including reliability, 
security concerns, and evaluation difficulties. 
 This thesis is structured into several chapters that guide the reader through the anal-
ysis. Chapter 2 presents the methodology used to select and analyze the relevant liter-
ature. Chapter 3 examines the capabilities of AI agents in software development, includ-
ing task automation and decomposition, code generation, debugging, produc tivity im-
provements, and multi-agent collaboration. Chapter 4 discusses the main limitations and 
challenges identified in the literature, such as reliability issues, security and privacy con-
cerns, limitations in complex tasks, and difficulties in evaluatin g agent performance. 
Chapter 5 interprets the findings and discusses their implications for software engineer-
ing practices. Finally, Chapter 6 summarizes the main conclusions of the study.

2 METHODOLOGY 
This bachelor’s thesis was conducted as a literature re view with the aim of providing a 
structured and critical overview of the capabilities and limitations of AI agents in software 
development. The study is guided by the following research question: What are the ca-
pabilities and limitations of AI agents in software development? A literature review is an 
appropriate methodological choice due to the novelty and rapid evolution of the topic, as 
well as the fragmented nature of existing research. Currently, no consolidated under-
standing of the phenomenon exists, making synthesis of prior studies necessary. 
 The research data were collected from several scientific databases and publication 
platforms, including Scopus AI, Google Scholar, and the JUFO portal. Initial searches 
were conducted between January and Febru ary 2026 using Scopus AI, based directly 
on the research question: “ What are the capabilities and limitations of autonomous AI 
agents in software development?”. This initial search produced 12 relevant sources. 
 To broaden the scope of the dataset, additio nal searches were conducted using the 
keywords “AI software development”, “AI agents”, and “AI agents in software develop-
ment”. These searches were complemented with the following Boolean query: 
(“artificial intelligence” OR “AI” OR “machine learning” OR “ deep learning”) AND (“soft-
ware development” OR “programming” OR “coding” OR “application development”) AND 
(“algorithm” OR “model” OR “framework” OR “tool”) AND (“automation” OR “optimization” 
OR “integration” OR “deployment”) AND (“testing” OR “debugging” OR “quality assur-
ance” OR “validation”). 
 These complementary searches identified 13 additional relevant sources. Google 
Scholar was additionally used to verify publication years, support citation formatting, and 
identify related studies through its recom mendation system. Suggested articles were 
considered for inclusion if they satisfied the predefined relevance and quality criteria, 
including evaluation through the JUFO publication forum classification system. Temporal 
filtering was applied to prioritize recent studies, and only publications from 2020 onwards 
were included, with particular emphasis placed on studies published after 2023 due to 
the rapid emergence and adoption of modern AI agent systems during this period. 
 Following the initial database searches, backward snowballing was applied by exam-
ining the reference lists of selected studies to identify further relevant literature. Source 
quality and publication legitimacy were evaluated using the JUFO publication forum clas-

 Preprints were included selectively because the field of AI agents in software engi-

peer-reviewed literature, particularly regarding task decomposition, autonomous work-
flow execution, productivity gains, and the limitations of current agentic systems. Never-
theless, the findings of preprint studies were interpreted cautiously and used primarily to 
complement, rather than replace, evidence from peer-reviewed sources. 
 All selected sources were published in English, reflecting both the international nature 
of the topic and the use of English-language search queries. During the search process, 
it became evident that research specifically addressing fully autonomous AI agents is 
still limited and largely concentrated in recent publications from 2024 –2025. Conse-
quently, the research scope was broadened by removing the restriction to fully autono-
mous agents, allowing the inclusion of partially autonomous and agent -based systems. 
This adjustment improved the coverage and representativeness of the dataset without 
altering the core focus of the study. 
 Source selection was based on three primary criteria: relevance to software develop-
ment, academic quality, and methodological credibility. First, only studies with a clear 
and explicit connection to software engineering or software development practices were 
included. Publications addressing AI agents in a general or theoretical context without 
direct applicability to software development workflows were excluded to ensure domain 
relevance. 
 Second, the peer-review status and publication venue quality were considered. Pref-
erence was given to articles published in established journals and conferences indexed

3 CAPABILITIES OF AI AGENTS 
AI agents have introduced significant advancements in software development by extend-
ing automation beyond simple assistance toward more autonomous and coordinated 
workflows. The literature highlights their ability to decompose complex tasks, generate 
code, and orchestrate multi-step development processes with minimal human interven-
tion. These capabilities are particularly evident in structured environments, where agents 
can transform high-level objectives into executable actions and support various stages 
of the software development lifecycle. 
 In addition, AI agents have been observed to demonstrate increasingly sophisticated 
behaviors such as self -correction, iterative improvement, and workflow acceleration. 
Their integration into development processes has been associated with improved effi-
ciency and productivity, especially in routine and well-defined tasks. The emergence of 
multi-agent systems is reported to further strengthen these capabilities by enabling role-
based collaboration and coordination between specialized agents. The following sec-
tions examine these capabilities in more det ail, focusing on task automation, self -cor-
recting mechanisms, workflow acceleration, and multi-agent architectures. 
 This chapter is structured around four interrelated capability areas of AI agents in soft-
ware development. Task decomposition and automatio n (Section 3.1) describes how 
agents translate high -level objectives into executable sub -tasks and structured work-
flows. Acceleration of development workflows (Section 3. 2) examines the measurable 
impact of agentic systems on development speed and productivity. Self-correcting capa-
bilities (Section 3.2) focus on post-generation reasoning mechanisms such as reflection, 
debugging, and iterative improvement. Finally, multi-agent systems (Section 3.4) extend 
single-agent capabilities through role specialization and coordination mechanisms. 
These themes were selected because they represent the commonly reported capability 
clusters in current literature and together cover both individual agent behavior and sys-
tem-level orchestration.

3.1 Task Decomposition and Automation 
The core capability that distinguishes autonomous AI agents from traditional predictive 
coding tools is their transition toward delegated autonomy, where they may interpret 
high-level goals and execute multi -step development workflows. Unlike reactiv e assis-
tants that require constant human prompting, autonomous systems use modular plan-
ning approaches to bridge the gap between abstract user intent and executable software. 
[19] 
 A fundamental strength of agentic systems is their ability to perform hiera rchical task 
decomposition, parsing complex natural language objectives into a sequence of struc-
tured sub-tasks [19]. In multi -agent frameworks like CodePori, a specialized Manager 
Agent acts as a central coordinator, analyzing project descriptions to identify necessary 
modules and components. This system ensures manageability by setting specific con-
straints, such as limiting individual modules to a maximum of 200 lines of code . [18] 
Similarly, the MetaGPT and FlowGen frameworks emulate established software process 
models by utilizing an Orchestrator to decompose goals into actionable sub -tasks and 
delegate them to specialized roles such as requirement engineers, architects, and de-
velopers. [6][7] This structural approach is consistent with human-centric agile and iter-
ative methodologies, allowing for the management of project interdependencies [6]. 
 Autonomous agents have been reported to enable the automation of the entire Soft-
ware Development Life Cycle (SDLC), ranging from requirements engineering and sys-
tem design to implementation and testing [17]. The autonomous agent workflow typically 
follows a loop comprising three roles: a Task Agent, a Prioritization Agent, and an Exe-
cution Agent. The Task Agent captures user objectives and populates a task list, the 
Prioritization Agent sets execution order, and the Execution Agent retrieves context from 
memory to complete the tasks. [21] These systems have been shown to support rapid 
prototyping; empirical studies report that multi-agent frameworks can develop functional 
applications, such as GUI -based games or chat platforms, in under ten minutes under 
controlled conditions. [17] 
 Furthermore, agents may autonomously interact with and orchestrate development 
tools, including compilers, interpreters, version control systems like Git, and web brows-
ers for research tasks. This tool use capability allows agents to clone repositories, modify 
source code, execute tests, and manage CI/CD pipelines without human intervention 
between steps. [19] By automating repetitive and technically demanding operations, au-
tonomous agents may allow human developers to shift their focus toward strategic ar-
chitecture and high-level decision-making. [7][15]

 Autonomous AI agents have also been reported to extend code generation from micro-

natural language prompts to generate code spanning front -end components, back-end

Reported results include small-scale applications, such as games or messaging plat-

RESTful API routes using Node.js [1]. 
 Several studies report 20–50 % improvements in developer efficiency, particularly in 
foundational and repetitive software development tasks [20]. Controlled experiments fur-

ments approximately 56 % faster than those working manually [3]. These systems can 
reduce cognitive load by providing real-time suggestions and explanations, allowing de-
velopers to focus on higher-level architectural concerns [15]. 
 Collaborative multi-agent architectures (LMAs) are reported to support this capability 
by assigning specialized roles to agents [18]. Roles include architect, developer, quality 
assurance engineer, designer, and project manager [8]. This specialization may improve 
robustness, as agents can iteratively refine outputs and verify that the final product meets 
expected performance criteria [17][18]. For instance, the CodePori system uses six spe-

proved performance in requirement-to-code translation compared to single-model base-

software [13]. 
 It should be noted that reported quantitative improvements depend strongly on exper-

89 % accuracy in CodePori were obtained on structured software engineering datasets

3.2 Acceleration of Development Workflows 
This section builds on the capabilities described in Section 3.1 by focusing on their meas-
urable impact rather than mechanisms. The integration of Large Language Models 
(LLMs) and autonomous agents is reported to be reshaping the Software Development 
Life Cycle (SDLC), shifting the paradigm from manual implementation toward high-level 
intent-driven orchestration. By automating complex reasoning and execution tasks, AI 
agents may function as AI teammates capable of initiating code changes and supporting 
end-to-end feature development with reduced human oversight. 
Development Speed 
Empirical evidence suggests that AI agents may significantly reduce the time required 
for coding and initial prototyping. Controlled experiments indicate that developers using 
AI-assisted tools such as GitHub Copilot can complete tasks 55.8 % faster than those 
working without such assistance [15]. Furthermore, multi -agent frameworks have 
demonstrated the ability to automate parts of the software lifecycle, including planning, 
design, and coding, within minutes under experimental conditions [17][18]. For example, 
the CodePori system can generate executable code for large-scale projects aligned with 
typical software engineering processes in reduced time compared to traditional work-
flows [18]. This acceleration is particularly associated with vibe coding workflows, which 
emphasize conversational ideation and rapid iteration over syntactic detail [19]. 
Task Efficiency and Workflow Optimization 
AI agents are reported to optimize workflows by streamlining routine, high-frequency de-
velopment activities that traditionally require substantial engineering effort. [3 ][9][15] 
These systems are particularly effective in boilerplate code generation, which reduces 
cognitive load and allows developers to focus on core logic and architectural concerns 
[15]. Beyond initial generation, AI agents may support systematic refactoring and legacy 
modernization by detecting outdated patterns and applying code transformations while 
maintaining behavioral consistency [13][19]. High-frequency maintenance tasks, such as

[[page 18]]

dependency updates, code formatting, and technical documentation generation, are in-
creasingly delegated to agents, contributing to reduced technical debt [19]. 
Productivity Gains and Multi-Agent Synergies 
The transition from individual LLM prompts to LLM -Based Multi-Agent (LMA) systems 
has been associated with productivity gains through collaboration specialization [6]. LMA 
systems operate by assigning specialized roles different agent instances, mirroring agile 
team structures [6][8]. This collaborative approach may improve robustness and fault 
tolerance as agents can engage in iterative cross-examination or peer-review cycles to 
detect errors early in the process [6]. 
 Recent metrics indicate that LMA frameworks such as CodePori may improve code 
accuracy and efficiency, contributing to increased software development productivity 
[18]. Multi-agent systems also provide scalability for com plex projects that may exceed 
the capacity of a single agent, as task graphs can be decomposed and distributed across 
specialized components [6]. Consequently, these systems may shift the role of human 
developers toward strategic supervision and system-level architecture design [15][19]. 
3.3 Self-Correcting Capabilities of AI Agents 
This section focuses on post-generation quality assurance mechanisms rather than ex-
ecution efficiency or workflow speed. A central feature of agentic AI in software engi-
neering is the capacity of Large Language Model -based Multi-Agent (LMA) systems to 
extend beyond code generation. These systems incorporate mechanisms such as sys-
tematic error detection, iterative self -reflection, and auto mated debugging, enabling 
agents to identify and refine outputs with reduced human intervention. 
Error Detection and Contextual Analysis 
AI agents are reported to identify a range of issues, from syntax errors to logical incon-
sistencies and design flaws. Unlike traditional assistants, agentic systems often use spe-
cialized roles where one agent acts as a programmer and another as a reviewer or au-
ditor, enabling iterative review process. For instance, systems like LOCALIZEAGENT 
use specialized agents combined with static analysis tools like PMD to detect design -
level issues such as poor modularity or excessive complexity. Furthermore, AI agents 
have been reported to detect up to 60 % of security vulnerabilities that human reviewers 
may overlook under certain evaluation conditions. [21]

[[page 19]]
13 
 
Self-Reflection and Iterative Improvement 
A defining characteristic of autonomous agents is the self-reflection mechanism. This is 
a post-action evaluation process in which the agent evaluates its own output against the 
initial objective, analyzes internal feedback, and adjusts its subsequent actions accord-
ingly. Assigning agents specialized roles, such as scrum master or a test engineer, al-
lows them to collaboratively review and engage in continuous self-refinement to improve 
code quality. [21] The goal of this iterative self -critique is to enable agents to identify 
hallucinations or logical gaps, such as misplaced methods or unnecessary variables, 
without human intervention. 
Automated Debugging and the Plan–Execute–Verify Loop 
Autonomous agents manage complex maintenance tasks through integrated automated 
debugging workflows. These systems typically operate in a plan–execute–verify loop, 
where the agent autonomously provisions sandboxed environments to invoke compilers, 
run test suites, and analyze execution logs. [17][21] In cases where tests fail, agents 
analyze the error messages to rectify the code and re-run the validation cycle iteratively. 
While systems have demonstrated the ability to generate and execute test cases auton-
omously, the effectiveness of these fixes often depends on the quality of the provided 
context [17]. 
 Despite these benefits, self-correction mechanisms are not uniformly effective across 
all contexts. Their performance depends heavily on correct initial task interpretation; in-
correct assumptions may propagate through iterative refinement processes. Plan –exe-
cute–verify loops are also constrained by test coverage quality. In poorly specified tasks 
or environments with incomplete eval uation criteria, agents may converge to solutions 
that pass local validation but fail to satisfy real-world requirements. Effectiveness is gen-
erally higher in structured programming tasks than in open -ended design problems 
where evaluation criteria are ambiguous or subjective [13][19].

3.4 Multi-Agents 
The integration of Large Language Models into Multi -Agent Systems (MAS) represents 
aa evolution in autonomous software engineering, shifting the research from single LLM-
based agents toward collaborative multi-agent architectures. While single-agent systems 
may face limitations in handling complex, multi-domain problems, MAS leverage special-
ized agents to address such complexity [6]. However, due to the early stage of the field, 
empirical evidence on full end-to-end SDLC automation remains limited [1][6] 
 Compared to single-agent systems, multi-agent architectures may improve scalability 
and specialization by distributing tasks across role-specific agents. While single agents 
are constrained by limited context wi ndows and sequential reasoning, multi -agent sys-
tems enable parallel task execution and structured role separation (e.g., planner, devel-
oper, reviewer). [6][18] However, this architectural shift introduces coordination over-
head, including communication latency, inconsistency between outputs, and increased 
system complexity. 
 Despite increased autonomy, human oversight remains an essential requirement. As 
MAS handle more technical execution, the role of human developers shifts from direct 
implementation toward strategic supervision and goal definition. [6][17] Human interven-
tion remains necessary in situations involving ambiguity, conflict resolution, or ethical 
considerations where current systems lack human-level judgment [6][13]. Consequently, 
the advancement of MAS suggests a paradigm shift toward a hybrid model, combining 
the rapid processing power of specialized AI agents with the strategic guidance and 
oversight of human engineers [1][6]. 
 Multi-agent systems also introduc e new failure modes that aren’t present in single -
agent setups. These include inter-agent disagreement, cascading errors across depend-
ent agents, and challenges in attributing responsibility for incorrect outputs. [5] As system 
complexity increases, debugging and evaluation become more challenging due to dis-
tributed decision-making structures.

4 LIMITATIONS AND CHALLENGES 
Despite the rapid advancement of AI agents in software development, the literature iden-
tifies several critical limitations that constrain their practical use. Key challenges relate to 
reliability, security, and performance, particularly as task complexity increases. AI agents 
are prone to generating incorrect or misleading outputs, and their behavior can become 
unstable in real-world development scenarios. In addition, the current body of research 
remains relatively limited and recent, which restricts the generalizability of existing find-
ings. 
 Further concerns arise from security and privacy risks, as well as difficulties in evalu-
ating agent performance using traditional metrics that may not reflect real software qual-
ity. The increasing reliance on AI -generated code also introduces risks related to tech-
nical and AI -related debt, potentially affecting long -term maintainability. The following 
sections examine these challenges in more detail across reliability, security, perfor-
mance, and sustainability perspectives. 
4.1 Reliability Issues 
Despite the potential of Large Language Models (LLMs) and multi -agent architectures, 
reliability remains a primary obstacle to their full integration into the Software Develop-
ment Life Cycle (SDLC). These systems are characterized by inherent technical limita-
tions that can compromise the integrity of software products if not managed through rig-
orous oversight. 
Reliability Issues and Hallucinations 
A significant challenge in LLM-based agents is the phenomenon of hallucinations, where 
models produce inconsistent or logically incorrect outputs. In software engineering tasks, 
this often manifests as the invocation of non -existent classes, methods, or library ver-
sions, as well as the generation of files in incorrect target paths. [21] Furthermore, the 
non-deterministic nature of LLMs introduces a n element of unpredictability , where the 
same input prompt may yield varying res ults across different executions. This lack of 
consistency poses severe risks for code generation and testing, as a solution that passes 
verification in one instance may fail in another, complicating debugging and mainte-
nance. [3][9]

[[page 22]]
16 
 
Error Propagation 
The transition to multi -agent architectures, while theoretically beneficial for complex 
tasks, introduces additional layers of coordination complexity. Errors generated by a sin-
gle agent, such as an incorrect architectural design or a flawed requirement specification, 
can propagate through the entire workflow, leading to cascading failures in downstream 
agents. [6] These failures are often rooted in miscommunication, unclear role definitions, 
or incorrect task decomposition. For instance, if Manager-type agent fails to correctly 
segment a project into modules, subsequent Developer-type agents may produce frag-
mented code that lacks necessary interdependencies, resulting in non -functional sys-
tems. [18] 
Limited Empirical Evidence and Research Maturity 
Agentic AI remains a n upcoming paradigm in software engineering research, and the 
state of technology is currently viewed as immature [6][13]. There is a pronounced gap 
between the theoretical performance observed in controlled benchmarks and practical 
utility in real-world applications. While benchmarks may show high success rates, real -
world data indicates that agent -generated pull requests are often accepted less fre-
quently than human-authored ones due to issues with maintainability and project-specific 
context. [7] 
4.2 Security and Privacy Concerns 
The integration of autonomous AI agents into the Software Development Life Cycle 
(SDLC) introduces complex security and privacy vectors that differ fundamentally from 
traditional development tools. Because autonomous agents are granted with the capacity 
to make independent decisions and take real-world actions, such as interacting with re-
positories, APIs, package managers, and deployment systems, they significantly expand 
the potential attack surface. [5][21] 
Security Risks and Vulnerability Propagation 
A primary security concern involves the potential for agents to introduce vulnerabilities 
through autonomous tool interaction. Agents may download inappropriate files, fall prey 
to cyber-attacks, or be directed by malicious users to execute unsafe code. [5][21] Fur-
thermore, AI models are frequently trained on datasets containing legacy issues and 
malpractices, leading to the generation of code with relevant vulnerabilities, such as 
buffer overflows and unsafe memory operations. [9][12]

[[page 23]]
17 
 
 The phenomenon of prompt injection poses a critical threat to workflow integrity. In a 
goal hijacking attack, the agent’s original instructions are replaced by an attacker, caus-
ing the system to execute unauthorized commands. More complex is the indirect prompt 
injection attack, where an agent retrieves information from external data sources, such 
as web pages or project files, that contains hidden malicious instructions. These attacks 
can lead to remote code execution, unauthorized SQL queries, or the exfiltration of API 
keys. [5] 
Privacy and Data Exposure 
Privacy remains a significant hurdle for enterprise -level adoption. AI agents require ex-
tensive project context to function effectively, yet transmitting source code or project 
metadata to external Large Language Model (LLM) APIs risks the exposure of proprie-
tary information, hardcoded credentials, and sensitive user data [14]. In multi-organiza-
tional projects, the lack of transparency in how agents handle data residing in silos can 
lead to unnoticed privacy violations [6]. Additionally, if agents generate outputs based on 
inference of incomplete instructions or hallucinated context, they may unintentionally dis-
close confidential information or fabricate access to sensitive resources [5]. 
Risks in Multi-Agent Systems 
The architectural complexity of Large Language Model -based Multi-Agent (LMA) sys-
tems introduces additional points of failure. While specialization enhances productivity, 
it can also lead to hallucination amplification, where minor errors generated by one agent 
are magnified through frequent cooperation. Furthermore, a single agent's error or mis-
leading information can rapidly propagate through the system, leading to flawed archi-
tectural decisions or cascading logic faults. Coordination also enabl es secret collusion, 
where agents may seemingly follow benign independent actions while cumulatively pro-
ducing outcomes that exhibit systemic bias or undermine operational integrity. [5] 
Mitigation and Governance Strategies 
To safeguard essential systems, the literature proposes a combination of technical 
guardrails and human oversight. Sandboxed execution environments are vital for isolat-
ing agent actions, allowing systems to simulate execution and assess threats within a 
containerized space before applying changes to the real-world environment. [5][19] Ac-
cess control models, such as Attribute -Based Access Control, should be extended to 
manage the dynamic permissions required by multi-agent systems [6].

[[page 24]]
18 
 
 Technical measures like data marking, encoding, and t he use of delimiters can help 
agents distinguish between instructions and untrusted external data [5]. Ultimately, hu-
man-in-the-loop (HITL) verification remains indispensable. Human engineers must serve 
as ethical gatekeepers, validating agent-generated artifacts against safety standards and 
providing strategic supervision to ensure the system aligns with human expectations. 
[12][15]

4.3 Performance Limitations and Evaluation Challenges 
While Large Language Model (LLM) -based agents demonstrate significant potential in 
automating routine software engineering tasks, their efficacy is notably constrained when 
applied to complex, ambiguous, or novel problems. Empirical evidence suggests a pro-
nounced performance degradation as task complexity increases, revealing a critical gap 
between theoretical autonomous capabilities and practical utility. 
Performance in Complex and Novel Tasks 
Current AI agents excel at generating discrete code snippets or handling well -defined 
routine tasks, such as creating boilerplate code or simple RESTful API routes [1]. How-
ever, they struggle with high -level architectural under standing and complex reasoning 
[2]. In studies using Auto -GPT, high-level prompts frequently led to conflicts and ambi-
guity, whereas successful execution required precise, context -rich information that the 
agents could not autonomously derive [21]. Furthermore, agents often implement basic 
logic resulting in low information density, making them more suitable for prototype sys-
tems than for production-grade, real-world applications [18]. 
 A notable limitation involves difficulty managing long-term dependencies and system-
level semantics across multiple abstractions [2]. For instance, a case study utilizing the 
ChatDev framework found that while the system efficiently developed a simple Snake 
game, it failed to implement the core logic of a more complex Tetris game, specifically 
the removal of completed rows, despite multiple attempts [6]. This highlights a deficit in 
the deep logical reasoning and abstraction required for non-trivial software components. 
Challenges in Automated Evaluation 
A central limitation of current evaluation practices is that benchmark performance does 
not necessarily correspond to production -quality software engineering outcomes. Most 
existing benchmarks evaluate isolated functional correctness, where success is meas-
ured by whether genera ted code passes predefined unit tests. In contrast, real -world 
software development requires broader qualities such as maintainability, readability, ar-
chitectural consistency, security, documentation quality, adherence to project -specific

[[page 26]]
20 
 
conventions, and successful integration into existing systems. Consequently, an agent 
may achieve high benchmark scores while still producing code that is unsuitable for de-
ployment or rejected during professional code review processes [7][9][18]. 
The evaluation of AI agent s is further complicated by the limitations of current bench-
marking frameworks. Popular datasets such as HumanEval and MBPP are primarily de-
signed for small-scale, function-level code generation and do not adequately represent 
the multi-faceted challenges of real-world software projects [18]. These benchmarks of-
ten focus on isolated problem -solving, overlooking collaborative requirements such as 
code integration, peer reviews, and maintainability [6]. 
 Furthermore, high success rates in controlled benchmark s often fail to translate to 
real-world trust. Empirical data from real-world applications reveals a stark disparity: alt-
hough agents can generate code faster than humans, their pull requests (PRs) are ac-
cepted and merged significantly less frequently [7]. This disparity highlights a methodo-
logical issue in evaluating AI agents: benchmark success primarily measures short-term 
task completion, whereas production software evaluation involves long -term software 
quality considerations. Human reviewers assess no t only whether code functions cor-
rectly, but also whether it aligns with architectural principles, minimizes technical debt, 
follows team conventions, and remains maintainable over time. As a result, benchmark-
oriented evaluation may systematically overest imate the practical reliability and readi-
ness of autonomous software engineering agents. This suggests that automated metrics, 
such as functional correctness or acceptance rates on evaluation platforms, do not ac-
curately reflect software quality, adherence to project-specific idioms, or long-term main-
tainability [7][9]. 
The Necessity of Human Judgement 
Because AI models often generate seemingly valid but logically flawed or unmaintainable 
code, human judgment remains indispensable. Automated metrics alone are insufficient 
to replace the nuanced evaluation required to assess a solution’s alignment with broader 
project goals, security standards, and architectural integrity. [1 ][7] For example, agents 
may overlook security vulnerabilities like buffer overflows or use deprecated library ver-
sions that a human reviewer would identify. 
 Consequently, the real-world adoption of agentic software engineering necessitates a 
shift toward human-in-the-loop (HITL) workflows. Human engineers must act as strategic 
supervisors, providing context-driven interventions and validating agent-generated solu-
tions against functional and safety standards. Addressing these evaluation gaps through 
improved, dynamic frameworks is critical for moving beyond rapid p rototyping toward

[[page 27]]
21 
 
reliable autonomous development. Therefore, evaluating autonomous AI agents solely 
through function-level benchmarks provides an incomplete picture of their practical ca-
pabilities in software engineering environments. 
4.4 Technical Debt and AI-Related Debt 
The integration of AI agents into software engineering introduces a complex interplay 
between traditional technical debt and emerging AI -related debt. While AI -assisted de-
velopment significantly accelerates initial implementation, empirical evidence suggests 
that rapid code generation often prioritizes short-term productivity at the expense of long-
term maintainability [11]. Solutions produced through vibe coding or agentic orchestra-
tion frequently manifest as latent technical debt, characterized by suboptimal design de-
cisions, inconsistent naming conventions, and a deficiency in nuanced documentation. 
Beyond structural issues, projects increasingly accumulate AI -specific debt. This cate-
gory includes so -called explainability debt and governanc e debt, which stem from the 
inherent opacity of large language models (LLMs), making their outputs difficult for hu-
man engineers to interpret. [11] Because LLMs exhibit non -deterministic behavior, the 
same prompt can yield varying results across different executions, complicating the re-
production and validation of software logic [1]. 
 The continuous use of AI agents creates compounding risks for a project’s lifecycle. 
The rapid accumulation of AI-generated solutions can lead to unmaintainable logic and 
systemic design traces that increase overall debugging complexity. [11][19] A critical 
long-term consequence is the erosion of expertise, known as deskilling, where develop-
ers acting as passive reviewers lose the technical depth and situational awareness re-
quired to identify subtle vulnerabilities or so-called stupid bugs introduced by the AI 
[12][15]. To mitigate these risks, the literature emphasizes human-in-the-loop workflows 
[15]. Essential strategies include the integration of AI agents with static analysis tools, 
mandatory code reviews for all agent -generated merges, and the implementation of 
prompt versioning to ensure traceability and reproducibility [11].

5 DISCUSSION 
The findings of this review demonstrate that AI agents are transitioning from passive 
completion tools to autonomous AI teammates capable of orchestrating the Software 
Development Life Cycle (SDLC) [7][21]. However, a central finding of this study is that 
the apparent capabilities of AI agents are strongly dependent on the evaluation context. 
While performance in controlled benchmarks is often high, real-world software engineer-
ing utility is significantly more constrained by system complexity, ambiguity, and lo ng-
term maintainability requirements. This section interprets the findings in relation to the 
research question, compress them with existing literature, and discusses implications 
and limitations of the study. 
Interpretation of Findings and Connection to Research Question 
The research question focused on the capabilities and limitations of AI agents in software 
engineering. The results indicate that the primary capability of modern agents is dele-
gated autonomy, supported by task decomposition, tool orchestr ation, and multi-agent 
synergy [18]. In practice, AI agents extend beyond code generation by performing roles 
associated with system design, implementation, and quality assurance [6]. 
 However, this capability profile is strongly context dependent. A key f inding of this 
review is that AI agents perform well in structured, well -scoped environments but show 
reduced reliability in open -ended or evolving software tasks. This creates a distinction 
between demonstrated capability in benchmarks and practical capab ility in real -world 
development environments. 
 A further limitation is the reliability gap observed across studies. While agents can 
implement features in reduced timeframes, outputs may include inconsistencies, hallu-
cinated components, or logically incomplete implementations [7]. This indicates high task 
coverage but variable depth of correctness, especially in complex systems requiring 
cross-module reasoning. 
Benchmark Performance vs Real-World Utility 
A central conclusion of this review is the mismatch b etween benchmark performance 
and real-world software engineering value. Benchmarks such as HumanEval and MBPP 
primarily measure functional correctness in isolated coding tasks. In contrast, production 
software development involves additional constraints, i ncluding architectural con-
sistency, maintainability, security requirements, and integration with existing systems.

[[page 29]]
23 
 
Empirical evidence highlights this discrepancy. Although agent -based systems often 
achieve high success rates on synthetic benchmarks, large -scale real-world evidence 
from pull request data indicates significantly lower acceptance rates for AI -generated 
code compared to human-authored contributions [7]. This suggests that benchmark per-
formance alone is not a reliable proxy for engineering quality in production environments. 
This finding can be interpreted as the core methodological limitation of current AI agent 
evaluation: existing benchmarks measure whether code works in isolation, while real -
world development evaluates whether code works within a system. 
Comparing with Existing Literature 
The literature generally reports substantial productivity gains from AI -assisted develop-
ment, particularly in controlled experimental settings and structured tasks [18]. However, 
this review highlights that such results are context-sensitive and may not fully represent 
production-grade software development. 
 A key factor underlying this discrepancy is the dependence of AI agents on explicit 
and well-scoped instructions. While agents can generate functional code efficiently, they 
tend to optimize for local task completion rather than global system properties such as 
maintainability, architecture alignment, or long-term extensibility. 
 This suggests that current systems lack robust implicit reasoning about softwa re en-
gineering constraints that are not explicitly stated in prompts. As a result, efficiency gains 
observed in experimental conditions do not necessarily translate into production reliabil-
ity. 
Implications for Software Engineering 
The shift toward agentic workflows implies a reconfiguration of the software developer’s 
role. Rather than primarily implementing code, developers increasingly function as sys-
tem supervisors, validators, and architectural decision-makers [7][19]. 
A key implication of this shift i s that effective use of AI agents requires a broader and 
more advanced skill set than traditional coding alone. Developers must be able to: 
 evaluate AI-generated code for logical correctness and architectural fit 
 identify subtle errors that are not captured by tests or benchmarks 
 understand system-level dependencies across modules 
 formulate precise prompts and constraints for agents 
 validate security and maintainability properties beyond functional correctness

[[page 30]]
24 
 
This creates a non-trivial cognitive load. Instead of reducing complexity, AI agents may 
redistribute it from implementation to supervision and verification. This effect is particu-
larly significant for less experienced developers, who may struggle to detect subtle is-
sues in AI-generated outputs or to provide sufficiently precise guidance to the system. 
 Furthermore, AI-assisted development introduces a form of AI-specific technical debt. 
Non-deterministic outputs complicate reproducibility and debugging, as identical prompts 
may produce different implementations over time [2]. This blurs traditional SDLC bound-
aries, merging design, implementation, and testing into continuous iterative loops [7][20]. 
Limitations of the Study 
This study is constrained by the rapidly evolving nature of AI technologies. Most empirical 
data come from the 2023–2025 period, reflecting a field in c ontinuous and accelerated 
development. As a result, findings may already be partially outdated at the time of the 
analysis. 
 In addition, many evaluated systems are either experimental prototypes or closed -
source commercial models, limiting reproducibility and restricting access to internal 
model architectures, training data, and implementation details [4][9]. This reduces the 
ability to perform fully transparent or independent validation of reported results. 
 A further limitation relates to the distribution of published research itself. The literature 
is likely influenced by publication bias and rapid industry -driven development cycles, 
where positive or performance-oriented results are more frequently reported than nega-
tive or inconclusive findings. This may result in an incomplete representation of failure 
cases, edge-case behaviors, or long-term maintenance issues. 
 Moreover, research on fully autonomous AI agents at an enterprise scale is still scarce, 
with most studies focusing on small-to-medium projects [18]. Which may not accurately 
reflect the complexity, legacy constraints, and organizational dependencies present in 
industrial systems. 
 Finally, the pace of advancement in AI systems presents a structural challenge for 
academic research. Peer -reviewed publication cycles are often unable to match the 
speed of iterative model improvements and tool releases, creating a persistent lag be-
tween state -of-the-art capabilities and their representation in the literature. Conse-
quently, findings should be interpreted as reflective of a rapidly shifting baseline rather 
than a stable technological state.

Future Research Directions 
Future research in AI -assisted software engineering should primarily address the gap 
between benchmark performance and real-world relevance identified in current studies. 
A key priority is the development of standardized evaluation frameworks that reflect real-
world repository complexity, including architectural constraints, evolving requirements, 
and project-specific conventions. Existing benchmarks often rely on simplified or isolated 
tasks, which limits their ability to capture the contextual decision-making required in pro-
duction environments. 
 Closely related to this is the need for longitudinal, real -world studies examining how 
AI agents perform as part of human development teams over extended periods. Such 
studies should focus not only on productivity outcomes but also on workflow integration, 
collaboration dynamics, and the cognitive overhead imposed on developers supervising 
AI systems. 
 Another critical research direction concerns the reliability and maintainability of AI -
generated code in productio n systems. This includes systematic analysis of bug fre-
quency, technical debt accumulation, and the additional burden placed on code review 
processes when integrating non -deterministic outputs into stable codebases. Future 
work should investigate methods for improving consistency and reducing variance in AI-
generated solutions. 
 Furthermore, given the non -deterministic nature of current large language models, 
there is a need for reproducibility and verification frameworks that allow developers to 
validate AI outputs more systematically. This includes techniques for tracing decision 
paths, constraining model behavior, and ensuring consistent results across similar in-
puts. 
 Finally, research into human–AI collaboration should move beyond general productiv-
ity metrics toward more fine -grained analysis of role distribution, trust calibration, and 
supervision strategies. Understanding how responsibility is shared between developers 
and AI agents will be essential for defining sustainable development practices in agent-
assisted environments.

6 CONCLUSION 
This thesis has explored the current state of autonomous AI agents in software engineer-
ing, specifically addressing the research question: "What are the core capabilities and 
inherent limitations of autonomous AI agents when applied to the software development 
life cycle?". Through a literature review, the research has synthesized the technical foun-
dations, empirical findings, and associated risks of agentic systems, highlighting a shift 
from traditional coding assistants toward more autonomous development support tools. 
The main findings suggest that AI agents possess significant capabilities in automating 
elements of SDLC. Key among these is the ability to perform hierarchical task decompo-
sition, orchestrate development tools such as compilers and version control systems and 
apply self-reflective mechanisms for error correction. Within the limitations of recent em-
pirical studies, evidence indicates that these systems may improve developer productiv-
ity in routine tasks a nd accelerate the implementation of software features under con-
trolled conditions. Multi-agent systems are reported to further support these outcomes 
by assigning specialized roles, such as architects and testers, enabling collaborative val-
idation and iterative refinement of outputs. 
 Despite these capabilities, the reviewed literature consistently highlights critical limita-
tions that constrain fully autonomous application. The reliability of AI agents remains a 
key concern due to issues such as hallucinations, where systems may generate incorrect 
or non-existent references or deviate from specified objectives. Furthermore, evidence 
suggests that performance decreases as task complexity increases, indicating limitations 
in deep reasoning and system-level understanding required for non-trivial software com-
ponents. The integration of AI agents is also associated with security and operational 
risks, including susceptibility to prompt injection, potential secret leakage, and the emer-
gence of both technical and AI-specific debt, which may negatively affect long-term main-
tainability. 
 The broader implications of the reviewed literature suggest a potential shift in software 
engineering practices. Within current evidence, AI agents appear most effective as sup-
portive collaborators for rapid prototyping and routine automation rather than as fully 
autonomous replacements for human engineers. The findings indicate that the role of 
the developer may increasingly shift toward strategic supervision, validation, and archi-
tectural decision-making.

[[page 33]]
27 
 
 However, this study is constrained by the limited availability of large -scale empirical 
evidence and the rapid evolution of the field, with much of the current research based on 
experimental or prototype -level systems. Therefore, th e conclusions should be inter-
preted within the limitations of recent empirical studies and may not fully generalize to 
long-term industrial-scale deployment scenarios. Future research should focus on devel-
oping evaluation frameworks that extend beyond functional correctness to include archi-
tectural integrity and real -world maintainability. Additionally, further investigation is 
needed into long-term security mitigation strategies and the potential impacts of AI -as-
sisted development on developer expertise and skill development. 
 In conclusion, while the reviewed literature suggests that AI agents provide a form of 
delegated autonomy in software engineering, human -in-the-loop oversight remains es-
sential to ensure security, reliability, and architectural cohere nce in practical software 
development contexts.
