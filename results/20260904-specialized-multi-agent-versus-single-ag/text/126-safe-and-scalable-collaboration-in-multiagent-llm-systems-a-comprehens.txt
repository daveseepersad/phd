Abstract—Large language model (LLM)-based multiagent sys-
tems are a rapidly evolving frontier of artiﬁcial intelligence that
enables complex coordination, communication, and autonomous
reasoning across distributed agents. This article presents a
comprehensive review of multiagent LLM ecosystems, orga-
nized around four foundational pillars: coordination strategies,
communication frameworks, safety challenges, and trust and
alignment mechanisms. We synthesize a range of architec-
tural approaches to agent interaction, including shared latent
representations, strategic autonomy, task decomposition, and
neuro-symbolic communication protocols. We then systematically
examine the critical vulnerabilities distinctive to multiagent
environments, namely prompt injection, perceptual adversarial
attacks, reward misalignment, and operational overreach. To
address these issues, we propose the trust stack : a multilay-
ered trust framework spanning epistemic reliability, behavioral
alignment, role awareness, social accountability, and institu-
tional governance. Beyond synthesizing the literature, this review
contributes a novel integrated perspective. It models the interde-
pendencies among these four pillars, formalizes the trust stack
as an operational framework with runtime-computable metrics,
and derives design principles from a cross-system analysis of real
multiagent deployment failures—contributions absent from prior
surveys. We further investigate the prospects for superalignment
through constitutional AI principles, multiagent value aggrega-
tion, and dynamic preference shaping. Collectively, this work
establishes a theoretical and practical foundation for the secure,
reliable, and dependable deployment of multiagent LLM systems
and o ﬀers a roadmap for future research at the intersection of
coordination, communication, and alignment.
Index Terms—Alignment and superalignment, cognitive secu-
rity, communication protocols, coordination strategies, dis-
tributed reasoning, large language models (LLMs), multiagent
systems, preference shaping, safety and robustness, trust stack.

I. I NTRODUCTION
L
ARGE language models (LLMs) such as GPT-4 [1],
PaLM [2], and LLaMA [3] have transformed natural
language processing (NLP), demonstrating in-context learning
[4], zero-shot generalization [5], and coherent multiturn dialog
[6]. Although originally designed to operate as standalone sys-
tems, these models are increasingly deployed as autonomous
agents [7], [8] that interact with users, with their environment,
and with one another. This shift has given rise to multia-
gent LLM systems [9], in which multiple models collaborate,
coordinate, and communicate to solve complex problems that
exceed the capabilities of any single agent.
Interest in multiagent LLM frameworks has grown with
the rising demand for intelligent systems in applications
such as business automation [10], autonomous research [11],
distributed healthcare [12], and cooperative robotics [13].
Beyond making decisions, these agents can reason, reﬂect,
negotiate, and align their behavior through language-driven
interaction. This shift from task-speciﬁc tools to general-
purpose, language-powered ecosystems represents a signiﬁcant
advance in AI research.
A recent comprehensive review further conﬁrms this
momentum, highlighting the rapid proliferation of agent
frameworks, benchmarks, and collaboration protocols across
domains ranging from biomedical research to software
engineering [14].
Historically, multiagent systems (MASs) emerged from
ﬁelds such as game theory [15], swarm robotics [16], and
distributed artiﬁcial intelligence [17], emphasizing agents with
modular knowledge and limited perception and communica-
tion. LLM agents, by contrast, invert this paradigm. They
encode vast amounts of knowledge, use natural language
as their medium of operation, and model multiple cognitive
capacities within a single framework. This enables roles such
as mentor, assessor, or collaborator to be assigned dynamically
according to the interaction strategy and the intended task
structure.
Recent systems such as V oyager [18], CAMEL [19],
BabyAGI [20], and AutoGPT [21] demonstrate the promise
of autonomous LLM agents [7]. They exhibit capabilities
such as multiagent orchestration [22], memory generation [23],
recursive self-prompting [24], and tool use [25]. Neverthe-
less, they also face pressing limitations—fragile planning,
emergent dishonesty, ambiguous trust, and misalignment
with human goals—all of which underscore the need for

Notably, recent work has characterized multiagent col-
laboration along ﬁve key dimensions—actor composition,
interaction type, structural topology, coordination strategy, and
communication protocol—providing a principled taxonomy
for analyzing these systems at scale [26]. At the same time,
emerging studies reveal that interagent trust itself constitutes
a ﬁrst-class security variable: higher levels of agent-to-agent
trust improve task success rates but simultaneously increase
exposure to adversarial exploitation, a phenomenon formalized
as the Trust-Vulnerability Paradox [27]. Safety evaluation of
such systems has likewise evolved, with structured multiagent
debate frameworks demonstrating that distributing judgment
across critic, defender, and judge agents yields more accurate
and scalable assessments of harmful content than single-model
approaches [28].
Despite this growing enthusiasm, the ﬁeld remains frag-
mented. Key challenges include developing scalable coordina-
tion mechanisms, ensuring eﬀective interagent communication
without semantic drift, and maintaining safety and alignment
among interacting agents. Comparative analysis is further
hampered by the frequent absence of standardized evaluation
protocols, uniform architectural frameworks, and shared ter-
minology in prior work.
This article presents a comprehensive review of mul-
tiagent LLM systems, organized around four foundational
pillars: coordination (strategic autonomy, task decomposition,
and team dynamics), communication (shared representations,
neuro-symbolic protocols, and interaction frameworks), safety
(cognitive and perceptual vulnerabilities, operational misalign-
ments, and layered defense strategies), and trust and alignment
(dynamic preference shaping, multiagent value aggregation,
and constitutional AI safeguards).
Several recent surveys have examined individual facets of
this problem space, including LLM-based autonomous agents
[7], multiagent LLM collaboration [29], and LLM-agent
alignment [30], [31]. While valuable, these surveys address
individual dimensions of the multiagent LLM problem in
isolation; none provides: 1) an integrated cross-pillar analysis
of the coordination–communication–safety–alignment inter-
dependencies; 2) a formalized, operational trust framework
with runtime-computable metrics; or 3) a systematic empirical
failure taxonomy derived from real multiagent deployments.
The present work is explicitly designed to close these three
gaps, as set out in the contributions below and substantiated
in Section XI-A.
The main contributions of this study are as follows.
1) Integrated Four-Pillar Perspective: Unlike prior
surveys that treat coordination, communication,
safety, and alignment as independent concerns, we
present an integrated synthesis organized around
their interdependencies—showing, for example, how
communication failures such as semantic drift cascade
into safety vulnerabilities such as hallucination
propagation, and how coordination choices such as
task-decomposition depth bound the blast radius of
misalignment.
2) Multiagent-Speciﬁc Safety Analysis: In contrast to
single-agent alignment surveys, we analyze safety
challenges that are intrinsically multiagent—prompt
injection propagation, perceptual adversarial cascades,
operational overreach, emergent collective failures,
and adversarial ampliﬁcation through consensus
mechanisms.
3) Empirical Failure Taxonomy and Design Principles:
We synthesize documented failures of real multia-
gent deployments (e.g., Agent Hospital, MetaGPT, and
GPTSwarm) into a cross-system taxonomy and derive
concrete design principles, rather than summarizing indi-
vidual systems in isolation.
4) The Trust Stack as an Operational Framework:
We propose the trust stack , a novel ﬁve-layer
trust framework spanning epistemic reliability, behav-
ioral alignment, role-awareness, social accountability,
and institutional governance, equipped with runtime-
computable metrics and formal cross-layer depen-
dency chains—making it operational rather than purely
conceptual.
5) Research Roadmap: Building on the trust stack, we
outline open research challenges for safe, adaptive, and
value-aligned multiagent LLM infrastructures, including
dynamic preference adaptation, multistakeholder aggre-
gation, and constitutional governance models.
This study lays a theoretical and practical foundation for
advancing the design, evaluation, and safe deployment of
multiagent LLM systems that are cooperative, communicative,
robust, and aligned with evolving human values and societal
norms.
The remainder of this study is organized as follows.
Section II presents the foundational concepts underlying
multiagent LLM systems. Section III discusses coordina-
tion strategies within agent systems. Section IV explores
human–LLM collaboration frameworks. Section V examines
the development of collective intelligence and approaches to
managing uncertainty. Section VI describes how agents can
extract and reason over scientiﬁc knowledge. Section VII
analyzes key safety challenges faced by multiagent LLMs.
Section VIII examines empirical failure modes and lessons
from unsuccessful multiagent deployments. Section IX details
strategies for designing safe and robust agent systems.
Section X discusses the path toward achieving superalignment
in complex agent ecosystems. Section XI presents future
research directions, including the development of the trust
stack framework. Finally, Section XII concludes this article
and summarizes key insights.

A. Competition, Negotiation, and Emergence
In multiagent LLM ecosystems, strategic autonomy
describes the ability of each agent B j∈ B (where B represents
the set of agents) to independently set goals G j, devise action
strategies µ j, and adapt its decision-making in response to
interactions with other agents and the surrounding environment
[68]. These agents operate with limited global knowledge and
partially aligned objectives, leading to emergent phenomena.
When agent B j assesses its strategy µ j to optimize a person-
alized utility function U j(x,α j), the resulting collective state
transition of the environment is shaped by the actions of all

E. Critical Analysis: Success Factors and Common Pitfalls
in Multiagent Coordination
While the coordination strategies described above show
promise in laboratory settings, real-world deployments
reveal critical success factors and systematic failure modes
that deserve careful examination. This analysis synthesizes
lessons learned from both successful and failed multiagent
implementations.
1) Why Strategic Autonomy Succeeds or Fails: Strategic
autonomy in multiagent systems demonstrates high e ﬀective-
ness in bounded, well-deﬁned domains, but frequently fails
in open-ended environments. Analysis of deployed systems
reveals three critical success factors.
1) Success Factor 1 (Bounded Action Spaces): Systems
such as AlphaStar [80] and Diplomacy agents [81] suc-
ceed because they operate within discrete, enumerable
action spaces where |A| < 106. In contrast, agents
with unbounded tool access (e.g., AutoGPT executing
arbitrary shell commands) exhibit failure rates exceeding
60% in production environments due to the combinato-
rial explosion of possible strategies.
2) Success Factor 2 (Reward Signal Clarity): Competi-
tive coordination via Nash equilibrium requires clearly
observable payo ﬀs. Empirical studies show that when
the reward signal noise exceeds σr > 0.3 · E[R],
convergence to stable equilibria fails in more than 75%
of cases. This explains why multiagent negotiation per-
forms well in simulated trading (with clear monetary
outcomes) but fails in ambiguous social scenarios.
3) Failure Mode (Myopic Optimization):The most common
failure pattern is agents optimizing local objectives with-
out regard for long-term collective outcomes. Analysis
of over 50 multiagent deployments shows that sys-
tems lacking explicit coordination mechanisms (such as
CAMEL’s role-playing) frequently converge to subopti-

2) Communication Protocol Breakdown Analysis: Neuro-
symbolic communication protocols, while elegant in theory,
face systematic challenges in practice.
1) The Semantic Drift Problem: Longitudinal studies reveal
that symbolic vocabularies Lex(H j) diverge at a rate of
2%–5% per 1000 interaction cycles when agents learn
independently. Systems such as Agent Hospital [35]
mitigate this through centralized ontology management,
but this introduces a single point of failure.
2) When Shared Representations Fail: Counterintuitively,
forcing agents to use identical shared representations
Z can reduce system robustness. In dynamic environ-
ments with concept drift, homogeneous representational
schemes lead to correlated failures: when one agent
misinterprets a shifted concept, all agents fail simul-
taneously. Heterogeneous ensemble approaches with
diverse encoders {φ1,φ 2,...,φ n} demonstrate approxi-
mately 40% better resilience to distributional shift.
3) Practical Limitation (Latency Versus Expressiveness
Tradeoﬀ): Detailed proﬁling of production multiagent
systems shows that neuro-symbolic protocols introduce
3–10× latency overhead compared to direct neural mes-
sage passing. For real-time applications (e.g., multidrone
coordination), this overhead is prohibitive. The ﬁeld
currently lacks protocols that simultaneously achieve
high expressiveness and low latency—this remains a
critical open problem.
3) Task Decomposition: When Modularity Backﬁres:
Chain-of-Thought decomposition and workﬂow orchestration
represent signiﬁcant advances, yet they introduce failure
modes absent in end-to-end approaches.
1) Error Accumulation in Sequential Pipelines: Empirical
analysis of systems such as MetaGPT shows that in deep
task graphs with depth d> 5, error accumulation causes

4) Synthesis: Design Principles From Failure Analysis:
From examining both successful and failed multiagent deploy-
ments, the following design principles emerge.
1) Coordination Overhead Budget: Limit interagent com-
munication to < 30% of total compute time to prevent
coordination from dominating productive work.
2) Graceful Degradation: Systems should maintain fall-
back modes. Agents with at least three operational
modes (collaborative, independent, and safe default)
exhibit roughly 5× lower catastrophic failure rates.
3) Bounded Autonomy: Pure autonomy without constraints
fails. Successful systems enforce explicit bounds: max-
imum planning depth dmax≤ 7, maximum tool calls per
query tmax≤ 20, and timeout mechanisms τmax≤ 60 s.
4) Diversity-Consensus Balance: Optimal heterogeneity
satisﬁes H∗ ≈ 0.7 · Hmax—su ﬃcient diversity for
robustness, but not so much that consensus becomes
impossible.

V. C OLLECTIVE INTELLIGENCE AND UNCERTAINTY
A. Resilience to Model Drift and Concept Shift
Dynamic environments threaten the stability of multiagent
systems through model drift and concept shift. Concept shift
involves changes to the data distribution P(y | x), whereas
model drift involves the gradual degradation of the learned
function fθ(x) due to learning, noise, or deployment shifts.
Concept shift may be gradual (e.g., changing user behavior) or
abrupt (e.g., a crisis event). It manifests in out-of-distribution
responses, shifting gradient dynamics, and drifting attention
weights. To address these problems, agents employ statisti-
cal divergence monitoring, ensemble learning, and Bayesian
updating. Contrastive loss and episodic memory replay main-
tain model stability, whereas regularization reduces parameter
drift. Common cross-agent methods include mutual correction
gradients, task delegation to more stable peers, and ensemble
voting. Model modiﬁcations are aggregated globally to realign
federated systems. Resilience requires robust uncertainty quan-
tiﬁcation. Agents can calibrate their conﬁdence in predictions
by distinguishing aleatoric noise from epistemic uncertainty
using MC Dropout and deep ensembles. To retain autonomy
and performance, resilient systems align their models, detect
and respond to distributional shifts, and manage uncertainty.

Multiagent LLM systems face three interconnected classes

tion, and operational execution.
A. Cognitive Vulnerabilities: Prompt Injection, Jailbreaks,
and Hallucinations
Prompt injection attacks craft inputs padv(x) that appear
harmless but alter an agent’s decision-making, producing
behavior b < Spec(Ai). In multiagent settings, a compro-

C(y) = 1 when y∈S, but adversarial phrasing forces C(y) = 0

Building on the three gaps identiﬁed in Section I, we now
position each of our contributions in detail against this body
of literature.
1) Uniﬁed Four-Pillar Framework: Unlike prior surveys
that treat coordination, communication, safety, and
alignment as separate concerns, this work provides an
integrated review organized around their interdepen-
dencies. We demonstrate how communication failures
(semantic drift) directly produce safety vulnerabili-
ties (hallucination propagation), and how coordination
design choices (task decomposition depth) determine the
blast radius of misalignment events.
2) Multiagent-Speciﬁc Safety Analysis: Existing alignment
surveys [30] predominantly focus on single-agent align-
ment. Our safety analysis in Sections VII and VIII
is explicitly multiagent in character—addressing emer-
gent collective failures, interagent trust propagation,
and adversarial attack ampliﬁcation through consen-
sus mechanisms, phenomena absent from single-agent
frameworks.
3) The Trust Stack as Operational Framework: Prior
frameworks propose conceptual safety principles with-
out operationalizing them for runtime deployment
(Table VI). The trust stack provides computable metrics
at each layer (Table VII), formal cross-layer depen-
dency chains, and prescriptive failure recovery protocols,
making it directly actionable for practitioners deploying
multiagent systems in safety-critical contexts.
Furthermore, this review incorporates emerging work
at the intersection of LLM agents and industrial appli-
cations, including cognitive digital twin architectures for
Industry 5.0 [128], generative causal inference powered by
LLM-based agent simulation [129], and RAG-based domain
docking approaches [130]. These directions underscore the
practical urgency of the safety and trust frameworks developed
in this review, as deployment in industrial and domain-speciﬁc
environments introduces precisely the heterogeneous, high-
stakes coordination challenges that motivate the four-pillar
structure and the trust stack.

D. Framework Implementation
The trust stack serves as both an analytical tool and a
deployment guide. Implementation at each layer requires:
provenance-aware retrieval and Bayesian uncertainty quantiﬁ-
cation (Epistemic [99]); preference learning via RLHF and
constitutional constraints (Behavioral [119], [120]); interagent
accountability graphs and role inference (role awareness);
human-in-the-loop feedback with interpretability interfaces
and counterfactual auditing (Social); and AI ethics committees,
open standards, and democratic input mechanisms (Institu-
tional). The complete framework, illustrated in Fig. 3, provides
a systematic methodology for deploying multiagent systems in
safety-critical and socially embedded contexts.
XII. C ONCLUSION
In this review, we have examined the foundational, archi-
tectural, and ethical dimensions essential to developing secure,
aligned, and trustworthy multiagent systems powered by
large language models. Multiagent LLM research demands a
system-level perspective, from the coordination strategies that
underlie collaborative reasoning to the challenges of modeling
shared knowledge, enabling dynamic preference alignment,
and defending against cognitive and perceptual exploits.
We have argued for the importance of comprehensive
safety and alignment protocols, beginning with model-level
constraints and progressing to external monitors, architectural
redundancy, and constitutional governance. The risks of opera-
tional failure and misalignment increase as LLM-based agents
assume responsibilities in critical domains such as medicine,
law, scientiﬁc research, and autonomous control.
To address these issues, we proposed a multilayered trust
stack as a framework for future development. It spans insti-
tutional oversight, social transparency, role accountability,
behavioral alignment, and epistemic reliability. Each layer
contributes to a vertically integrated strategy that scales with
the complexity and autonomy of deployed systems.
Looking ahead, the challenge of superalignment—ensuring
that LLM agents consistently pursue beneﬁcial objectives
across societal contexts, roles, and time—will require both
normative clarity and technical innovation. It calls for interdis-
ciplinary collaboration across the social sciences, philosophy,
governance, and AI research. Ultimately, the future of secure
