A Comparative Empirical Evaluation of Single -Agent and 
Multi-Agent LLM Prompting Strategies for Automated 
Formative Feedback in Education 
LAI, Jiawen 1* LI, Zan 2 
1 University of California, US 
2 Peking University, CN 
 
* LAI, Jiawen is the corresponding author , E-mail: timi5645@gmail.com 
 
Abstract: Automated formative feedback has emerged as a focal point in educational technology research, as large language 
models (LLMs) offer the prospect of providing personalized commentary on student writing at a scale that human instructors 
alone cannot match. W hat is less well examined, however, is how the underlying prompting design —particularly the choice 
between single-agent and multi -agent setups —shapes the pedagogical value of the feedback produced. To examine this 
question, we conducted a control led comparison across four prompting configurations on a corpus of 200 undergraduate 
argumentative essays: a zero -shot single-agent baseline, a chain -of-thought single-agent variant, a dual -role multi-agent 
pipeline in which one model drafts feedback and a nother critiques it, and a tri -role multi-agent pipeline that introduces a 
dedicated revision stage on top of the draft -and -critique loop. Each set of feedback outputs was assessed along a multi -
dimensional rubric covering accuracy, specificity, constructi veness, and tone, with three trained raters scoring independently. 
We also computed automated textual similarity metrics against expert -authored reference feedback to complement the human 
ratings and provide a more independent check. The tri -role multi-agent configuration produced the highest composite quality 
scores and, notably, the lowest rates of over -praise and hallucinated claims about essay content. The chain -of-thought single-
agent variant, while not topping the rankings, delivered surprisingly clos e quality at a fraction of the inference cost, making it 
an attractive option when computational budget or latency matters. We close by discussing what these patterns mean in 
practice for educators and developers looking to integrate LLM -based feedback age nts into higher-education writing 
workflows at scale.

1 INTRODUCTION 
1.1 BACKGROUND: THE GROWING ROLE OF 
LLM-BASED AGENTS IN EDUCATIONAL 
FEEDBACK 
The rapid advancement of large language models has 
reshaped computational approaches to natural language 
understanding and generation. Chain -of-thought prompting, 
as demonstrated by Wei et al. [1], revealed that structured 
reasoning steps embedded within prompts can substantial l y 
improve the quality and coherence of LLM outputs across a 
range of complex tasks. This foundational insight has since 
been extended to educational applications, where the demand 
for high -quality, individualized feedback far exceeds the 
capacity of human instructors in large enrollment courses. 
Kasneci et al. [2] provided one of the earliest 
comprehensive analyses of LLM integration in educational 
settings, identifying personalized feedback generation as a 
particularly promising application domain. Their work 
highlighted that LLMs possess the linguistic fluency an d 
contextual adaptability required to approximate the formati ve 
feedback traditionally delivered by human tutors. The 
educational landscape has since witnessed a proliferation of 
LLM-powered tools designed to support learning, ranging 
from automated essay scoring systems to interactive tutoring 
chatbots. The convergence of these technological capabilities 
with pedagogical needs creates a compelling case for 
systematic investigation into how different prompting 
architectures influence feedb ack quality.

1.2 RESEARCH GAP: LACK OF EMPIRICAL 
COMPARISON BETWEEN SINGLE-AGENT AND 
MULTI-AGENT FEEDBACK STRATEGIES 
Despite growing interest in LLM -based educational 
tools, a critical gap persists in the literature: the absence of 
controlled empirical comparisons between single -agent and 
multi-agent prompting architectures specifically tailored to 
formative feedback tas ks. Wu et al. [3] introduced the 
AutoGen framework, which enables the orchestration of 
multiple LLM agents engaged in structured conversational 
workflows. This multi -agent paradigm offers the theoretical 
advantage of distributing cognitive subtasks —such as content 
evaluation, tone calibration, and error detection —across 
specialized agent roles. Yet, whether this architectural 
complexity translates into measurably superior feedback 
quality in educational contexts remains an open empirical 
question. 
1.3 RESEARCH QUESTIONS AND CONTRIBUTIONS 
OF THIS STUDY 
Chen et al. [4] established in their extensive review that 
artificial intelligence applications in education require 
rigorous, evidence -based evaluation to move beyond 
anecdotal claims of effectiveness. Building on this principle, 
the present study poses three research q uestions: (RQ1) How 
do single -agent and multi -agent LLM prompting strategies 
differ in the quality of formative feedback they generate? 
(RQ2) What types of feedback errors —over-praise, over -
inference, and hallucination —are most prevalent und er each 
strategy? (RQ3) Under what conditions does multi -agent 
collaboration provide measurable advantages over single -
agent approaches? The contributions of this work include a 
reproducible experimental framework, a multi -dimensional 
evaluation rubric, an d empirically grounded 
recommendations for deploying LLM -based feedback agents 
in higher education. 
2 RELATED WORK 
2.1 LLM-BASED AUTOMATED FEEDBACK IN 
EDUCATION 
The application of LLMs to automated feedback 
generation has evolved rapidly from simple prompt -response 
paradigms to more sophisticated agentic architectures. Yao et 
al. [5] proposed the ReAct framework, which interleaves 
reasoning traces with action steps, enabling LLM agents to 
decompose complex tasks into manageable sub -procedures. 
This reasoning -action synergy has direct relevance to 
educational feedback, where an effecti ve response must 
simultaneously diagnose student errors, generate corrective 
suggestions, and maintain a supportive pedagogical tone. 
Guo et al. [6] extended this line of inquiry by developing 
a multi -agent pipeline specifically designed to address 
persistent quality issues in AI -generated educational 
feedback. Their work identified two dominant failure 
modes —over-praise, where the agent provides unwarranted 
positive evaluation, and over -inference, where the agent 
attributes understanding or intentions not evidenced in the 
student's work. The multi -agent architecture proposed in their 
study assigned distinct evaluative roles to separate a gents, 
demonstrating statistically significant reductions in both error 
categories compared to single -agent baselines. 
2.2 SINGLE-AGENT VS. MULTI-AGENT 
ARCHITECTURES IN LLM APPLICATIONS 
The distinction between single -agent and multi -agent 
LLM configurations reflects a broader architectural debate 
within the AI research community. Yan et al. [7] conducted a 
systematic scoping review of LLM applications in education, 
noting that the majority of deployed systems rely on single -
agent designs due to their simplicity and lower computational 
overhead. Their analysis also revealed that single -agent 
systems are more susceptible to systematic biases, as the 
absence of internal review mechanisms allo ws errors to 
propagate unchecked through the generation pipeline [8]. 
2.3 PROMPTING STRATEGIES FOR EDUCATIONAL 
CONTEXTS: CHAIN-OF-THOUGHT, ROLE-
PLAYING, AND RUBRIC-GUIDED 
APPROACHES 
The design of prompting strategies constitutes a critical 
variable in LLM -based educational applications. Abu -
Rasheed et al. [9] demonstrated that augmenting LLM prompts 
with structured knowledge representations —such as 
knowledge graphs —can substantially improve the contextual 
relevance and explanatory depth of generated educational 
content. This finding suggests that the informati on 
architecture embedded within prompts exerts a measurabl e 
influence on output quality, independent of the underlying 
model's capabilities [10]. 
Dan et al. [11] provided a comprehensive taxonomy of 
LLM agent applications in education, categorizing prompting 
strategies along dimensions including role specification, 
reasoning depth, and collaborative structure. Their survey 
identified three dominant prompting parad igms in current 
educational AI research: chain -of-thought prompting, which 
guides the agent through explicit reasoning steps; role -
playing prompting, which assigns the agent a specific 
pedagogical persona; and rubric -guided prompting, which 
anchors the agent's evaluation to predefined assessment 
criteria. [12][13]The present study operationalizes each of these 
paradigms within both single -agent and multi -agent 
configurations to enable direct empirical comparison.

3 METHODOLOGY 
3.1 TASK DESIGN: FORMATIVE FEEDBACK 
GENERATION ON STUDENT ASSIGNMENTS 
The experimental task required each prompting strategy 
to generate formative feedback on undergraduate 
argumentative essays. Bernabei et al. [14] emphasized that 
educational AI agents must be evaluated against authenti c 
pedagogical tasks rather than synthetic benchmarks to ensure 
ecological validity. Guided by this principle, the study 
collected a corpus of 200 argumentative essays written by 
second-year undergraduate students enrolled in an English 
academic writing course at a comprehensive university. Eac h 
essay addressed one of four assigned topics related to 
contemporary social issues, with a required length of 500 to 
800 words [15]. The essays were pre -scored by the course 
instructor using a standardized analytic rubric covering thesis 
clarity, argument development, evidence use, organization, 
and language mechanics. This pre -scoring served as the 
ground-truth quality reference agai nst which AI -generated 
feedback was later compared. 
The formative feedback task was defined as follows: 
given a student essay and the analytic rubric, the LLM agent 
must produce a feedback response that (a) identifies specific 
strengths and weaknesses in the essay, (b) provides actionabl e 
suggestions for re vision, and (c) maintains a constructive and 
pedagogically appropriate tone. No constraints were imposed 
on feedback length, as variation in verbosity across strategies 
constituted an additional variable of analytical interest [16][17].

3.2 PROMPTING STRATEGY DESIGN: SINGLE-
AGENT BASELINES AND MULTI-AGENT 
CONFIGURATIONS 
The design of each prompting strategy was informed by 
established frameworks in both prompt engineering and 
educational assessment theory. Dai et al. [18] outlined key 
principles for aligning LLM -generated feedback with 
formative assessment practices, including the need for 
diagnostic specificity, forward -looking guidance, and 
learner-centered language. These principles were embedded 
into the prompt templat es for all four strategies, ensuring that 
observed quality differences could be attributed to 
architec tural and prompting variations rather than 
inconsistencies in instructional framing.

Strategy S1 served as the minimal baseline. The prompt 
consisted of a system instruction defining the agent's role as 
a writing instructor, followed by the student essay and the 
analytic rubric. No additional reasoning scaffolding was 
provided [19][20]. Strategy S2 augmented the S1 template with 
an explicit chain -of-thought directive, instructing the agent to 
evaluate the essay along each rubric dimension in sequence, 
document its reasoning for each dimension, and only then 
compose the integrated feedba ck response. 
Strategy S3 introduced a dual -agent architecture. The 
Evaluator agent received the same prompt as S2 and 
generated an initial feedback draft. This draft, along with the 
original essay and rubric, was then passed to the Reviewer 
agent, whose prompt instruct ed it to identify instances of 
over-praise, unsupported claims, vague language, or missing 
rubric dimensions in the Evaluator's output [21]. The Reviewer 
produced a revised feedback response incorporating its 
corrections. Strategy S4 extended S3 by introdu cing a Student 
Simulator agent positioned between the Evaluator and 
Reviewer. The Student Simulator's prompt instructed it to 
read the Evaluator's feedback from the perspective of the 
essay's author and generate a list of potential points of 
confusion, per ceived unfairness, or unclear suggestions [22]. 
The Reviewer then received both the Evaluator's original 
feedback and the Student Simulator's concerns, producing a 
final feedback response that addressed both evaluati ve 
accuracy and student comprehensibilit y. 
All experiments were conducted using GPT -4-Turbo 
(gpt-4-turbo-2024 -04-09) accessed through the OpenAI API 
with temperature set to 0.3 and maximum output tokens set 
to 2048. Each essay was processed through all four strategies, 
yielding a total of 800 feedb ack instances (200 essays × 4 
strategies).

3.3 EVALUATION FRAMEWORK: AUTOMATED 
METRICS AND HUMAN EXPERT SCORING 
The evaluation framework combined human expert 
scoring with automated textual similarity metrics to provide 
a comprehensive assessment of feedback quality. Zhang et al . 
[23] demonstrated that multi -source evaluation combining 
human judgment with computational metrics yields more 
robust assessments of LLM -generated educational content 
than either approach alone. 
Three doctoral students with experience in English 
composition instruction served as independent human raters. 
Each rater evaluated all 800 feedback instances using a five -
dimension analytic rubric, with each dimension scored on a 
five-point Likert scale (1 = very poor, 5 = excellent). The five 
evaluation dimensions were: Accuracy (factual correctness of 
the feedback relative to the essay content), Specificity (degree

3.4 DATASET DESCRIPTION AND EXPERIMENTAL 
SETUP 
The essay corpus was collected during the Spring 2024 
semester with institutional ethics approval and informed 
consent from all participating students. Essays were 
anonymized by removing student identifiers and replacing 
proper nouns with generic placehold ers where necessary. The 
four essay topics were: (T1) the role of social media in 
democratic participation, (T2) the ethics of artificial 
intelligence in hiring decisions, (T3) the impact of remote 
work on organizational culture, and (T4) universal basi c 
income as a policy instrument. Topic assignment was 
randomized across students to control for self -selection 
effects. 
The computational infrastructure consisted of a 
dedicated server running Python 3.11 with the OpenAI 
Python SDK (v1.14.0). API calls were executed sequentiall y 
with a 2-second inter-request delay to avoid rate -limiting. For 
multi-agent strategies (S3 and S 4), agent interactions were 
orchestrated through a custom pipeline that managed message 
passing and context accumulation across agent turns. All 
prompts, API responses, and evaluation scores were logged 
in a structured SQLite database for reproducibility.

4.2 ANALYSIS OF OVER-PRAISE, OVER-
INFERENCE, AND HALLUCINATION RATES 
A critical dimension of feedback quality concerns the 
prevalence of systematic errors. Scarlatos et al. [25] 
demonstrated that dialogue -driven LLM agents can reduce 
diagnostic errors through iterative conversational probing, a 
mechanism functionally analogous to the multi -turn review 
process embedded in the multi -agent strategies evaluated here. 
In the present s tudy, all 800 feedback instances were 
independently coded by two raters for the presence of four 
error types: Over-Praise (unwarranted positive eval uation not 
supported by the essay content), Over -Inference (attribution 
of student intentions or understanding not evidenced in the 
text), Hallucination (reference to essay content that does not 
exist), and Omission (failure to address a rubric dimension 
with identifiable issues) [26].

threshold in all categories and S1 bars e xceeding it in three of 
four categories (all except Hallucination, which falls just 
above at 21.5%). Error bars representing 95% confidence 
intervals are displayed atop each bar. The chart includes a 
secondary annotation overlay highlighting the relative 
reduction percentages between S1 and S4 for each error type, 
connected by diagonal arrows spanning each cluster. 
4.3 IMPACT OF AGENT ROLE DECOMPOSITION 
ON FEEDBACK SPECIFICITY AND 
ACTIONABILITY 
The Specificity and Actionability dimensions merit 
focused analysis, as these qualities most directly determine 
the practical utility of formative feedback from a student's 
perspective. Across all 200 essays, feedback instances 
generated by S4 contained a mean of 4.7 specific textual 
references (direct mentions of particular sentences, phrases, 
or structural elements in the student's essay), compared to 3.8 
for S3, 2.9 for S2, and 1.6 for S1. A Kruskal -Wallis test 
confirmed a significant effect of strategy on specific 
reference count, H(3) = 189.4, p < 0.001. 
The Actionability scores also revealed meaningful 
qualitative differences [27]. S1 feedback frequently employed 
generic directives such as "improve your argument structure" 
without specifying which structural element required revision 
or how the revision should be approached. S2 feedback 
showed improved specificity in diagnosis but occasionall y 
lacked concrete revision guidance. S3 feedback benefited 
from the Reviewer agent's tendency to transform vague 
evaluative statements into targeted suggestions [28]. S4 
feedback demonstrated the highest actionability scores, 
attributable to the Student Simulator's role in flagging 
ambiguous or potentially confusing suggestions before the 
Reviewer finalized the response. 
4.4 DISCUSSION: WHEN DOES MULTI-AGENT 
COLLABORATION OUTPERFORM SINGLE-
AGENT APPROACHES? 
The empirical evidence presented above reveals a 
consistent advantage for multi -agent configurations across al l 
evaluated dimensions. Cohn et al. proposed that adapti ve 
scaffolding in LLM -based pedagogical agents should be 
grounded in learning -theoretic pr inciples, with agent roles 
mapped to specific instructional functions. The present 
findings align with this theoretical position: the 
decomposition of the feedback generation task into 
specialized sub -roles—evaluation, student perspective -taking, 
and quali ty review —mirrors the multi -faceted nature of 
expert human feedback practices. 
The performance gap between single -agent and multi -
agent strategies is not uniform across essay quality levels. A 
stratified analysis by instructor pre -score quartiles revealed 
that the advantage of S4 over S2 was most pronounced for 
essays in the second q uartile (instructor scores 61 –71), where 
the CQS difference was 0.74 points. For the highest -scoring 
essays (fourth quartile, scores 85 –96), the CQS difference 
narrowed to 0.38 points. This pattern suggests that multi -
agent architectures provide the greate st incremental value 
when processing essays of intermediate quality, where 
diagnostic complexity is highest and the risk of over -praise or 
omission is most acute. High -quality essays present fewer 
diagnostic challenges, reducing the marginal benefit of 
add itional agent perspectives. 
The computational cost of multi -agent strategies 
constitutes a practical consideration. The mean API token 
consumption per feedback instance was 847 tokens for S1, 
1,264 tokens for S2, 2,531 tokens for S3, and 3,892 tokens 
for S4. The S4 strategy consumed approximately 4.6 times 
the tokens of S1, translating to proportionally higher API 
costs. For institutions operating under budget constraints, S2 
offers a pragmatic middle ground —achieving 86.5% of S4's 
CQS at 32.5% of its token cost.

5 CONCLUSION AND FUTURE 
WORK 
5.1 SUMMARY OF KEY FINDINGS 
This study conducted a systematic empirical 
comparison of four LLM prompting strategies —ranging from 
zero-shot single -agent to tri -role multi -agent 
configurations —for automated formative feedback 
generation on undergraduate argumentative essays. The 
experimental results demonstrate that multi -agent 
architectures produce measurably superior feedback across 
all five evaluated dimensions: accuracy, specificity, 
actionability, constructive tone, and completeness. The tri -
role configuration (S4), which incorpora tes an Evaluator, 
Student Simulator, and Reviewer, achieved the highest 
composite quality score (CQS = 4.21 on a 5 -point scale) and 
the lowest error rates across all four error categories. The 
chain -of-thought single -agent strategy (S2) emerged as a 
cost-effective alternative, delivering competitive quality at 
substantially lower computational expense. 
5.2 PRACTICAL IMPLICATIONS FOR AI-ASSISTED 
EDUCATIONAL FEEDBACK 
The findings carry direct implications for the design and 
deployment of AI -assisted feedback tools in higher education. 
Instructors seeking to implement LLM -based feedback at 
scale should consider the trade -off between feedback quali ty 
and computational co st. The dual -agent strategy (S3) may 
represent an optimal compromise for many institutional 
contexts, providing meaningful reductions in over -praise and 
hallucination rates at moderate token costs. The inclusion of 
a Student Simulator agent in S4 addresses a dimension of 
feedback quality —comprehensibility from the learner's 
perspective —that is frequently overlooked in automated 
feedback systems. This role -based decomposition can be 
adapted to other educational tasks beyond essay feedback, 
including code rev iew, lab report assessment, and peer 
evaluation scaffolding. 
5.3 LIMITATIONS AND DIRECTIONS FOR FUTURE 
RESEARCH 
Several limitations warrant consideration. The study 
relied on a single LLM (GPT -4-Turbo), and the 
generalizability of the observed performance patterns to other 
model families —such as Claude, Gemini, or open -source 
alternatives like Llama —remains to be es tablished through 
cross-model replication studies. The essay corpus was drawn 
from a single institution and a single disciplinary context 
(English academic writing), limiting the ecological breadth of 
the findings. The human evaluation, while conducted by 
trained raters with acceptable inter -rater reliability, involved 
only three evaluators; expanding the rater pool in future work 
would strengthen the robustness of quality assessments. 
Future research should investigate the longitudinal 
effects of multi -agent feedback on student writing 
development. The present study evaluated feedback quali ty 
as a static property of a single interaction; tracking whether 
students who receive multi -agent feedback demonstrate 
greater revision gains over a semester would provide stronger 
evidence for the pedagogical value of architectural 
complexity. Cross -disciplinary replication in STEM writing, 
foreign language learning, and professional communicati on 
contexts would help delineate the boundary conditions of 
multi-agent advantages. The integration of student percepti on 
surveys and think -aloud protocols would complement the 
expert evaluation approach employed here by capturing how 
learners actually interpre t and act upon AI -generated 
feedback.
