ABSTRACT Large language models (LLMs) are emerging as powerful tools for hardware design, with
recent work exploring their ability to generate register-transfer level (RTL) code directly from natural-
language specifications. This paper presents a critical review and empirical evaluation of LLM-based
RTL generation. We examine thirty-one published efforts, classifying their use of techniques such
as fine-tuning, reinforcement learning, retrieval-augmented prompting, and multi-agent orchestration
across eight methodological dimensions including debugging support, post-RTL metrics, and benchmark
development. Building on this synthesis, we experimentally evaluate frontier commercial and open-weight
models—GPT-4.1, GPT-4.1-mini, Claude Sonnet 4, and Llama 4 Maverick —on the V erilogEvalV2 and
RTLLM-v2.0 benchmarks under both single-shot generation and a lightweight ReAct-style reflection loop,
with compilation and simulation performed through Icarus V erilog interfaced via the Model Context Protocol
(MCP). Results show that these models achieve up to 89.74% on V erilogEval and 96.08% on RTLLM,
matching or exceeding prior domain-specific pipelines without specialized fine-tuning. Detailed failure
analysis reveals systematic error modes, including FSM mis-sequencing, handshake drift, blocking vs.
non-blocking misuse, and state-space oversimplification. Finally, we outline a forward-looking research
roadmap toward natural-language-to-System-on-Chip (SoC) design, emphasizing realistic benchmarks and
open flows, richer specification formalisms, AI-human collaborative design environments, and system-level
feedback that spans physical design, firmware, and design space exploration. Together, this work provides
a synthesis of recent advances and a baseline evaluation of frontier LLMs, highlighting opportunities and
challenges in moving toward AI-native electronic design automation.

I. INTRODUCTION
Designing modern system-on-chips (SoCs) remains one of
the most challenging tasks in computing systems engi-
neering. An SoC integrates heterogeneous accelerators for
cryptography, signal and image processing, and machine
learning, each with deep pipelines, hierarchical reuse, and
stringent power, performance, and area (PPA) require-
ments [1], [2]. The design flow involves multiple stages—
capturing specifications, planning microarchitectures, imple-
menting register-transfer level (RTL) modules, synthesizing

task of translating natural-language or informal specifications
into correct RTL remains largely manual, error-prone, and

The emergence of large language models (LLMs) has
opened new opportunities to address this gap [7]. LLMs

of work to identify the techniques explored—ranging from

tool use [13] and reinforcement learning [14]—and to

Two recent surveys examine the use of large language
models across the EDA stack, but differ fundamentally
from our work in both scope and methodology. Pan et al.
[17] present a comprehensive catalog of LLM applications
spanning system-level design, RTL, synthesis, physical

type and customization strategy. While valuable as a broad
reference, their analysis is largely descriptive and does
not include controlled experimental evaluation, cross-model
comparison under a unified protocol, or critical examination
of benchmark limitations. Similarly, Xu et al. [18] provide
an extensive taxonomy of LLM-based techniques and outline
high-level challenges and future directions across the EDA
workflow, but stop short of empirically evaluating model
behavior or validating proposed methodologies.
By contrast, our work is narrowly focused on the
specification-to-RTL problem, a stage that is both less
mature and more open-ended than downstream synthesis
or physical design, and where errors in interpretation,
decomposition, and architectural reasoning are most pro-
nounced. We explicitly focus on the prevailing Natural
Language (NL)-to-RTL paradigm to rigorously quantify its
limits, establishing an empirical baseline that demonstrates
where prose-based generation breaks down and motivating
the transition to formal specifications for complex, long-
context designs. This focused scope enables a deeper
critical analysis of agentic refinement strategies, benchmark
adequacy, and specification structure. Crucially, unlike prior
surveys, we conduct a controlled experimental evaluation
of multiple frontier LLMs—both proprietary and open-
weight—on standardized benchmarks (V erilogEvalV2 and
RTLLM-v2.0), directly comparing baseline and agentic
approaches under identical conditions. This allows us to
quantify when agentic refinement meaningfully improves
reliability, identify benchmark saturation effects, and analyze
systematic failure modes exposed by current evaluation suites
— insights that are largely absent from existing surveys.
Finally, while automated RTL-to-GDSII flows such as
OpenROAD [6] and recent AI-assisted backend tools [19],
[20], [21], [22] address downstream phases of the design
flow, they operate on already well-structured RTL and
constraints. Our review deliberately excludes these stages to
concentrate on the specification-to-RTL bottleneck, where
ambiguity, weak contracts, and inadequate benchmarks
remain fundamental obstacles. In this sense, our work com-
plements prior surveys by providing empirical grounding and
critical depth in a narrowly scoped but still methodologically
evolving phase of the hardware design pipeline, particularly
with respect to benchmark rigor, agentic evaluation, and
systematic failure analysis.
This paper makes three contributions:
1) Analysis of recent work and benchmarks: We review
and synthesize techniques proposed in the literature,
showing how approaches have evolved over the past
three years and how they align with benchmark design.
2) Experimental evaluation of state-of-the-art LLMs:
We show that baseline single-shot generation and
simple ReAct-style [23] reflection with Icarus V er-
ilog simulation interfaced via MCP—achieves strong
performance on current benchmarks, suggesting that
these benchmarks may no longer stress the limits of
today’s models. This highlights the need for richer, more
complex benchmarks to drive further progress.
3) Identification of recurring failure modes: By analyz-
ing failed designs in depth, we surface systematic issues
such as finite state machine mis-sequencing, handshake
protocol drift, and misuse of blocking vs. non-blocking
semantics, offering insight into why LLMs struggle with
hardware concurrency and temporal reasoning.
Together, these contributions provide the reader with
both a comprehensive map of existing approaches and a
grounded assessment of how well current LLMs perform.
More importantly, they highlight where progress has been
made, where gaps remain, and what research directions are
most promising.

Section V outlines a research roadmap for natural-language-

II. PRIOR WORK ON LLM BASED RTL GENERATION
We surveyed the literature on RTL generation using large
language models (LLMs), focusing on work published
from the introduction of publicly accessible LLMs in late
2022 through mid-2025. This period spans the release
of foundational models such as ChatGPT [24], Code
Llama [25], DeepSeek [26], Gemini [27], and Qwen [28],
which made LLM-based code generation broadly feasible for
hardware applications.
We selected 31 works that address RTL synthesis, verifi-
cation, or refinement using generative models. Collectively,
they span the state of the art in LLM-based hardware
design, employing techniques such as supervised fine-
tuning [12], reinforcement learning, [14] retrieval-augmented
prompting [9], and multi-agent coordination [10].

A. SURVEY METHODOLOGY
Each project is evaluated along eight methodological dimen-
sions that capture the breadth of techniques employed in
LLM-based RTL generation. The fine-tuning (FT) dimension
reflects whether the model is further trained on RTL-specific
corpora, typically drawn from GitHub or curated repositories.
The reinforcement learning (RL) dimension identifies
models that incorporate feedback-driven optimization,
such as policy-gradient or reward-weighted fine-tuning,
to improve correctness under evaluation constraints.
Agentic execution (Ag) denotes the use of multi-agent
workflows, where distinct agents (e.g., planner, generator,
verifier) coordinate task decomposition, code generation, and
evaluation.
The retrieval or in-context learning (RAG) dimension cap-
tures systems that enhance generation with example-based
conditioning or retrieval-augmented prompts, often improv-
ing robustness on long-tail designs.
Debugging support (Dbg) refers to mechanisms that
help localize and correct faults, such as Abstract Syntax
Tree(AST)-level backtracing, simulation waveform check-
points, or compiler error alignment.
Post-RTL metrics (Beyond) assesses whether the approach
accounts for downstream qualities of the generated RTL—
such as gate count, synthesis efficiency, Boolean Satisfiabil-
ity (SA T) solving time, or resource utilization.
The benchmark contribution (BM) dimension marks
whether a paper introduces a new benchmark suite for
evaluating RTL generation systems.
Finally, prompt engineering or chain-of-thought (PE)
refers to the deliberate design of structured prompts that guide
model reasoning or decompose complex specifications, and
can be extended beyond text to include multimodal inputs
such as block diagrams, dataflow graphs, timing waveforms,
and interface schematics that anchor architectural intent and
reduce ambiguity in specification interpretation. Together,
these dimensions provide a structured basis for comparison
across systems and inform the taxonomy presented in
Table 1.

D. OBSERVATIONS
• Fine-tuning as a starting point. Early systems such
as V erigen, AutoVCoder, BetterV , and ChipNeMo
rely primarily on supervised fine-tuning on GitHub-
style datasets. More recent work (e.g., V eriReason,
RTLCoder) incorporates RL to improve functional
correctness on benchmark suites like V erilogEval.
• Rise of agentic frameworks. Projects such as MAGE
and V erilogCoder demonstrate that multi-agent orches-
tration with simulation feedback can outperform mono-
lithic prompting. This trend extends to AssertLLM
(multi-role pipeline for SV A generation), V eriOpt
(PPA-aware agent loop), and CorrectBench (LLM-
as-generator-and-judge). A key challenge is enabling
agents to reason collectively over a shared context.
• Retrieval and in-context learning address edge cases.
Systems like AutoVCoder and RTLFixer incorporate
RAG to retrieve relevant design examples or compiler-
error templates. PyHDLEval extends this to multi-
ple Python based DSLs. Recent HLS-oriented work
(C2HLSC, HLSPilot, LHS) relies heavily on in-context
strategies and synthesis-feedback prompting to refactor
C code, while V eriOpt incorporates exemplar-guided
design optimizations.
• Debugging strategies are becoming more sophis-
ticated. Early work relied on binary pass/fail simu-
lation outcomes. Recent methods integrate Abstract
Syntax Tree (AST) back-tracing (V erilogCoder),
checkpoint-based waveform inspection (MAGE), and
pattern-matching against compiler diagnostics (RTL-
Fixer). AutoBench and CorrectBench add iterative
correction and voting-based self-judgment, while
V eriOpt leverages synthesis reports and timing diagrams
for reflective corrections. Nevertheless, accurate fault
attribution remains an open problem.
• Post-RTL metrics are underexplored. BetterV intro-
duces design-time metrics such as Y osys node count
and SA T solve time. V eriAssist adds FPGA resource
utilization. V eriOpt advances this trend further with
explicit PPA-aware prompting, while LHS optimizes for
latency/area trade-offs. However, systematic integration
of such post-RTL metrics remains limited.
• Benchmarks shape progress. V erilogEval and RTLLM
remain the dominant evaluation suites, while HDLBits
and RTL_Repo expand test coverage to code-completion
and long-context inference. AssertLLM contributes
a dedicated SV A benchmark. Still, few datasets
stress synthesis quality, timing closure, or end-to-end
hardware viability.
E. EVOLUTION OVER TIME
LLM-based RTL generation is a recent area of study,
emerging only after large language models became widely
accessible with the public release of ChatGPT in November
2022. This accessibility catalyzed a wave of research that
adapted LLMs to hardware design tasks, initially through
supervised fine-tuning and later through more structured,
interactive, and feedback-driven approaches. The following
timeline outlines key phases of technical progression:
1) 2023. Initial efforts such as ChipChat and ChipNeMo
demonstrated the feasibility of applying large language
models to hardware design tasks, including code gener-
ation and EDA tool interaction. These systems primarily
focused on exploratory use cases and domain adaptation
of general-purpose models.
2) Early 2024. Projects such as V erigen and BetterV
established a baseline for RTL generation using LLMs
through domain-specific supervised fine-tuning and
basic prompt engineering. These approaches improved
performance on benchmark tasks but lacked structured
reasoning or interactive workflows.
3) Mid 2024. Research shifted toward more structured
prompting methods, including instruction prompting,
chain-of-thought reasoning, in-context learning, and
retrieval-augmented generation. Most systems in this
phase remained single-agent, relying on static prompt
composition or few-shot exemplars with limited use
of external tool feedback. Notable examples include
AutoVCoder, RTLFixer, and PyHDLEval.
4) Late 2024. Agentic execution emerged as a dominant
paradigm, with systems such as MAGE, V erilogCoder,
and AIVRIL introducing multi-agent workflows capable
of decomposing tasks, coordinating generation and
verification, and iteratively refining outputs based on
simulation and analysis. AutoBench extended this line
of work to automatic testbench generation, integrating
iterative correction and evaluation strategies.

III. EVALUATION OF BENCHMARKS WITH
STATE-OF-THE-ART LLMS
While prior work has introduced techniques aimed at
improving benchmark scores, comparatively little attention
has been paid to the adequacy of the benchmarks themselves
or to the specific failure modes they reveal. A central question
is whether current benchmarks are well designed to capture
RTL complexity, or whether construction artifacts — such
as incomplete prompts, underspecified contracts, or flawed
testbenches confound the interpretation of reported results.
In parallel, it remains unclear how effectively contemporary
LLMs—without task-specific agents, multi-step planning,
or external optimizers—can directly produce verifiable RTL
from specification.
We therefore establish a baseline along two axes: (i) the
generative capabilities and limitations of current models, and
(ii) the validity and diagnostic power of existing benchmarks.
Our study is guided by the following research questions:
• RQ1: How well do current LLMs perform on prompt-
to-RTL generation when evaluated without auxiliary
agents, planning mechanisms, or external optimization?
• RQ2: To what extent do observed failures stem from
intrinsic model limitations versus artifacts of benchmark
design (e.g., incomplete prompts, incorrect or under-
specified testbenches)?
• RQ3: Are existing benchmarks sufficiently well
designed to provide a reliable and comprehensive

We focus on two widely used benchmarks: V erilogEvalV2

To isolate model quality from system-level orchestration,
we adopt two inference settings. The first is a non-agentic
baseline: single prompt with temperature sampling, reporting
pass@5 (i.e., success if any of five samples passes the
testbench). The second is an agentic ReAct-style loop
allowing up to 10 iterations of feedback from simulation to
guide revisions. This approximates the outer loop of recent
agentic frameworks while preserving a controlled evaluation
interface.

systems. The models selected include GPT-4.1 and its

B. TOOL INTEGRATION VIA MODEL CONTEXT PROTOCOL
(MCP)
The evaluation framework connects Icarus V erilog [60] to
the LLM via MCP , a standardized interface for external
tool calls by agents [61]. At the backend, an MCP server
manages compilation and simulation tasks: it invokes Icarus
with configurable timeouts, captures both stdout and stderr
streams, and normalizes these into structured JSON responses
that report status, outputs, and errors. At the frontend,
an MCP client embedded in the agent runtime issues requests
to this server through well-defined endpoints. This separation
allows the agent to remain agnostic to tool invocation
details while still receiving actionable, structured feedback.
In practice, agentic runs invoke MCP endpoints to (i)
compile generated RTL modules, (ii) execute correspond-
ing testbenches, and (iii) collect error traces or assertion
violations. These responses are fed back into the reasoning
loop, enabling the agent to iteratively design, test, and refine
candidate implementations until convergence or a maximum
loop count is reached. While the current setup employs
Icarus V erilog for simulation, the MCP abstraction makes
it straightforward to extend the framework to other tools
such as V erilator [62] for simulation, SymbiY osys [63] for
formal verification, or Y osys [36] for synthesis, ensuring the
architecture can evolve with broader evaluation needs.
C. AGENTIC SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE
The SystemV erilog generation framework is implemented
as a stateful workflow in LangGraph [64], an open-source
library for building multi-step LLM applications as explicit
state machines. A directed graph coordinates two core stages:
(i) candidate RTL generation and (ii) MCP-backed validation.
Control flow routes based on pass/fail, enabling termination
when tests succeed or iterative refinement when they fail.
Context persists across iterations, combining the original
prompt with compiler/simulation feedback. A reflection step
proposes targeted edits, which seed the next generation
attempt. LangGraph manages state transitions and loop
control, enabling robust iterative improvement.

IV. ANALYSIS AND DISCUSSION
The quantitative results in the previous section show that
newer foundation models, when paired with a single agent
ReAct refinement loop, achieve strong performance on both
V erilogEval and RTLLM. However, aggregate pass rates do
not explain why failures persist or which mistakes dominate.
A closer analysis of failures provides insight into current
LLM limitations for RTL generation, the kinds of corrections
needed in benchmark prompts/testbenches, and systematic
error patterns across designs.
We first examine RTLLM, where several prompt/testbench
adjustments were required and where the radix-2
divider remained unsolved despite repeated attempts.
We then turn to V erilogEval, focusing on Prob154
fsm_ps2data as a representative failure case in FSM
reasoning. Building on these, we synthesize recurring error
patterns and close with recommendations toward reliable
spec-to-RTL generation.
A. RTLLM
During RTLLM evaluation, we made targeted modifications
to align prompts, reference designs, and the simulation
harness. These addressed inconsistencies in text descrip-
tions, missing parameters, and testbench behavior. Examples
include clarifying parameterization in the 64-bit pipelined
adder; adding the missing res_ready signal in the radix-
2 divider prompt; toggling display statements in FIFO
modules to aid reflection; fixing module-name mismatches
in frequency dividers; adjusting the ring-counter testbench
loop bounds; clarifying shift vs. rotate semantics in the barrel
shifter; and correcting instantiation order in the LFSR test-
bench. These adjustments improved benchmark robustness
and enabled systematic reflection across iterations, with an
overall success rate of 96.08%.
Despite these changes, radix2_div failed across mod-
els and settings. Dominant error sources included:
Specification ambiguity: Prompts leave degrees of freedom
(restoring vs. non-restoring, tie-breaking, don’t-care han-
dling), and models often pick a valid but incompatible variant.
Cycle accuracy: Precise per-cycle sequencing (shift–
subtract–restore, final commit) is required; off-by-one or
misordered steps corrupt results.
Bit-width arithmetic: Restoring division needs a 9-bit remain-
der path and borrow detection; models frequently implement
8-bit compares/subtracts.
Finalization: The last quotient bit and final remainder must
be explicitly committed; this step is often omitted.
Sign rules: Signed division requires |a|/|b| internally, quo-
tient sign via a⊕b, and remainder sign matching the dividend;
frequently mishandled.
Handshake logic: Misuse of res_ready/res_valid and
level vs. edge confusion produce deadlocks or stale outputs.
State control:Incorrect sequencing ofstart/active/done
leads to premature termination or extra cycles.
Edge cases: Divide-by-zero, 0/N , N /N are often unspecified
or incorrectly handled.
Syntax vs. semantics: Syntactically valid code with seman-
tic errors (e.g., blocking in sequential logic, replacing
borrow-detect with simple compare).
Lack of internal verification: No internal symbolic trace or
truth-table reasoning to catch subtle algorithmic mistakes
during generation.
Overall, division circuits stress algorithmic rigor, cycle-
level timing, and careful state/handshake control—areas
where current models most often stumble.

C. OBSERVATIONS ON ERRORS MADE BY LLMS
We observe recurring error classes spanning protocol han-
dling, control sequencing, and RTL semantics:
1) PROTOCOL HANDLING AND CONTROL SEQUENCING
Transition priority mistakes: In V erilog, constructs like
if/else if or case statements create an implicit pri-
ority. The first condition that evaluates to true is executed.
This error occurs when the LLM writes code for mutually
exclusive conditions without a clear priority, leading to
behavior that is unpredictable and may differ between
simulation and the actual synthesized hardware.
Moore vs. Mealy confusion: This is a fundamental error in
Finite State Machine (FSM) design. A Moore machine’s
outputs depend only on the current state (they are registered).
A Mealy machine’s outputs depend on the current state and
the current inputs (they are combinational). The error occurs
when a Mealy output is created where a stable, registered
Moore output was intended. This can introduce glitches on
the output, as it will change immediately with the input rather
than only on a clock edge.
Over/under-registering signals: This refers to incorrect
pipeline staging. Over-registering is adding unnecessary
registers (always @(posedge clk)) in a data path,
which adds clock cycle latency. Under-registering is failing
to register a signal when needed, such as exposing the
combinational ‘‘next-state’’ logic of an FSM as an output
instead of the registered ‘‘current-state,’’ which can cause
timing violations or glitches.
Missing hysteresis/invariants: Hysteresis is the dependence
of a system’s state on its history. An LLM might generate
logic that reacts to every single rising edge of a noisy input
signal, causing an output to ‘‘flap’’ rapidly. A proper design
would include logic to ensure the signal is stable for a few
clock cycles before acting on it.
Boundary/off-by-one errors: This is a common programming
error that is particularly problematic in hardware. It involves
using the wrong comparison operator (e.g., > instead of >=)
or incorrect counter limits, causing an operation to run for one
cycle too many or too few.
Handshake timing drift: In protocols using signals like
valid and ready, timing is critical. This error occurs
when the LLM asserts valid one cycle before the data
is actually stable or de-asserts ready a cycle too late.
This desynchronizes the communicating modules, leading to
incorrect data being latched.
Event vs. level detection: This is the confusion between
reacting to a signal changing versus reacting to its state.
Event/Edge Detection (posedge clk) triggers on the
rising edge. Level Detection (if (enable)) checks if the
signal is high. The error is using one when the other is
required, which can cause missed or repeated triggers.
State-space oversimplification: This is an FSM design flaw
where the LLM improperly combines distinct states. For
example, a protocol might require a module to enter a WAIT
state for at least one clock cycle. The LLM might omit this
state and transition directly, violating the required timing gap.
2) RTL SEMANTICS AND STRUCTURE
Blocking vs. non-blocking misuse: This is one of the
most fundamental V erilog errors. Blocking assignments (=)
are used for combinational logic (always @( *)). Non-
blocking assignments (<=) are used for sequential logic
(always @(posedge clk)). The error is using blocking
assignments in a sequential block, which can create a
simulation race condition and a mismatch between simulation
and synthesized hardware.
Reset semantics drift: An incorrect reset implementation.
This can mean using the wrong polarity (active-high vs.
active-low), using the wrong type (synchronous vs. asyn-
chronous), or forgetting to initialize all registers, which
causes them to start with an unknown (X) value.
Bit-ordering/shift-direction mistakes: A critical error where
data bits are misinterpreted. This includes swapping Most
Significant Bit (MSB) and Least Significant Bit (LSB)
ordering (e.g., wire [7:0] data vs. wire [0:7]
data) or using a left-shift (≪) when a right-shift (≫) was
needed.
Asymmetry violations: Occurs when a design assumes inputs
or protocols are symmetric when they are not. For example,
a request signal might need to be held high until an
acknowledge is received, while the acknowledge itself is a
single-cycle pulse. Treating both as symmetric violates the
protocol.
Algebraic incompleteness: The LLM attempts to simplify a
Boolean logic expression but does so incorrectly by dropping
a term. The resulting logic may pass simple test cases but will
fail on the specific corner cases covered by the missing term.
Output-validity contract ignored: In many designs, output
data is only valid during a specific window, often indicated
by a data_valid signal. This error occurs when the
LLM allows the output bus to be driven with changing
or intermediate values outside of this window, risking a
downstream module latching ‘‘garbage’’ data.
Error-path underspecification: The generated code works
perfectly for normal operation but fails to define what the
hardware should do when an error occurs. Without a defined
error state or recovery mechanism, the module can get stuck
or behave unpredictably.
These patterns show that while models produce syntacti-
cally valid RTL, they often miss temporal correctness, state
consistency, and interface contracts—precisely the aspects
that matter for reliable hardware. Table 6 summarizes these

D. CRITICAL ASSESSMENT OF CURRENT BENCHMARKS
While V erilogEvalV2 and RTLLM-v2.0 provide valuable
standardized evaluation suites, our experimental findings
reveal structural limitations that constrain their diagnostic
power for modern frontier models.
1) SATURATION EFFECTS AND CEILING PERFORMANCE
Several model-benchmark pairs approach high pass rates
under both single-shot and lightweight agentic settings.
In particular, RTLLM exhibits strong baseline performance
for most frontier models, leaving limited headroom for itera-
tive refinement. This suggests partial saturation: benchmarks
originally designed to distinguish earlier-generation models
may no longer adequately stress contemporary systems.
As pass rates converge toward the upper bound, aggregate
metrics become less discriminative, masking qualitative dif-
ferences in architectural reasoning and temporal correctness.
2) LIMITED STRUCTURAL COMPLEXITY
Both benchmarks primarily emphasize single-module
designs with clearly scoped functionality. Although they
include arithmetic, control, and memory elements, they
rarely require hierarchical composition across multiple
interacting modules. Realistic SoC subsystems involve
interface contracts, pipeline boundaries, arbitration logic, and
cross-module invariants that extend beyond the complexity
represented in most current tasks. As a result, present
benchmarks predominantly measure localized synthesis
capability rather than system-level architectural reasoning.
3) SHORT-CONTEXT AND REPOSITORY INDEPENDENCE
Tasks are presented as self-contained specifications with no
requirement to reason over multi-file repositories or long-
context dependencies. Consequently, models are evaluated in
isolation from challenges common in production RTL design,
such as interface reuse, parameter propagation across mod-
ules, shared signal conventions, or incremental modifications
within existing codebases. This setup favors short-horizon
reasoning and limits assessment of long-context consistency.
4) FUNCTIONAL CORRECTNESS AS THE SOLE PRIMARY
METRIC
Evaluation is driven almost exclusively by testbench pass/fail
outcomes. While this is necessary for baseline verification,
it does not capture architectural optimality, resource effi-
ciency, timing feasibility, or maintainability. Two designs
may be functionally equivalent under testbench stimuli yet
differ significantly in pipeline depth, state encoding, reset
discipline, or PPA implications. Current benchmarks do
not distinguish between minimal and structurally robust
solutions.
5) LIMITED STRESS ON TEMPORAL AND PROTOCOL
CONTRACTS
Although certain tasks (e.g., division or FSM protocols)
expose temporal reasoning challenges, most problems
emphasize combinational logic or straightforward sequential
constructs. Complex handshake protocols, multi-cycle invari-
ants, backpressure semantics, and concurrency-heavy designs
are underrepresented. As shown in our failure analysis, these
dimensions are precisely where current LLMs struggle most.
6) BENCHMARK CONSTRUCTION ARTIFACTS
During evaluation, we observed cases requiring prompt
clarification or minor harness corrections. While such adjust-
ments improve robustness, they highlight that benchmark
artifacts—such as underspecified control signals, ambiguous
variants, or fragile testbench assumptions—can confound
interpretation of model capability. Distinguishing intrinsic
model failure from dataset construction noise remains a
nontrivial challenge.
7) IMPLICATION
Collectively, these observations suggest that existing bench-
marks primarily measure short-horizon functional synthesis
under controlled conditions. They are effective for standard-
ized comparison but insufficient for evaluating architectural
reasoning, hierarchical composition, contract satisfaction,
or tool-flow integration. Advancing the field will require
next-generation benchmarks that (i) incorporate multi-
module composition, (ii) integrate synthesis- and timing-
aware metrics, (iii) stress protocol-level temporal correctness,
and (iv) support iterative agentic workflows rather than
single-shot evaluation.
E. FUTURE WORK: TOWARD RELIABLE SPEC-TO-RTL
GENERATION
The recurring errors observed in current LLM-generated RTL
suggest several avenues for advancing research:
Contract-based synthesis: Embedding explicit temporal and
structural contracts into the design process can ensure that
generated control logic respects precedence rules, handshake
timing, and reset semantics by construction.
Schema-driven specifications: Capturing I/O, signal roles,
invariants, and protocol semantics in machine-checkable
schemas can reduce ambiguity and provide stronger
guardrails against misinterpretation during generation.
Counterexample-guided refinement: Leveraging simula-
tion and formal verification traces to drive iterative repair
can help address subtle corner cases such as off-by-one
errors, algebraic incompleteness, and state-space underspec-
ification.
Automated protocol and invariant checkers: Integrating
lightweight protocol monitors and invariant analyzers into the
workflow can catch timing drift, invalid outputs, and missing
recovery sequences early in the design loop.

V. FUTURE WORK: TOWARD
NATURAL-LANGUAGE-TO-SOC DESIGN
Realizing the vision of natural-language-to-SoC (NL-to-
SoC) design requires coordinated advances across multiple
layers of the hardware–software stack. Below we outline key
areas where future research can drive progress.
A. REALISTIC BENCHMARKS AND OPEN FLOWS
Existing spec-to-RTL benchmarks remain limited in realism,
as they predominantly target small, self-contained modules
and evaluate correctness in isolation. To meaningfully
advance AI-driven hardware design, future benchmarks must
introduce concrete structural and methodological enhance-
ments that reflect real SoC development workflows. First,
benchmarks should scale from single modules to composable
subsystem templates, including processor pipelines, memory
controllers, cache hierarchies, accelerators, and on-chip
interconnects, with clearly defined interfaces and integration
constraints. Such compositions naturally expose architectural
trade-offs and cross-module dependencies that are absent
in current module-level suites. The recently published
ArchXBench [65], [66] take an important step toward realism
by moving beyond toy arithmetic and control blocks to a
hierarchically organized, multi-level benchmark suite that
spans cryptography, signal processing, image processing, and
machine learning accelerators.
Second, benchmarks should incorporate PPA-aware evalu-
ation hooks, where tasks are not only functionally constrained
but also specify latency, throughput, area, or power targets.
This can be achieved by coupling RTL correctness with
synthesis- and place-and-route-derived metrics, enabling
assessment of whether generated designs satisfy architectural
intent beyond simulation pass rates. Importantly, these
metrics should be reported in standardized, tool-agnostic
forms to allow reproducible comparison across methods.
Third, specification modalities must extend beyond prose.
Recent work on multi-modal RTL generation [42], [59] sug-
gests that block diagrams, dataflow graphs, FSM diagrams,
timing waveforms, and memory-layout schematics encode
essential structural information that is difficult to capture in
text alone. Future benchmarks should therefore treat such
visual artifacts as first-class specification inputs, paired with
structured textual constraints and executable reference mod-
els. This multi-modal formulation better reflects industrial
practice and enables evaluation of an AI system’s ability to
reason over hierarchy, connectivity, and temporal behavior.
Fourth, benchmarks should be explicitly designed to sup-
port agentic design workflows. Rather than single-shot spec-
to-code tasks, they should expose intermediate artifacts—
partial specifications, failing test cases, architectural mis-
matches, or PPA violations—that invite iterative refinement,
debugging, and architectural exploration. By embedding
checkpoints for diagnosis and repair, benchmarks can eval-
uate whether agentic AI systems can plan, decompose, and
adapt across the full design loop.
Fifth, benchmarks should measure architectural reasoning,
not just code generation. In addition to pass/fail functional
metrics, tasks should include criteria that distinguish whether
the generated RTL matches the intended microarchitecture
(e.g., pipelined vs. iterative realizations, hierarchy and mod-
ule boundaries, and latency/throughput contracts), thereby
rewarding correct architectural choices rather than incidental
functional equivalence.

B. STRUCTURED SPECIFICATIONS BEYOND PROSE
Free-form natural language is too ambiguous for automation,
particularly for complex, long-context repositories where
context windows limit the precision of prose. Controlled
Natural Language (CNL) schemas are required to precisely
encode design contracts, including I/O protocols, timing
constraints, invariants, resource bounds, memory maps,
arbitration policies, and interrupt behavior. To be actionable,
such contracts must be machine-checkable and amenable
to automated reasoning, rather than remaining informal
annotations over prose specifications.
One viable implementation path is to represent contracts
using formal constraint systems grounded in logic program-
ming or standardized verification languages. For example,
interface compatibility, timing bounds, and structural invari-
ants can be formalized using SystemV erilog Assertions
(SV A) or Property Specification Language (PSL). These can
be mapped to facts and rules in an Answer Set Program-
ming (ASP) [67] or SMT-based framework [68], enabling
automated consistency checking, design-space pruning, and
counterexample generation prior to RTL synthesis. In this
formulation, contract satisfaction becomes a solvable deci-
sion problem, allowing an AI-driven design agent to reason
explicitly about which architectural choices are admissible
before generating or refining RTL.
Visual specifications, such as block diagrams, dataflow
graphs, and pipeline schematics, can complement this
approach by supplying structured architectural facts—
component connectivity, hierarchy, and concurrency—that
are difficult to infer reliably from text alone. Rather than
serving as primary specifications, these visual artifacts
can be parsed into symbolic representations that popu-
late the contract system, anchoring high-level architectural
intent while leaving correctness enforcement to formal
constraints.
This naturally supports an agentic specification-to-RTL
workflow in which agents iteratively propose microarchitec-
tural refinements, check them against formalized contracts
using logic solvers, and generate RTL only for contract-
satisfying designs. Contract violations—such as unmet
latency bounds, incompatible interfaces, or illegal arbitration
patterns—can be surfaced as solver-derived counterexam-
ples, enabling targeted debugging and refinement rather than
blind regeneration.
From a benchmarking perspective, this suggests that future
spec-to-RTL benchmarks should include explicit, machine-
checkable contracts alongside specifications and testbenches.
Crucially, this shifts the evaluation metric from simple
functional pass/fail rates to concrete verification targets, such
as Formal Property V erification (FPV) coverage and Bounded
Model Checking (BMC) depth. Such benchmarks would
evaluate not only whether generated RTL is functionally
correct on a limited set of vectors, but whether AI systems
can reason about, satisfy, and debug formal design con-
tracts, moving beyond purely generative evaluation toward
constraint-aware architectural synthesis.
C. AI–HUMAN COLLABORATIVE EDA ENVIRONMENTS
The next generation of design tools should resemble AI
copilots for hardware engineers—akin to Cursor[69] or Copi-
lot [70] for software but tailored to RTL and SoC contexts.
Such environments would allow designers to express intent
via natural language, block diagrams, or waveform sketches,
while the AI generates corresponding code, assertions, and
refinements. Interactive visualization of datapaths, FSMs,
and timing diagrams would enable human oversight and
correction. Seamless interfacing with both open-source and
commercial flows is critical to make these systems practical
in real-world design environments.
D. PHYSICAL DESIGN AND PPA-INFORMED FEEDBACK
Although RTL-to-GDSII flows are mature, their outputs
rarely inform upstream architectural decisions. Future work
should enable PPA-in-the-loop design, where synthesis
and place-and-route metrics directly guide design choices
such as pipelining depth, memory partitioning, and inter-
connect topology. Lightweight machine-learning surrogates
for backend flows can provide near-real-time feedback,
enabling interactive exploration under realistic constraints.
Open backends such as OpenLane [3] and OpenROAD [6]
offer research-friendly entry points, while integration with
commercial flows is necessary to validate scalability and
industry transferability.
E. FIRMWARE AND SYSTEM SOFTWARE GENERATION
Hardware design does not end at RTL. SoC assembly requires
generators that automatically derive interconnects, memory
maps, interrupt tables, and glue logic directly from specifica-
tions. Parameterized IP instantiation should be paired with
interface metadata that drives firmware generation. From
the same specification, the flow should produce device-
tree entries, driver skeletons, and RTOS bring-up collateral,
enabling early hardware–software co-validation.
F. AI-DRIVEN DESIGN SPACE EXPLORATION
Today, design space exploration (DSE)—balancing pipelin-
ing depth, parallelism, memory hierarchy, and interconnect
choices—is largely a manual and time-consuming pro-
cess, restricting the breadth of configurations that can be

VI. CONCLUSION
This paper presented a survey and evaluation of large
language models (LLMs) for specification-to-RTL (spec-
to-RTL) generation, synthesizing findings from thirty-one
recent works and benchmarking frontier models on V er-
ilogEval and RTLLM. Our review highlights the rapid
evolution of the field: early efforts relied on supervised
fine-tuning over GitHub-style datasets, while more recent
approaches embrace multi-agent orchestration, retrieval-
augmented prompting, reinforcement learning, and post-RTL
metrics. Alongside these methods, new benchmarks such as
ArchXBench are beginning to push beyond toy problems
toward realistic datapaths and subsystem-level complexity.
Our experimental evaluation shows that frontier commer-
cial models such as GPT-4.1 and Claude Sonnet 4, when
paired with lightweight reflection loops, achieve strong per-
formance on V erilogEval (up to 89.74%) and RTLLM (up to
96.08%). These results approach or surpass those of domain-
specific fine-tuned pipelines, highlighting both the strength of
today’s frontier models and the need for richer benchmarks.
Case studies on the radix-2 divider (RTLLM) and FSM
detection task (V erilogEval) reveal recurring LLM failure
modes in algorithmic sequencing, temporal reasoning, and
FSM design. Systematic error patterns—including transition-
priority mistakes, handshake drift, blocking vs. non-blocking
misuse, and state-space oversimplification—illustrate the
challenges of applying natural-language-driven generation to
hardware with tight concurrency and protocol constraints.
From this analysis, several lessons emerge. First, bench-
mark design must evolve: underspecified prompts and brittle
testbenches obscure true capability and hinder progress.
Second, structured specification methods, combined with
systematic debugging aids such as AST- or graph-based
reasoning, offer practical ways to address recurring classes
of design errors. Third, evaluation must expand beyond
functional correctness to include post-RTL metrics such
as power, performance, and area (PPA), bridging the gap
between correctness and design quality. Finally, moving from
module-level generation to SoC-scale automation will require
advances in formal specification languages, hierarchical
composition techniques, and scalable assurance frameworks.
Looking ahead, we outlined a research roadmap toward
natural-language-to-SoC design. Key priorities include
developing controlled natural language schemas for speci-
fications, building open and reproducible end-to-end flows,
incorporating PPA feedback into early design stages, and
advancing modular contracts for assurance. Bridging RTL
generation with SoC assembly, system software interfaces,
and physical implementation will require both open-source
and commercial tool integration, as well as new benchmarks
that capture subsystem- and system-level complexity.
