ABSTRACT
Background.Multi-agent systems based on large language models (LLMs) have become popular as
architectures for complex orchestration tasks, but the concepts that inspire them —self-organization,
nonlinear dynamics, information theory, and nonequilibrium thermodynamics— come from complex
systems and classical swarm intelligence, and their validity in the LLM domain has not been
systematically established.
Objective.To map the available evidence on self-organization mechanisms, nonlinear dynamics,
information theory, and nonequilibrium thermodynamics applied to LLM-based multi-agent systems,
as well as the observables and metrics used to characterize their collective behavior and the conditions
under which these concepts could be transferred to this domain.
Methods.Scoping review following the JBI methodological guidance and the PCC framework.
Searches were conducted in OpenAlex, arXiv, and Scopus. Two independent reviewers —language
model instances with human verification— screened the records; disagreements were resolved through
adjudication with a third reviewer and human review. Data were extracted with a standardized 16-
domain instrument, and each study was classified according to its evidence proximity to the LLM
domain (direct evidence, plausible transfer, or analogical transfer).
Results.A total of 229 studies were included. Only 16 (7.0%) constitute direct evidence on LLM-
based multi-agent systems, and only 3 of them report quantitative metric values. Transferable evidence
from related systems is abundant (163 non-direct studies with at least one evaluable thematic block),
but its availability and consistency vary across constructs. None of the six constructs evaluated
achieves full empirical support; four show partial support (S2–S5) and two remain non-evaluable
(S1, S6). Reproducibility reporting is limited: seed, number of runs, and sampling frequency
are not reported in any study. A full-text audit of the 16 direct studies confirmed a reporting
asymmetry: descriptive fields (model, dataset, task, and baseline) are reported in all 16 studies,
whereas seed and sampling frequency are not reported in any, number of runs in 3, and temperature
in 1. A pre-registered contrast between field families (median difference 0.90; 95% CI [0.80; 1.00];
exact permutation test p= 0.015 ) is consistent with a systematic reporting asymmetry under the
prespecified operationalization.
Conclusions.The literature provides a broad repertoire of mechanisms, observables, and metrics
potentially transferable to the design of LLM-based multi-agent systems, but the direct evidence
currently available is insufficient to establish these mechanisms as empirical properties of the domain.
The constructs evaluated constitute plausible experimental hypotheses whose validation requires a
dedicated experimental program.

1 Introduction
Multi-agent systems based on large language models
(LLMs) have become one of the most widely used ar-
chitectures for tasks requiring coordination, delegation,
and distributed reasoning. Agent orchestration platforms,
multi-model debate systems, and architectures with shared
memory or asynchronous communication have proliferated
in recent years, driven by the capacity of LLMs to interpret
instructions, use tools, and maintain differentiated roles
within a collective [Li et al., 2023, Wang et al., 2023, Xi
et al., 2023].
This growth relies, to a large extent, on a vocabulary
borrowed from complex systems science and classical
swarm intelligence: emergence, phase transitions, criti-
cality, entropy, and self-organization are discussed as if
they were observable properties of LLM-based agent col-
lectives. However, most of these concepts were developed
and validated in other domains: classical multi-agent sys-
tems, biological neural networks, nonequilibrium physical
systems, and abstract mathematical models [Cucker and
Huepe, 2008, Zierenberg et al., 2018, Hintze and Adami,
2015, Shin and Kim, 2004]. The question of whether these
mechanisms effectively operate in systems composed of
language models —and with which metrics they could be
characterized— remains open.
This situation generates a concrete methodological risk.
When a concept is transferred from one domain to another
without verifying the applicability conditions, claims about
system behavior can become metaphors without empirical
support. In the case of LLM-based multi-agent systems,
the problem is especially relevant because design decisions
—communication topology, coordination mechanisms, con-
trol variables— are frequently justified by appealing to
thermodynamic or nonlinear dynamics principles whose
validity in this domain has not been demonstrated.
The literature on the topic is extensive but heterogeneous,
combining empirical work on LLM agents, simulation stud-
ies of classical multi-agent systems, statistical physics re-
sults, and theoretical proposals. This heterogeneity makes
it difficult to distinguish what is known with direct evi-
dence from what is inferred by analogy. Given this situa-
tion, a scoping review is appropriate: the objective is not
to estimate effects, but to map the evidence, identify gaps,
and delimit which claims are justified [Munn et al., 2018,
Peters et al., 2020].
The present study addresses the following research ques-
tion:what evidence exists on self-organization mech-
anisms, nonlinear dynamics, information theory, and
nonequilibrium thermodynamics applied to LLM-based
multi-agent systems, and what observables and metrics
have been used to characterize collective behavior in these
systems and under what conditions could they be transfer-
able to LLM-based multi-agent systems?
Six secondary questions were derived from this question,
one per construct: (S1) self-organization and bioinspired
mechanisms; (S2) nonlinear dynamics; (S3) information,
entropy, and complexity; (S4) criticality and edge of chaos;
(S5) swarm state and health; and (S6) orchestrator control
variables.
The overall objective of the review was to map the avail-
able evidence for each construct, explicitly classifying the
proximity of each study to the LLM domain, so that the
conclusions distinguish in a verifiable way among direct
evidence, transferable evidence, and hypotheses.
2 Methods
2.1 Design
A scoping review was conducted following the updated JBI
methodological guidance [Peters et al., 2020] and the PCC
framework (population, concept, context) [Munn et al.,
2018]. The report follows the structure recommended by
the PRISMA extension for scoping reviews [Tricco et al.,
2018]; the completed checklist is included as supplemen-
tary material (Supplementary S2). The review protocol
(v0.3) was prespecified and frozen before the searches were
executed (2026-08-24); the search strategy was approved
by the author in an explicit review gate. Given the mapping
nature of the question, no effect estimation or risk-of-bias
assessment for exclusion purposes was performed.
2.2 Eligibility criteria
Empirical, simulation, and experimental studies on LLM-
based multi-agent systems were included when they re-
ported dynamics, complexity, or information metrics, or
characterization of collective behavior (stability, coordi-
nation, adaptability, efficiency). Studies of related sys-
tems —classical multi-agent systems, complex systems,
bioinspired swarms, and theoretical methods— were also
included when they presented mechanisms or metrics with
explicit applicability conditions plausible for the LLM do-
main. Editorials, marketing material without data, work
without operational relevance to the question, and content
without verifiable identifiers were excluded.
2.3 Information sources and search strategy
Searches were conducted in OpenAlex, arXiv, and Sco-
pus, without language restriction, covering the period up
to August 2026. The strategy combined terms from three
axes: (a) multi-agent systems and swarms (multi-agent,
swarm,agent orchestration,collective behavior); (b) mech-
anisms and metrics (self-organization,nonlinear dynamics,
Lyapunov,synchronization,entropy,mutual information,
transfer entropy,criticality,edge of chaos,power law,
avalanche,free energy); and (c) language models (large
language model,LLM,GPT,foundation model). The com-
plete strategies per database, the filters applied, and the
coverage checks are available as supplementary material
(Supplementary S1). The complete list of the 229 included
studies, with their evidence domain classification, transfer

2.4 Study selection
The retrieved records were subjected to title and abstract
screening performed independently by two reviewers.
Each reviewer was an independent instance of a language
model (LLM) that applied the prespecified eligibility cri-
teria of the protocol; the human author verified a sample
of the decisions and resolved doubtful cases. Disagree-
ments between reviewers were resolved through an adjudi-
cation procedure with a third reviewer (also an independent
LLM instance) and final human review of the discrepan-
cies. Agreement between reviewers was quantified with
Cohen’s kappa over the screened universe (n= 7 371 ):
raw agreement of 93.1% andκ= 0.83 (substantial agree-
ment). After screening, eligible studies were verified by
identifier (DOI or title) to remove duplicates. Full text
was consulted only when necessary to verify eligibility or
record identity; the information used for characterization
comes exclusively from the abstracts. Screening was ex-
ecuted with custom automation scripts (batch processing
with provenance recording per record); no commercial
screening platforms were used.
2.5 Data extraction
Systematic extraction was performed exclusively on the
retrieved abstracts, with a standardized 16-domain instru-
ment, applied independently by two reviewers (indepen-
dent LLM instances). The full text did not provide addi-
tional information to the extraction fields. The domains
included: metrics mentioned; formula definition; reported
values; thermodynamic label of the metric; applicability
conditions; collective observables; task performance; de-
sign type; agent framework; number of agents; perturba-
tion protocol; reproducibility fields (model version, tem-
perature, seed, prompts, tools, topology, communication,
scheduler, environment, task, number of runs, sampling
frequency, metric implementation, software, hardware);
null or negative evidence; relevance by construct; and
ambiguity notes. Each extracted value was supported by
the corresponding textual fragment of the abstract. Dis-
agreements between reviewers were resolved through an
adjudication procedure with a third reviewer and human
review of the discrepancies. Of the 775 discrepant fields
between reviewers, 35 (4.5%) required explicit human de-
cision due to the absence of a majority among the three
reviewers; the remaining ones were resolved by agreement
or majority with human verification of the discrepancies.
Agreement between reviewers in extraction was quanti-
fied over the double-reading sample (n= 770 decision
pairs): raw agreement of 73.5% and κ= 0.54 (moder-
ate agreement), consistent with the expected variability
in the adjudication of semantic fields. The use of lan-
guage models as eligibility and extraction reviewers has
validation precedents in systematic reviews: recent studies
report sensitivity comparable to humans in title and ab-
stract screening [Matsui et al., 2024, Guo et al., 2023] and
effectiveness in screening and data extraction in multiple
languages [Khraisha et al., 2024]. In this review, the role
of the LLM was limited to classification and extraction
with prespecified criteria; every final inclusion decision
and every discrepancy were reviewed by the human author.
2.6 Evidence proximity classification
Because the question combines direct and transferable evi-
dence, each study was classified on two axes. Henceforth,
the termLLM swarmsis used as equivalent toLLM-based
multi-agent systems: collectives of autonomous agents im-
plemented with large language models that interact with
each other to solve tasks. The first axis, evidence domain,
distinguishes: direct evidence on LLM-based multi-agent
systems; systems using LLMs without multi-agent archi-
tecture; classical computational multi-agent systems; com-
plex systems; bioinspired swarms; and theoretical methods.
The second axis, transfer status, distinguishes: direct evi-
dence (the study directly observes LLM swarms); plausible
transfer (the mechanism or metric presents explicit appli-
cability conditions plausible for LLM swarms, without
demonstration in that domain); and analogical transfer (the
connection to LLM swarms is indirect). This classification
ensures that indirect evidence is never presented as direct
evidence.
2.7 Analysis and synthesis
Data were aggregated descriptively by construct (S1–S6).
For each construct, the following were computed: number
of studies, number and proportion of studies with direct
evidence, distribution of evidence domains and transfer
status, reported metrics and observables, and reproducibil-
ity fields. Two distinct concepts were defined. First, the
transferable evidence of a construct was rated asbroad
when four descriptive criteria are met: (C1) at least 20
studies in the construct; (C2) at least one reproducibility
field reported, as a minimal indicator of methodological
documentation; (C3) the dominant evidence-domain cell
concentrates at least 50% of the studies; and (C4) presence
of at least one direct or analogical study. These criteria
characterize the availability of transferable evidence, not
its quantitative soundness. Second, the strength of the em-
pirical support of the construct was assessed separately,
through a deterministic rule on the direct studies: not sup-
ported if there are no direct studies; non-evaluable if there
are fewer than five; partial support if there are five or more.
Full support was not assigned to any construct. This limit
does not derive from criteria C1–C4, but from an indepen-
dent criterion: the absence of quantitative metric values
in most direct studies (3 of 16) and the null reporting of
seed, number of runs, and sampling frequency prevent sus-
taining verifiable quantitative claims. These thresholds are
prespecified operational rules for the internal classifica-
tion of this review and do not constitute general criteria of
scientific evidence sufficiency.

2.8 Full-text audit of the direct studies
To bound the bias of abstract-based extraction, a targeted
full-text audit was conducted on the 16 direct-evidence
studies. Full texts were acquired from legitimate sources
(arXiv, ACL Anthology, Frontiers, PLOS, and Nature) and
processed with GROBID 0.8.0 to extract the document
structure (paragraphs, tables, figures, and notes). A sys-
tematic deterministic search —without language models—
of 13 reporting fields was applied to this corpus: seed,
number of runs, sampling frequency, model version, tem-
perature, dataset, task, baseline, ablation, statistical test,
variability reporting, code availability, and hardware envi-
ronment. Each field was classified as reported, not found,
ambiguous, or not applicable, with dual status (abstract and
full text) and localized textual evidence (fragment, section,
page). The relationship between what the abstract allows
concluding and what the full document allows conclud-
ing was recorded per field. A second validation pass cor-
rected false positives of the automatic search (substrings,
verbal uses, idiomatic expressions, wrong domains, and
third-party mentions), with all corrections recorded. The
distinction betweennot foundandreal absencewas strictly
maintained: the absence of a field in the processed text
does not imply that the study does not document it in some
appendix or unprocessed material.
2.9 Reporting asymmetry analysis
Based on the full-text audit, a statistical contrast was pre-
registered (protocol frozen before observing the results).
The 13 fields were classified into families according to
their epistemic role: family A (descriptive: model, dataset,
task, and baseline), family B1 (execution: temperature and
sampling frequency), family B2 (replication and variabil-
ity: seed, number of runs, and variability reporting), family
B3 (inference: statistical test), and family C (infrastruc-
ture: code and hardware). The primary contrast compared
the proportion of reported fields of family A against fam-
ilies B1+B2 in the 14 empirical studies (excluding not-
applicable fields), through the per-study paired difference
di =p A,i −p B1+B2,i. The primary inference was an exact
paired permutation test (sign-flip, 214 = 16 384 permuta-
tions) on the median ofdi, with a 95% confidence interval
by percentile bootstrap (10 000 resamples, fixed seed) and
paired Cliff’s delta. Seven sensitivities were prespecified
(alternative treatment of ambiguous fields, inclusion of all
16 studies, sequential exclusion of one study, alternative
family mapping, Wilcoxon test, and per-field Fisher tests)
and a robustness analysis with mixed logistic regression
subject to convergence. The falsification criteria were set
before observing the results. This analysis measuresdocu-
mentary reporting; it does not measure reproducibility or
study quality.
3 Results
3.1 Study corpus
A total of 14 742 records were identified through the
searches in the three databases. After independent screen-
ing by two reviewers, 1 202 unique records were included
by at least one of them, and 774 by both. After identifier
verification and application of the eligibility criteria, 229
studies were subjected to systematic extraction and com-
plete adjudication (775 fields). Figure 1 presents the flow
diagram of the selection process following the PRISMA-
ScR extension. Table 1 summarizes the distribution of the
corpus by evidence domain and transfer status.
The dominant cell of the corpus is computational multi-
agent systems with plausible transfer (106 studies, 46.3%).
Direct evidence on LLM-based multi-agent systems rep-
resents 16 studies (7.0%), of which 13 were assigned to
at least one thematic construct. These 16 studies include
work on inter-agent communication [Li et al., 2023], sur-
veys of autonomous agents [Wang et al., 2023, Xi et al.,
2023], scientific review generation [D’Arcy et al., 2024],
financial sentiment analysis [Xing, 2024], agent platforms
[Gao et al., 2024], robotic collaboration [Chen et al., 2024],
persona consistency [Baltaji et al., 2024], multi-LLM di-

agent debate [Tian et al., 2024], swarm intelligence appli-
cations [Jimenez-Romero et al., 2025], emergent coordina-

[Shaikh et al., 2025], small-world networks [Wang et al.,
2025], semantic early stopping [Shrivastava, 2026], and
limits of collaboration between models [Kim et al., 2026].

3.3 Metrics and observables
Metric mentions were identified in 86 studies in the corpus,
with 136 mentions that do not fit the predefined families
of the instrument (entropies, mutual information, infor-
mation transfer, Lyapunov exponents, fractal dimension,
power laws, avalanches, among others). The most frequent
families among those classified were synchronization met-
rics, consensus metrics, and entropy metrics. Among the
direct-evidence studies, only 3 of 16 report quantitative
metric values: a multi-agent review generation system re-
ports comment rates per document [D’Arcy et al., 2024]; a
model council evaluated on a standardized medical exami-
nation reports accuracy percentages and a confidence in-
terval [Shaikh et al., 2025]; and a semantic early-stopping
mechanism reports information score gains [Shrivastava,
2026]. In related systems, reported values include results
on information transfer in swarms with update rate limits
[Devasia, 2019], homeostatic plasticity in neural networks
[Zierenberg et al., 2018], and causal understanding with
uncertainty in single-LLM systems [Lithgow-Serrano et al.,
2025].
The most frequent collective observables in the corpus
were consensus, synchronization, and neuronal avalanches.
Regarding the applicability conditions of the metrics, 49
studies mention metrics without reporting applicability
conditions, while 37 report conditions without quantitative
values, indicating partial documentation of the transfer
conditions.
3.4 Reproducibility
Table 3 summarizes the reporting of reproducibility fields
in the 156 studies that recorded at least one reproducibility
field. Task (51.3%) and topology (36.5%) are the most
reported fields; communication is reported in 18.6% and
environment in 12.2%. The fields critical for quantita-

is reported in 1.9% of studies and temperature in 0.6%.
These percentages correspond to reporting in the abstracts:
the absence of a field in the abstract does not necessarily
imply its absence in the full text of the study.

not by itself constitute confirmatory evidence. Overall,
these results measure documentary reporting in the au-
dited universe; they do not establish that the studies are
irreproducible nor that their authors do not use seeds.
3.7 Null or negative evidence
Eight studies report null or negative evidence relevant to
the constructs. These include results on metastable phase
transitions in public goods games [Hintze and Adami,
2015], effort profiles in multi-agent resource allocation
[Parunak et al., 2002], emergence of classes in negotiation
models [Axtell et al., 2001], information transfer in the
sensorimotor loop [Thorniley, 2015], phase transitions in
experimental financial markets with agents [Cartlidge and
Cliff, 2013], self-organized criticality in emerging neural
networks [Shin and Kim, 2004], precise phase transitions
in random feature methods [Liao et al., 2020], and col-
lective response of microrobot swarms to external threats
[Chen and Bechinger, 2022]. None of these studies cor-
responds to LLM-based multi-agent systems, which rein-
forces the scarcity of direct evidence, including negative
evidence, in the domain of interest. Additionally, ten stud-
ies explicitly report absence of evidence in some of the
dimensions evaluated, which contributes to delimiting the
scope of the findings.
4 Discussion
4.1 Interpretation of the findings
This scoping review shows that the literature on self-
organization mechanisms, nonlinear dynamics, informa-
tion theory, and nonequilibrium thermodynamics in LLM-
based multi-agent systems is at an early stage. The cor-
pus is dominated by transferable evidence from related
systems: 206 studies present plausible transfer, 7 analogi-
cal transfer, and only 16 constitute direct evidence. This
asymmetry has a direct methodological consequence: in
the analyzed corpus, the evidence used to support claims
related to criticality, entropy, or self-organization comes
predominantly from systems outside the direct domain of
LLM swarms.
The distinction between direct and transferable evidence is
not an academic refinement. It determines which claims
can be sustained with empirical support and which must
be treated as hypotheses. In this corpus, no construct
achieves full support: four show partial support and two
are non-evaluable. Even in the construct with the greatest
availability of direct evidence (S5, 15.9%), the number of
studies is insufficient and the reproducibility reporting is
too weak to sustain quantitative conclusions.
The scarcity of reproducibility reporting deserves specific
attention. The fact that seed, number of runs, and sam-
pling frequency are not reported in any study in the corpus
prevents evaluating the stability of the results, even in the
direct studies. This limitation does not imply that the re-
sults are incorrect, but it does imply that they cannot be
independently verified, which restricts the type of claims
the literature can sustain.
The full-text audit of the 16 direct studies provides addi-
tional precision. The observed pattern is not a generalized
absence of documentation: the descriptive elements of
the experiment (model, dataset, task, and baseline) are re-
ported in 100% of the direct studies, while the execution,
replication, and variability parameters are reported much
less frequently. The pre-registered contrast between these
two families of fields is consistent with a systematic report-
ing asymmetry under the prespecified operationalization
(median difference 0.90; 95% CI [0.80; 1.00];p= 0.015 ),
although the support is not uniform across sensitivities.
This asymmetry has a practical consequence: the litera-
ture allows identifyingwhatwas studied, but frequently
does not allow reconstructinghowthe experiment was ex-
ecuted and replicated. It is important to emphasize that
this finding describes documentary reporting in the audited
universe; it does not establish that the studies are irrepro-

4.2 Comparison with the literature
The findings are consistent with the observation that the
field of LLM-based agents is in a phase of descriptive
expansion, with abundant surveys and architecture propos-
als [Wang et al., 2023, Xi et al., 2023, Jimenez-Romero
et al., 2025] and a growing but still small number of em-
pirical studies with quantitative metrics [D’Arcy et al.,
2024, Shaikh et al., 2025, Shrivastava, 2026]. The clas-
sical literature on multi-agent systems and complex sys-
tems offers a mature repertoire of mechanisms and metrics
—synchronization, information transfer, Lyapunov expo-
nents, avalanches, power laws— with documented appli-
cability conditions [Cucker and Huepe, 2008, Zierenberg
et al., 2018, Hintze and Adami, 2015, Shin and Kim, 2004,
Chen and Bechinger, 2022], but the connection to the LLM
domain remains largely unvalidated.
A relevant finding is the heterogeneity across constructs.
Transferable evidence is broad and consistent in self-
organization, nonlinear dynamics, swarm state, and control
variables, but not in information and complexity nor in
criticality, where the distribution across domains is more
dispersed. This variability suggests that transferability is
not a uniform property of the field, but must be evaluated
construct by construct.
4.3 Limitations
This review has several limitations. First, extraction was
based on abstracts, not full texts, which may underestimate
the reporting of metrics and reproducibility fields in studies
whose abstracts are brief. In particular, the reproducibility
reporting percentages (including the 0% for seed, number
of runs, and sampling frequency) should be read asabstract
reporting percentages, not asstudy reporting percentages:
it is possible that some studies document these fields in the
body of the article or in appendices. To bound this bias, a
full-text audit of the 16 direct-evidence studies was con-
ducted, whose results are presented in sections 3.5 and 3.6.
Even so, the audit processed the main text, tables, and
notes of the documents; appendices and supplementary
materials not included in the processed files could con-
tain additional information, so the distinction betweennot
foundandreal absenceis maintained in the interpretation.
Second, the evidence proximity classification, although
prespecified, involves a judgment about the plausibility of
transfer that may vary across reviewers; the adjudication
procedure mitigates but does not eliminate this variability.
Third, the search covered three databases; it is possible
that relevant literature indexed in other sources was not
captured. Fourth, the absence of reproducibility report-
ing in the corpus limits confidence in the results of the
primary studies, which in turn limits the conclusions of
this review. Fifth, the number of direct studies is small
(16), and conclusions about constructs with fewer than
five direct studies must be interpreted with caution. Sixth,
the reporting asymmetry analysis is based on 14 empirical
studies with a field family saturated at 100% reporting; the
effective contrast measures the distance of the execution,
replication, and variability family from that maximum, and
the support is not uniform across sensitivities (in particu-
lar, treating ambiguous fields as reported does not reach
conventional significance). Seventh, the analysis measures
documentary reporting, not the effective reproducibility or
scientific quality of the studies.
4.4 Implications and future work
The implications of this review are twofold. For research,
it delimits the evidence gap: there is a broad repertoire of
candidate mechanisms and metrics, but their validity in
LLM-based multi-agent systems is not established. This
gap should not be interpreted as an absence of research, but
as an insufficiency of direct evidence for empirical prop-
erties. For system design, it implies that design principles
inspired by thermodynamics or nonlinear dynamics must
be treated as working hypotheses, not as validated rules.
The reporting asymmetry documented in the direct studies
has a concrete methodological implication: any experi-
mental program in this domain should automatically and
completely record the execution, replication, and variabil-
ity fields (seed, number of runs, sampling frequency, tem-
perature, model version, topology, communication rules,
prompts, environment, and metrics), so that its results can
be independently verified. The present review does not
include its own experimental results; the design of a pos-
sible experimental program is at a preliminary stage and
requires validation before any empirical claim.
The natural next step is an experimental program that eval-
uates, under controlled conditions, whether a controller
based on dynamic metrics —selected among the candidate
observables identified in S5— improves performance on
real tasks compared with conventional architectures. Such
a program should include explicit comparators, an opera-
tional definition of the system state based on the mapped
evidence, and complete recording of the reproducibility
fields, so that its results can be independently verified. The
results of this review —in particular the reporting asym-
metry and the scarcity of direct evidence— motivate that
program, but do not replace it: hypotheses about thermody-
namic or nonlinear dynamics mechanisms in LLM-based
multi-agent systems remain open to experimental valida-
tion.
5 Conclusions
The literature provides a broad repertoire of mechanistic
evidence potentially transferable to the design of LLM-
based multi-agent systems, although its availability and
consistency vary across constructs. The direct evidence
currently available is insufficient to establish that these
mechanisms produce the desired properties in the domain:
only 16 of 229 studies directly observe LLM-based multi-

agent systems, only 3 report quantitative metric values,
and reproducibility reporting is limited. The full-text audit
of the 16 direct studies confirmed a reporting asymme-
try between the descriptive elements of the experiment
and the execution, replication, and variability parameters,
consistent with a systematic asymmetry under the prespec-
ified operationalization. There is limited direct evidence
that these constructs have been considered or examined in
LLM swarm contexts, but that evidence is insufficient to
establish them as empirical properties of the domain.
These findings delimit the current state of the evidence:
there is a transferable repertoire of mechanisms and met-
rics, but their validity as empirical properties of LLM-
based multi-agent systems remains unestablished. The
constructs of self-organization, nonlinear dynamics, in-
formation, criticality, swarm state, and control constitute
plausible experimental hypotheses whose validation re-
quires a dedicated experimental program. The contribution
of this review is precisely that verifiable delimitation of
what can be justified, what cannot be justified, and which
hypotheses remain open. The review ends at the frontier
of knowledge: the available evidence does not establish
that thermodynamic or nonlinear dynamics mechanisms
produce the desired collective properties in LLM-based
multi-agent systems, and the validation of those hypotheses
remains for future experimental research.
