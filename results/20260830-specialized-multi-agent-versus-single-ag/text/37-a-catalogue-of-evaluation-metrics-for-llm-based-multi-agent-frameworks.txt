Abstract
LLM-based Multi-Agent (LMA) frameworks have recently gained
traction in software engineering, attracting attention due to their
potential to enhance productivity by automating tasks such as code
generation, testing, and quality assurance. However, evaluation
practices in this area remain fragmented due to the lack of stan-
dardised methodologies. Frameworks often rely on self-defined or
inconsistent metrics, hindering reproducibility and making frame-
works appear optimal within their own settings, which obscures the
true state of the art and can produce artificially inflated performance
results. To address these challenges, this study conducts a compre-
hensive analysis of evaluation metrics used by state-of-the-art LMA
frameworks, revealing inconsistencies and conceptual gaps. We
propose 12 novel metrics, combine them with 26 existing ones,
resulting in a structured catalogue of 38 metrics across four techni-
cal categories: Outcome, Process, Product, and Framework. These
contributions provide a structured foundation for rigorous, repro-
ducible LMA framework evaluation, enabling direct and meaningful
comparisons between frameworks and supporting the systematic
advancement of LMA frameworks for software engineering.

1 INTRODUCTION
LLM-Based Multi-Agent (LMA) frameworks (or systems) have gained
significant attention for their potential in solving complex real-
world tasks through collaboration among intelligent agents [ 4].
Within this context, software engineering is particularly suitable
for LMA frameworks, where typically specialized agents (e.g., pro-
grammer, reviewer, tester) interact via natural language to achieve
shared goals through distributed reasoning and task decomposi-
tion, as in [7]. These systems span multiple software development
phases, from requirements engineering to code generation, testing,
and maintenance. Various multi-agent frameworks have emerged
for this purpose, such as ChatDev [ 16], MetaGPT [5], and Agile-
Coder [12], each implementing different coordination strategies
and evaluation methods. However, as the number of frameworks
grows, so does the fragmentation in how their performance is
evaluated. Current frameworks often rely on heterogeneous or
self-defined metrics, employ inconsistent evaluation pipelines, and
sometimes conflate or rename equivalent measures—making each
system appear optimal within its own scope and hindering meaning-
ful comparison. Moreover, some studies inappropriately compare
frameworks from different categories, such as software-oriented
systems (e.g., ChatDev) versus reasoning-oriented ones (e.g., MAS-
GPT [20]), despite fundamental differences in their objectives and
evaluation contexts. This practice, combined with the frequent use
of identical evaluation concepts under different names, complicates
the establishment of a shared and coherent metric structure.
To address these issues, we present a comprehensive catalogue
of evaluation metrics for LMA frameworks in software engineer-
ing. Derived from a systematic analysis of state-of-the-art systems,
the catalogue organizes 38 metrics—including 12 novel ones—into
four categories: Outcome (software performance quality), Process
(development workflow efficiency), Product (code integrity), and
Framework (usability and human involvement). This categorisa-
tion aims to provide researchers with a structured reference point
for identifying, selecting, and comparing evaluation metrics and
frameworks. Our main contributions are threefold: (i) the catalogue
of metrics, organised into four conceptual dimensions; (ii) a set
of new metrics to improve the comparability, reproducibility, and
methodological rigour of assessing LMA systems; and (iii) the iden-
tification and discussion of recurrent issues in current evaluation
practices, such as the comparison of non-equivalent systems (e.g.,
software-oriented vs. reasoning-oriented frameworks).

The remainder of this paper is organised as follows: Section 2

2 METHODOLOGY
To address these challenges, we developed a four-phase method-
ological workflow, illustrated in Figure 1, designed to systematically
review, identify, and organise evaluation metrics used in LLM-based
multi-agent systems for software engineering.
Figure 1: Overview of the methodological workflow.
Phase 1 — Review of Multi-Agent Systems
A comprehensive review and exploratory analysis were conducted
on existing LLM-based multi-agent frameworks designed for soft-
ware engineering. This stage focused on identifying system func-
tionalities, domains of application, and evaluation practices. Promi-
nent frameworks [16] [5] were examined to characterise the di-
versity of goals and architectures to generate functional software
artifacts. Frameworks that did not employ multi-agent collaboration
mechanisms were excluded. The reviewed frameworks represent
the state-of-the-art in multi-agent LLM-based software engineering
and are further detailed in the catalogue and discussion sections.
Phase 2 — Review of Metrics and Evaluation
Studies
In the second phase, a targeted literature review was performed
to identify prior works on the evaluation of LLM-based and multi-
agent systems. This included empirical studies, technical reports,
and framework documentation. Metrics were examined both in
their formal definitions and implicit occurrences (e.g., within exe-
cution logs, appendices, or supplementary material). During this
stage, we also mapped the main benchmarks recurrently adopted
in the literature—such as HumanEval [12], MBPP [12], and their
variants. Although datasets such as MATH [20] are widely used,
they are not equally representative of all system categories, as they
favour reasoning-orientated agents rather than those optimised
for software generation. Therefore, our analysis focused on soft-
ware engineering tasks and benchmarks that are most aligned with
code-centric activities.
This phase revealed significant methodological inconsistencies
and gaps, especially the absence of standardised evaluation pipelines
and the conflation of conceptual definitions. The field of multimodal
agents is experiencing rapid growth, with research efforts expand-
ing at an unprecedented pace [13]. However, this oversight presents
a critical gap in understanding their effectiveness in real-world de-
ployments, particularly when communicating with humans. These
observations reinforce the need for structured and transparent
methodologies to evaluate LMA systems consistently across con-
texts.
Phase 3 — Framework Selection and Expansion
Building upon the prior phases, a focused selection of represen-
tative systems was carried out. Starting from [4], we employed a
snowballing technique to identify additional frameworks based on
citation relevance, experimental reproducibility, and methodolog-
ical clarity. Only frameworks explicitly capable of generating or
executing code for software engineering tasks were retained, while
those outside this scope, such asMobileSteward [10], were excluded.
Priority was given to well-cited systems considered benchmarks
in the literature, ensuring representativeness and methodological
consistency.
Phase 4 — Metrics Cataloguing and Proposal
The collected metrics were consolidated into a comprehensive cat-
alogue, systematically classified into four conceptual categories:
Outcome, Process, Product and Framework. Beyond organising
existing measures, we proposed additional metrics to address as-
pects frequently discussed in the literature but often informalized
or absent in current evaluations. These include dimensions such
as collaboration efficiency, adaptability to evolving tasks, human-
in-the-loop influence, and overall system usability. These proposed
metrics aim to reduce fragmentation and provide a foundation
for more rigorous, reproducible, and comparable assessments. The
resulting catalogue serves both as a reference resource and as a
methodological foundation for future studies, enabling consistent
evaluation, fair comparison, and informed development of LLM-
based multi-agent systems for software engineering.
This approach aligns with critical analyses, which highlights the
lack of reproducible evaluation and the overestimation of system
performance in isolated or self-defined settings. By identifying and
formalising both existing and previously implicit qualitative dimen-
sions, this study consolidates current knowledge while opening
new avenues for understanding evaluation paradigms and method-
ological trade-offs in LLM-based multi-agent systems for software
engineering, and helping guide the development of future multi-
agent frameworks.

As previously discussed, the catalogue organises evaluation met-
rics into four categories, each capturing a complementary aspect
of LLM-based multi-agent frameworks. Outcome: Metrics assess
the tangible results by the framework (e.g., quality and complete-
ness). Process: Metrics evaluate the efficiency and dynamics of the
software generation workflow (e.g., token usage, execution time).
Product: Metrics analyse the properties and integrity of produced
artifacts (e.g., number of errors, amount of dead code). Frame-
work: Metrics focus on the system itself (e.g., support for human
involvement, ease of use).
For clarity and conciseness, several framework names appearing
in the catalogue and in Table 2 have been abbreviated. Specifically,
we use SOA to refer to Self-Organized Agents [6], IER for Iterative
Experience Refinement of Software-Developing Agents [15], ToP for
Think-on-Process [9], MLC to denote Multi-LLM Cooperation [8],
and Croto for Cross-Team Orchestration [1]. These abbreviations
are used consistently throughout this paper to improve readability
without loss of specificity.

In some studies (e.g., ChatDev, MetaGPT), metrics are clearly pre-
sented and systematically measured, was in others, they are implicit,
embedded within logs, supplementary materials, or even inferred
through result tables. In particular, the SOA framework demon-
strates this challenge: although it proposes valuable dimensions, its
metrics are not explicitly formalised, requiring careful interpreta-
tion of the paper to reconstruct the underlying measures.
Another relevant aspect observed during the analysis concerns
the fragmentation of evaluation practices across frameworks. Al-
though our catalogue focuses only on metrics from the selected
frameworks, this phenomenon was also observed among the dis-
carded ones, indicating a broader lack of methodological consis-
tency in the field. Due to the absence of standardised evaluation
protocols, we identified metrics sharing the same name but as-
sessing distinct aspects, as well as metrics with different names
referring to nearly identical constructs.
A particularly notable case involves frameworks that measure the
same phenomenon in divergent ways—such as through percentages,
discrete counts, or boolean indicators. For example, Error-Free
Execution in MegaAgent and Executability1 in ChatDev, Croto, and
IER represent conceptually equivalent aspects of code operability
but are quantified differently: as a boolean in MegaAgent and as
a percentage in the others. While a unified naming convention
could improve comparability, defining normative standards for
metric nomenclature or computation lies beyond the scope of this
work. With the exception of the new metrics explicitly proposed
by us, this catalog intentionally preserves the original definitions
and formulations adopted by each framework, aiming to faithfully
reflect current evaluation practices rather than impose prescriptive
conventions.
In addition to cataloguing existing metrics, this study introduces
twelve new measures to capture evaluation dimensions that are
widely relevant yet often implicit in current LMA frameworks.Out-
come metrics, such as K-Executions to Success, user-perceived Ease
of Use and Sounds Good aim to capture whether the results gen-
erated by the framework meet minimal structural and usability
expectations and how reliably functional outcomes are achieved.
Process metrics, namely Rework Ratio, Iteration Count, Time per
Iteration, Tokens per Agent, Number of Human Clarifications and
Human Clarification Score focus on development dynamics beyond
final success, with the first four characterising resource consump-
tion and convergence speed and the last two formalizing how of-
ten and how effectively human-in-the-loop interaction occurs (for
frameworks that allow it). Product metrics, such as Messages/LOC
and Amount of Dead Code , evaluate the structural quality of the
generated software artifacts by quantifying unused code and the
amount of inter-agent communication required to produce the fi-
nal output. By assessing the code itself, these metrics can show
problems that are hidden by Outcome metrics, such as programs
that run (good Outcome), but contain unused function or reduntant
modules. Finally, the Framework dimension is extended by the
metric Framework Ease of Use , which assesses how easily users
can interact with and adopt a given LMA framework. More de-
tailed explanations with examples of use of each of the metrics
have been made available 1. Furthermore, to ensure long-term rele-
vance in this rapidly expanding field, the catalogue is maintained
as an open-source resource in a GitHub repository. This allows
for continuous versioning and community contributions, ensuring
the artifact remains ’living’ and updated as new frameworks and
metrics emerge.
It is worth noting that traditional software engineering met-
rics were not identified among the assessment practices of the
analysed frameworks. However, we consider these conventional
measures—such as cyclomatic and cognitive complexity, code dupli-
cation, code smells, and test coverage—to be highly complementary
and relevant for a comprehensive evaluation of the generated sys-
tems. These metrics were not included in this catalogue because
they are not novel contributions and were not explicitly assessed
by the reviewed frameworks. Nevertheless, we highlight their im-
portance and encourage their systematic inclusion, particularly
when evaluating the Product dimension of LLM-based multi-agent
frameworks. Overall, these observations underscore the need for
systematic evaluation practices and validate the structure, relevance
and content of the presented catalogue.
4 CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK
Current LLM-based multi-agent frameworks for software engineer-
ing remain fragmented in their evaluation methodologies, leading
to imprecise and often biased assessments. Frameworks are fre-
quently compared using heterogeneous datasets or selectively de-
fined metrics that highlight their own strengths while overlooking

initial step toward standardisation by consolidating and catalogu-

of performance. This catalogue offers a structured foundation for
future evaluation and comparative studies.
Looking ahead, we plan to systematically validate each identi-
fied metric to assess its relevance and applicability across contexts.
Building on this catalogue, we aim to perform controlled cross-
framework comparisons and explore less-studied frameworks, re-
fining our proposed metrics toward a standardised, reproducible,

multi-agent systems in software development.
