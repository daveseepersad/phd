Abstract— This paper presents a structured overview of 
recent research on the application of Large Language Models 
(LLMs) and multi -agent systems in software requirements 
analysis and introduces a conceptual, role -based multi-agent 
architecture for addressing identified limitations. The proposed 
approach emphasizes hierarchical supervision, collaboration 
between Large and Small Language Models, and structured 
cross-checking based on report comparison and uncertainty -
aware arbitration to improve robustness and reliability. The 
contribution of this work is theoretical and exploratory, 
providing a foundation for future research on multi-agent LLM 
systems aimed at improving the software requirements analysis 
process. 
Key words: software requirements analysis , LLM, SLM, Multi-
agent 
I. INTRODUCTION 
Software requirements analysis represents one of the most 
critical and error -prone phases of the software development 
lifecycle. Decisions made at this stage directly influence 
system architecture, implementation effort, cost, and long -
term maintainability , while errors introduced early often 
propagate to later development phases with significant 
negative consequences [ 1]. According to the Software 
Engineering Body of Knowledge (SWEBOK) [2], 
requirements analysis encompasses activities such as basic 
requirements analysis, addressing quality -of-service 
constraints, formal analysis, and conflict resolution, all of 
which require a high degree of precision, consistency, and 
domain understanding. In practice, however, requirements are 
predominantly expressed in natural language, which makes 
the analysis process inherently ambiguous, time-consuming, 
and susceptible to inconsistencies and errors. 
Large Language Models (LLMs) have emerged as a 
promising technological enabler for supporting and partially 
automating software engineering activities, in cluding 
requirements analysis [3]. Due to their ability to process and 
interpret natural language, LLM s have demonstrated 
significant potential in extracting, summarizing, and 
structuring information from textual requirements. Recent 
 
 
surveys and literature reviews indicate that LLM -based 
systems are increasingly applied across various software 
engineering tasks, highlighting their capability to understand, 
reason about, and generate software -related artifacts [4][5]. 
Furthermore, the integration of LLMs into autonomous agents 
and multi -agent systems (MAS) has been identified as a 
significant shift in the research landscape, enabling 
collaborative reasoning, coordination, and task 
decomposition for complex problem solv ing [6]. 
LLM-based agents have found applications in different 
stages of complex problem solving. According to Li et al. [4], 
LLM-based MAS a re considered a promising pathway 
towards realizing general artificial intelligence that is 
equivalent to or surpasses human -level intelligence. Such 
systems can perceive, reason, and act collaboratively, often 
outperforming single-agent approaches in complex domains 
[4][7]. Existing research identifies five key components of 
LLM-based MAS architecture: profile, perception, self -
action, mutual interaction, and evolution , each describing 
how agents react and adapt within a given environment [4]. 
However, this interaction -driven behavior may lead to 
suboptimal decisions . 
Despite their potential, MAS remains limited in solving 
certain classes of problems. To address scalability and 
coordination challenges, concepts such as MegaAgenta, a 
large-scale autonomous LLM -based system capable of 
dynamically creating agents based on task complexity , have 
been proposed [8]. Further advances include decentralized 
coordination paradigms such as AgentNet, which introduce 
dynamic agent graph topologies and retrieval-based memory 
mechanisms [9]. Although recent progress has significantly 
enhanced the ability of multiple LLM -based agents to 
perceive, learn, reason, and act collaboratively [ 7], several 
challenges remain unresolved, including hallucination, 
limited learning from immediate feedback, and high 
computational resource requirements caused by model 
scaling [10]. These issues are particularly problematic in the 
context of software requirements analysis, where errors and 
ambiguities may propagate into later development phases 
with significant negative consequences. 
To mitigate some of these limitations, recent research has 
explored the role of smaller, task -specialized language 
models, commonly referred to as Small Language Models 
(SLMs). Compared to LLMs, SLMs are often more efficient, 
context-focused, and better su ited for narrowly defined

Empirical studies in software requirements engineering 
confirm that, while language models can significantly 
improve efficiency, they do not fully eliminate issues related 
to clarity, consistency, and reliability. For example, existing 
work on automated us er story generation and prioritization 
shows that LLM-based approaches can produce functionally 
relevant requirements with high semantic similarity, yet still 
suffer from variability across runs and inconsistencies in 
expression [ 13]. Similarly, frameworks such as MetaGPT 
attempt to address cascading hallucinations and logical 
inconsistencies by introducing standardized operating 
procedures and structured collaboration among agents [ 14]. 
Despite these advances, a comprehensive understanding of 
the effects, limits and reliability of LLM-based approaches in 
software engineering remains at an early stage [5]. 
These observations motivate a hybrid perspective in which 
LLMs and SLMs are combined within a coordinated multi -
agent system, leveraging their complementary strengths. In 
such a system, LLMs operate at a higher level of abstraction, 
providing global unders tanding, supervision, and 
orchestration, while SLMs act as specialized agents 
responsible for well-defined subtasks. This naturally leads to 
a role -based multi -agent architecture, in which 
responsibilities are distributed according to the level of 
abstract ion and analytical scope. However, introducing 
multiple agents also raises challenges related to coordination, 
validation, and decision reliability. Disagreements between 
agents, inconsistencies in generated artifacts, and uncertainty 
in analytical outcomes must be explicitly addre ssed. One 
possible approach is to introduce systematic cross -checking 
mechanisms, where the outputs of multiple specialized agents 
are compared and evaluated under the supervision of a higher-
level agent. Such mechanisms can be interpreted as a form of 
role-based collaboration, where agents operating at different 
levels of abstraction mutually reinforce or challenge each 
other’s conclusions to reduce ambiguity and error 
propagation. 
The goal of this paper is to provide a structured overview 
of existing research on the application of large language 
models and multi -agent systems in software requirements 
analysis, and to propose a conceptual, role-based architecture 
as a research vision for improving the process of software 
requirements analysis. The contribution of this work is 
predominantly theoretical and exploratory. Rather than 
presenting a fully implemented framework or empirical 
evaluation, the paper identifies key limitations of current 
approaches and outlines a conceptual foundation for mu lti-
agent, LLM -enabled systems supporting more reliable 
requirements analysis. Specifically, it proposes a role -based 
agentic architecture, defines a redundancy -based double-
checking mechanism supervised by an LLM, introduces a 
task-level decomposition of requirements analysis, and 
provides a probabilistic reliability formulation explaining 
how redundancy may reduce cumulative error. 
II. MATERIALS AND METHODS 
In this research the model of organizing the relationship 
between the LLM and SLMs is proposed to obtain acceptable 
results in the process of software requirement analysis. 
According to the SWEBOK guide established by IEEE 
Computer Society [2], it is possible to state that the software 
requirements analysis is almost the most important part in the 
process of software design. It encompasses four steps: Basic 
Requirements Analysis, Economics of Quality -of-Service 
Constraints, Formal Analysis, and Addressing Conflict 
Requirements. Within this decomposition, each step defines a 
distinct analytical role in the system, while concret e tasks 
represent instances of applying that role to particular 
requirement artifacts serving as input. The agents generate 
role-specific structured outputs, whose form and granularity 
depend on the analytical responsibility of the assigned role. 
To reduce individual model errors, tasks are executed in 
parallel by paired SLM agents, and their outputs are compared 
using a predefined similarity thresholds (ϵ). In cases of 
disagreement, a supervisory LLM performs arbitration 
through contextual reasoning and refines or selects the final 
result. Although this represents only the “top of the iceberg” 
in requirements engineering, the emphasis here is not on 
detaili ng all possible roles, but on proposing a redundancy -
based validation approach to reduce errors and ambiguity. In 
mathematical interpretation it could be written as follows:

In this model, the LLM operates as a supervisory agent 
responsible for evaluating the outputs generated by SLM 
agents. Upon receiving a report from an SLM, the LLM 
assesses its logical consistency, identifies potential 
ambiguities, and evaluates the risk o f error propagation in 
subsequent analytical steps. Double checking therefore 
consists of two complementary levels of validation: report -
level comparison based on the distance threshold defined in 
formulas (1) and (2), and supervisory arbitration based on the 
uncertainty comparison defined in formula (3). The 
supervisory decision is guided by the uncertainty measures 
associated with the LLM and SLM outputs. Since smaller 
values of σ correspond to higher reliability, the model favors 
the result associated with lower uncertainty. In this way, 
double checking is not limited to simple report comparison 
but incorporates hierarchical supervision aimed at ensuring 
global consistency and analytical robustness. The overall 
structure of the proposed model is illustrated in Fig. 1.

IV. DISCUSSION 
This research explored the potential of LLM-based MAS to 
improve efficiency and accuracy in software requirements 
analysis. Grounded in the SWEBOK view of requirements 
engineering, the study focused on conceptual challenges that 
arise when requirements are analyzed manually, including 
ambiguity, inconsistency, and error propagation. Motivated 
by recent advances in LLM and SLM, the work investigated 
how a coordinated, role-based agent model could support key 
analytical activities and reduce such issues. 
The proposed approach introduces task -specialized SLM 
agents supervised by a higher -level LLM, combined with a 
double-checking mechanism applied across analysis steps. A 
simplified probabilistic abstraction was used to illustrate how 
agreement, disagreement, and supervisory arbitration affect 
overall analytical reliability, rather than to provide empirical 
performance estimates. This abstraction highlights the 
importance of maintaining sufficiently high step -level 
reliability to avoid cumulative degradation of results . 
The presented work represents an early -stage, exploratory 
contribution. As such, it does not claim empirical validation 
and is intended to inform future research directions. Further 
studies should investigate different classes of software 
requirements, alt ernative combinations of LLM and SLM 
agents, and systematic methods for measuring uncertainty, 
hallucination, and error propagation. Additional research is 
also required to assess potential limitations of the proposed 
model and to refine its architectural assumptions. 
V. CONCLUSION 
This paper reviewed recent research on the application of 
Large Language Models and multi-agent systems in software 
requirements analysis, highlighting both their potential and 
remaining limitations. While LLM-based approaches provide 
strong support for la nguage-intensive requirements 
engineering activities, existing studies reveal persistent 
challenges related to reliability, consistency, hallucination, 
and error propagation. 
To address these issues, the paper outlined a conceptual, 
role-based multi -agent vision in which LLM and SLM 
collaborate through hierarchical supervision and structured 
cross-checking with uncertainty -aware arbitration. Rather 
than proposing a fully implemented framework, the 
contribution of this work lies in synthesizing existing research 
and formalizing architectural principles and reliability 
considerations that can guide future development of multi -
agent LLM systems for improving the software requirements 
analysis process .
