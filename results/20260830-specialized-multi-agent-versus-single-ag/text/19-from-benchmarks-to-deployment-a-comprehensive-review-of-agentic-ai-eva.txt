Abstract
This review systematically examines evaluation methodologies for agentic AI systems, 
agentic AI systems capable of multi-step planning, tool usage, and environmental inter -
action across diverse domains. Current evaluation practices exhibit a critical disconnect 
between benchmark performance and deployment viability, where agents achieving high 
scores on standardized benchmarks frequently fail in real world applications due to fun -
damental inadequacies in assessment methodologies that prioritize task completion over 
deployment critical dimensions such as cost efficiency, safety compliance, maintainability, 
and workflow integration. We critically analyze 15 major agent benchmarks including 
AgentBench, WebArena, SWE-bench, PaperBench, MLGym, BrowserGym, HumanEval, 
MBPP, GAIA, ToolBench, Terminal-Bench, Mind2Web, ALFWorld, BabyAI, and Hot -
PotQA, examining their methodologies, metrics, datasets, and application domains with 
software development serving as our primary case study. Our analysis reveals that evalua -
tion methodology not model capability constitutes the primary bottleneck limiting reliable 
agent deployment. We demonstrate how test passing metrics systematically ignore code 
quality, security vulnerabilities, and integration complexity while binary success metrics 
obscure planning coherence, resource efficiency, and safety violations (0/15 benchmarks 
integrate security or safety into scoring. This review provides a cross-domain taxonomy 
exposing evaluation inadequacies, trajectory level evaluation frameworks addressing cost 
reproducibility validity trade-offs, systematic identification of metric insufficiencies in -
cluding absence of safety-aware and cost aware scoring, and synthesis of emerging evalu -
ation paradigms with adoption barriers. Quantitatively, 0/15 benchmarks integrate safety 
or security into scoring, 0/15 include cost-efficiency metrics in their primary evaluation 
protocol, and 13/15 rely exclusively on binary success measures confirming that evalua -
tion methodology, not model capability, is the primary bottleneck to reliable deployment. 
Progress toward trustworthy agentic AI fundamentally depends on evolving evaluation 
infrastructure beyond binary metrics toward comprehensive, multidimensional assessment 
frameworks that capture these deployment-essential dimensions.

1 Introduction
Artificial intelligence has been radically changed in the last decade to abandon task-specific 
models and embrace agentic systems, often with autonomous decision-making and multi-
step planning and interaction with complex environments. The shift has occurred mainly 
due to the development of large language models (LLMs) which have proven to be capable 
of unprecedented natural language understanding, logical thinking and code creation in a 
way that was previously unattainable Wang et al. (2024a). Present-day agentic AI systems 
operate today in a wide range of high-stakes tasks such as autonomous software engineer -
ing, automation of web-based tasks, scientific discovery, and enterprise decision support, 
and their capability to break down high-level goals, use external tools, and adapt to dynamic 
situations constitutes a shift in paradigm to the traditional AI system architecture (Wang 
et al. 2024b; Hou et al. 2024). Unlike classical AI models assigned to isolated tasks with 
fixed inputs and outputs, agentic AI systems act in a goal-directed manner, maintain mem -
ory, perceive their surroundings, and improve continuously through feedback. This review 
primarily focuses on LLM-based agentic systems agents that leverage large language mod-
els for planning, reasoning, and tool invocation as these represent the dominant paradigm 
in current benchmarks (AgentBench, WebArena, SWE-bench). While we acknowledge RL-
based autonomous agents and hybrid neuro-symbolic systems, our evaluation analysis spe-
cifically targets LLM-based agents due to their prevalence in the 15 examined benchmarks 
and their immediate deployment relevance in software development, web automation, and 
research assistance domains.
The systems combine the planning algorithms, tool-use capabilities and multi-step rea -
soning to achieve the goals that might involve hundreds or thousands of sequential steps 
over longer periods of time horizons (Wang et al. 2024a). The application of LLM-based 
agents in practice revealed that there is a fundamental need to prioritize the reliability, 
safety, and intentionality of human goals, especially in the case of the autonomy of these 
systems in areas where mistakes may have serious technical, financial, or ethical implica -
tions on human lives and welfare at large (Terragni et al. 2025). This functional complexity 
implies the need to evaluate at a level beyond mere accuracy measures, to include extensive 
measures of the consistency of planning, ability to recover errors, resource use efficiency 
and behavioral robustness in the face of uncertainty.
The adoption and spread of agentic AI systems has given rise to an ever growing ecosys-
tem of benchmarks and evaluation regimes that seek to quantify the performance of agents 
in a variety of dimensions. Over fifty different agent benchmarks have been proposed by 
the research community, since 2021, and they include web navigation, software develop -
ment, scientific reasoning, embodied robotics, general-purpose task completion, etc Hou 
et al. (2024). Nevertheless, this has been growing in a highly disjointed fashion, which has 
led to high levels of fragmentation, when it comes to evaluation approaches, definitions of 
metrics, environmental presuppositions, and ability taxonomies. Current benchmarks are 
widely heterogeneous in terms of scope, between limited single-turn interaction and open-
ended multi-session interaction, but different benchmarks have not established standardized

There are still critical gaps in the existing assessment environment that inhibit scientific 
knowledge and real-world application of agentic systems. To begin with, there exist no stan-

activities. Second, a majority of benchmarks focus on the task completion rates and over -

with human preferences, safety assurances, and reproducibility in multiple implementations

assess trade-offs between cost-efficiency, because the agents with greater success rates tend 
to be orders of magnitude more computational or API calls than other agents. Fourth, rapid 
advances in foundation models and prompting have made benchmark validity a moving 
target. Apparent performance gains may reflect dataset contamination or overfitting rather 
than genuine capability improvementsEvtikhiev et al. (2023). In addition, automated test 
generation, and evaluation techniques do not have the ability to establish whether the gener-
ated tests adequately test the intended functionality or are just superficially measured tests 
(Dakhel et al. 2024; Arabiat et al. 2023). All of these constraints weaken the validity of 
agent assessment as a science, as well as prevent principled system construction
Although recent surveys have studied the evaluation of LLM Chang et al. (2024) and 
software engineering applications, e.g., with a survey of use in software engineering, we 
find no studies that systematically examined the failure to predict deployment success across 
agent domains with benchmark performance (Wang et al. 2024b; Hou et al. 2024; Martínez-
Fernández et al. 2022). The previous work lists the means of evaluation or explains the 
possibilities but does not address the critical analysis of methodological sufficiency to mea-
sure the autonomous decision-making. There are three fundamental disconnects, namely, 
(1) between measures of task success and process quality (coherence of planning tools) (2) 
between benchmarking performance and cost-efficiency of deployment; and (3) between an 
isolated task performance and end-to-end workflow integration. This review is unique in its 
positioning of evaluation methodology, but not model capability, as the principal constraint 
to reliable agent deployment.
It is a systematic review of 15 large benchmarks ( AgentBench, WebArena, SWE-bench, 
PaperBench, MLGym, BrowserGym, HumanEval, MBPP, GAIA, ToolBench, Terminal-
Bench, Mind2Web, ALFWorld, BabyAI, HotPotQA). We do not give the description of the 
methods of evaluation; in contrast to the current surveys which present the description of the 
evaluation methods, we critically examine one of the reasons why the current practices do 
not capture the dimensions which are critical in deployment. Table 1 in locates this review 
in comparison with current surveys.

unify, critique and integrate the patchwork quilt of agent evaluation methodologies. With 
organizations investing a lot in the creation and implementation of agentic systems, lack of 
stringent and standardized evaluation practices is a threat to technological advancement as 
well as stakeholder confidence. In addition, the prevailing broadcasting of incomparable

sheds light on the general issue of judging agents in all fields.

2.1 Historical background
The development of artificial intelligence evaluation has been followed by four paradig -
matic shifts, each of which mirrors the fundamental changes in the nature of AI systems 
and their connection to the computational environments. Rule-based and symbolic methods 
predominated the early AI evaluation period (before 2010) with benchmarks concentrating 
on discrete reasoning problems with deterministic results Du and Ding ( 2021). The early 
AI evaluation era (before 2010) was where benchmarks were in the such form of discrete 
reasoning problems with deterministic answers. The metrics of evaluation were focused on 
accuracy and precision in closed world assumptions where system logic did not depend on 
the dynamics of the environment. This paradigm fitted quite well with experts systems and 
symbolic planners, in which success could be decided by logical correctness.
In 2010-2017, the statistical and deep learning revolution offered large-scale supervised 
benchmarks that were focused on optimizing single-task performance. ImageNet paradigm 
created evaluation standards based on fixed datasets, held-out test sets, and scalar evaluation 
metrics Lebovitz et al. (2021). This method was very effective in assimilating perceptual 
activities and showed underlying constraints in evaluating higher-order cognitive processes, 
including reasoning and adaptive decision-making Wang et al. (2024b). The benchmarks 
of this period viewed AI systems as passive functions approximators and quantified what 
models could predict as opposed to what they could do by sequential action. As systems 
were unable to cope with distribution shift and task generalization, the difference between 
the performance in laboratories and in the real-world grew larger and larger Nashaat and 
Miller (2024).
With the advent of foundation models and large language models (2018-2021), the con-
ventional evaluation schemes were broken because they introduced systems that can learn in 
few shots, instruct, and use crude tools in a rudimentary fashion. The pretraining-finetuning 
paradigm allowed models to be able to learn generic skills that can be used in a variety of 
tasks Wang et al. (2024a). Pretraining-finetuning paradigm enabled models to have general 
skills which can be used in a wide range of tasks Dong et al. (2024). Initial studies found 
that Chain-of-thought reasoning and other forms of emergent behavior that were not directly 
optimised during training were observed in the LLMs, and were not well captured by exist-
ing metrics Munikoti et al. (2023). The problem with the use of the static evaluation sets 
emerged as the evidence was accrued of the contamination of benchmarks by the use of 
web-scale pretraining data Evtikhiev et al. (2023).
The subsequent emergence of agentic AI (2021-present) has required the very conceptu-
alization of the evaluation approach to be revisited because AI systems have ceased to serve 
as passive predictors and have become autonomous decision-making agents, functioning 
in the complex dynamic environments Wang et al. (2024a). Modern agents have long-term 
strategic planning, long-lasting memory between agents, can use external tools and APIs, 
and respond to environmental feedback by adjusting their behavior Nair et al. (2023). This 
change revealed the deficiency of accuracy-based measures because agent performance 
lies in the completion of tasks cumulatively, efficiency of resources, and correspondence to 
human wills in multi-step paths and trajectories, among others, as the human wills are inad-
equately pronounced in accuracy-based metrics Dakhel et al. (2024). Agents were tested in

2.2 Technical background
There are five key capabilities that single out agentic AI systems as distinct and separate 
as compared to traditional machine learning models. Long-horizon reasoning and planning 
help agents to break down complex goals into executable sub-goals and have consistent 
strategies through hundreds of steps of interaction in a coherent way Mohan et al. (2024). 
The use of tools and APIs enables agents to expand their functionality by calling on external 
computational services, databases, and specialty models, which requires evaluation of tool 
selection fidelity and a high level of integration of tool outputs to be productive Munikoti 
et al. (2023).
This review concentrates on LLM-based agentic AI systems where language models 
serve as the core reasoning engine for multi-step decision-making, tool selection, and envi-
ronmental interaction. The evaluated benchmarks (Sect. 4) predominantly assess this archi-
tecture. While reinforcement learning-based agents (e.g., game-playing AI, robotic control) 
and hybrid neuro-symbolic systems exist, they employ fundamentally different evaluation 
paradigms reward optimization and logical reasoning verification, respectively which fall 
outside our systematic analysis. Our findings regarding metric inadequacy, cost blindness,

2.3 Relationship between historical and technical evolution
The historical development of the AI ability and the assessment modes shows certain pat -
terns according to which evaluation systems are always behind the technological one. Fixed 
benchmarks matched well with the initial AI systems as stateless functions. Terragni et al. 
(2025). With the acquisition of adaptive behavior by the systems based on machine learning, 
the evaluation changed towards probabilistic evaluation, although the assumption of passive 
model operation persisted. This paradigm is disrupted by the shift to agentic AI system and 
needs evaluation at the environment level as well of courseYan et al. (2025).
Greater realism in evaluation brings about harsh trade-offs. Simulated task settings 
improve ecological validity at great computer cost. The process of benchmark saturation 
accelerates when competent agents learn to do prevailing tasks, thus requiring the constant 
creation of benchmarks Lebovitz et al. (2021). Agents are prone to increased safety risks 
when they are put in real-life circumstances with real-life implications. Cross-benchmark

2.4 Challenges and limitations in historical context
Modern agent analysis continues to struggle with the historical issues of agent analysis. 
The problem of benchmark overfitting and dataset contamination has been exacerbated by 
foundation models being trained on web-scale corpora which may contain benchmark data 
Evtikhiev et al. (2023). The lack of a shared evaluation taxonomies renders formal catego -
rization of agent capabilities and evaluation procedures. Measurement The measurement of 
generalization and robustness is still unsatisfactory, since benchmarks generally measure 
how well prediction is on particular task distributions without describing how such predic -
tion will degrade with a change in the distribution of the testNashaat and Miller (2024).
The issue of safety and alignment is not given appropriate attention in the agent bench -
marks which only ensure that the tasks are done and they do not measure harmful actions 
avoidance appropriately. Realistic agent evaluation has high computational and financial 
costs that cause barriers to accessibilityChen et al. (2024). Environment nondeterminism 
and lack of proper documentation of evaluation protocols are some of the challenges that 
face reproducibility. Past tendencies show that the evaluation systems are always lagging 
behind the development of capabilities. This shortcoming encourages swift creation of 
ongoing, adaptive, and integrated agent assessment systems that can quantify new abilities 
and identify safety dangersDakhel et al. (2024).
3 Systematic review methodology and evaluation techniques
Assessment of agentic AI systems poses methodological challenges never seen before that 
place it at the core of what is different about the assessment of traditional machine learning. 
The agentic AI system have sequential decision-making, tool invocation, environmental 
interaction, and adaptive planning over long periods of time as compared to the static mod-
els which give rise to single outputs. It is a systematic analysis of the methods of evaluation 
that explicitly target agentic systems and presents the methodology as a logical taxonomy 
that includes the basic benchmarking techniques, interactive assessment procedures, and 
domain-specific techniques of evaluation. We focus on software development agents (as a 
main case study), illustrating how the evaluation issues arise in the context of application, 
and how the issue of architectural decisions has an impact on the strategy of assessment.
3.1 Benchmark selection and evaluation protocol
To ensure reproducibility and transparency, we establish explicit criteria for benchmark 
selection and our evaluation framework. Our 15 benchmarks were selected based on four 
inclusion criteria. First, temporal relevance was ensured by focusing on publications between 
2021–2025 to capture the LLM-based agent era, consistent with the PRISMA search win -
dow reported in Section 3.2. Second, all selected benchmarks require multi-step interac -
tion involving sequential decision-making beyond single-turn responses. Third, community 
adoption was verified through citation counts ( ≥ 50 citations) or publication in major ML

venues including NeurIPS, ICLR, ICML, ACL, and EMNLP. Fourth, we ensured domain 
coverage by selecting benchmarks representing diverse application areas such as software 
development, web navigation, embodied AI, scientific reasoning, and general question 
answering.
We explicitly excluded single-turn QA benchmarks without environmental interaction 
(e.g., MMLU, HellaSwag), domain-specific benchmarks without generalizable evalua -
tion patterns (e.g., medical diagnosis-only systems), and closed-source proprietary bench -
marks without public documentation. Each benchmark was systematically analyzed across 
six evaluation dimensions. Task structure analysis examined horizon length (categorized 
as short for less than 5 steps, medium for 5-20 steps, and long for more than 20 steps), 
interaction type (static versus interactive), and environment determinism. Metrics coverage 
analysis identified what each benchmark measures (such as success rate, efficiency, and 
cost) and critically, what dimensions are systematically omitted (including safety, robust -
ness, and process quality). Dataset properties were assessed for size, contamination risk, 
and trajectory annotation depth. Evaluation validity was examined through correlation with 
deployment outcomes where empirical data exists. Reproducibility was assessed based on 
environment stability, documentation quality, and public availability. Finally, deployment 
gaps were identified by documenting mismatches between benchmark performance and 
real-world requirements.
Rather than applying numerical weighting, which would impose artificial commensu -
rability across heterogeneous benchmarks, we employ comparative inadequacy analysis. 
For each benchmark, we identify which deployment-critical dimensions are systemati -
cally absent from evaluation protocols. This qualitative-comparative approach is appropri -
ate given that benchmarks measure fundamentally different capabilities (code generation 
versus web navigation versus embodied control) and cannot be meaningfully ranked on a 
single numerical scale. Our contribution lies in revealing structural patterns of evaluation 
inadequacy across domains rather than scoring individual benchmarks. Tables 2, 3, and 5 
document our systematic application of these evaluation dimensions to each benchmark, 
enabling independent verification of our analysis.
To assess rubric reliability, two authors independently applied the gap-scoring proto -
col (10) to five benchmarks (AgentBench, SWE-bench, WebArena, HumanEval, and 
ALFWorld) across all five scoring dimensions (25 ratings per annotator, 50 total). Exact 
agreement was achieved on 21 of 25 dimension-benchmark pairs (84%), with the remain -
ing 4 cases differing by one point on the 0–5 scale. Disagreements were resolved through 
structured discussion before scores were finalized for all 15 benchmarks.

The search period covered January 2021 to December 2025, corresponding to the emer -
gence and rapid development of LLM-based agentic systems as documented in our histori-
cal background. We employed three complementary search string strategies. The primary 
search string combined (“agentic AI” OR “AI agent” OR “autonomous agent”) with 
(“evaluation” OR “benchmark” OR “assessment”). Our secondary search string targeted 
(“LLM agent” OR “language agent”) paired with (“testing” OR “validation” OR “metrics”). 
Domain-specific searches used (“software agent” OR “web agent” OR “code generation”) 
combined with (“evaluation framework” OR “performance metric”). These strings were 
adapted to each database’s syntax requirements while maintaining semantic equivalence.
The initial database search identified 1,247 papers. After removing duplicates and con -
ducting title and abstract screening, 384 papers remained for detailed review. We applied 
explicit inclusion criteria requiring that papers must propose or evaluate agent benchmarks, 
demonstrate multi-step autonomous behavior assessment, be peer-reviewed or high-impact 
preprints (arXiv papers with more than 50 citations), and provide sufficient evaluation 
methodology details. Exclusion criteria eliminated papers focusing solely on single-task 
static ML models, purely theoretical work without empirical evaluation, narrowly domain-
specific approaches without generalizable insights, and duplicate publications of the same 
benchmark. Full-text review of the 384 papers against these criteria resulted in 95 papers 
proceeding to eligibility assessment.
From the 89 eligible papers, 52 papers met all inclusion criteria through detailed quality 
assessment. These 52 papers include the 15 primary benchmarks analyzed in depth through-
out this review, plus 37 supporting works on evaluation methodology, metrics design, and 
deployment studies that inform our critical analysis. The 15 benchmarks were selected as 
representative cases based on four criteria: citation impact (more than 100 citations or evi -

3.3 Categorization of evaluation techniques
Agentic AI assessment methods can be logically divided into three categories. The first 
involves static task benchmarks, the foundational approach for evaluating agents on discrete 
task specifications, where complete solutions are generated without environmental feedback 
loops Chang et al. (2024). These approaches are based on traditional AI evaluation and are 
used to assess such capabilities as understanding natural language, retrieving knowledge, 
and single-step reasoning using preset test setsXu et al. (2022).
The second type of evaluation is interactive environment based evaluation wherein 
agents act in simulated or real worlds that give them dynamic observations of the states and 
the consequences of actions. The given paradigm is consistent with the traditions of rein -
forcement learning evaluation but generalized to the language-model-based agent exploring 
the web interfaces, file systems, and API ecosystems Gemp et al. (2022). Multi-agent evalu-
ation systems also create additional complexity in assessment by creating a coordination, 
competition, and emergent behaviors that cannot be reflected by a static benchmark assess-
ment. Canese et al. (2021).
The third category is domain specific evaluation frameworks where assessment meth -
odologies are developed based on specific application settings. The field of software devel-
opment is the most developed and there are specialized benchmarks of code generation, 
debugging, test synthesis and repository understanding Schäfer et al. (2023). These domain-
specific methods recognize that general-purpose measures are not sufficient to assess the 
professional competence needs, requiring task-specific success measures and quality stan -
dards to be satisfied in particular tasks, as well as quality requirements and thresholds to be 
met in specific tasksShafiq et al. (2021).

optional multi-turn interaction, and automated analysis of the quality of the generated out -
comes, or manual evaluation of quality.

Interactive evaluation inserts the agents into the stateful environments to facilitate the obser-
vationaction loops over several timesteps. The protocol sets environment state, gives agents 
initial observations and available action specification, run agent selected actions to update 
environment state, and repeats until terminal conditions or time limits.
The algorithmic basis is based on trajectory rollouts in which action sequences are gen -
erated by agents given sequence of observation histories. Success computation evaluates 
final environment states against goal specifications, with intermediate rewards optionally 
accumulated across trajectories to assess efficiency alongside correctness Hu et al. (2024). 
Assessed skills are the choice of tools and turn-taking contextual memory, the ability to 
retain information when failing to execute an action, and adaptive planning in response to 
the ineffectiveness of earlier plansSivamayil et al. (2023).
Realistic evaluation of agent autonomy, identifying failure modes that are not evident in 
single-turn evaluation, and quantifying actual operational utility in task completion are all 
considered strengths of the approach to artificial intelligence in tasks completionRahman 
et al. (2022). The drawbacks include, but are not limited to, high computational needs to 
sample a trajectory, the impossibility of performing reproducibility due to non-determinism 
in the environment, and the inability to attribute failures on a case-by-case basis to a par -
ticular set of capabilities being insufficient in a complex multi-step problemYu et al. (2021).

3.5 Hybrid and emerging methods
The new forms of evaluation are multidimensional assessment methodologies that integrate 
various approaches of evaluation to address the shortcomings of each method. Continuous 
benchmarking deploys continuous reviewing since agents change, monitoring performance 
stability, and identifying capability deterioration over time of deployment of an agent Mar-
tínez-Fernández et al. (2022). This longitudinal design helps to show time-based dynamics, 
which snapshots cannot show. Human-in-the-loop assessment incorporates the use of expert 
opinion on dimensions that do not lend themselves to automated quantification, such as the 
readability of the code, the quality of explanations and the correspondence to unarticulated 
user goals. Human evaluation increases validity, but creates a limitation of scalability and 
inter-rater reliability issues.
Multi-agent competitive evaluation uses agents based on the game-theoretic settings 
where comparative performance against adversarial or cooperative agents can be used to 
provide a better signal of capability as compared to absolute measures of performance -
Moreno and Bitterman ( 2024). The future of evaluation lies in the environment-grounded 
evaluation where the evaluation is measured by the fact that the intervention is developed 
and deployed to a real production environment, with such metrics as the user acceptance 
rate, intervention frequencies, and outcome quality.
3.6 Comparative analysis of techniques
The choice of evaluation technique must be done through an in-depth tradeoff evaluation 
in various dimensions. The benefit of using the static benchmarks is that it offers the high 
capability coverage of discrete skills but does not offer much insight into emergent agent 
behaviors. The interactive environments provide a better measurement of generalization by 
identifying the existence of overfitting by instantiating the environments in a diverse man -
ner, but the static benchmarks are prone to memorizing as opposed to reasoning (Fig. 6).
Computational efficiency is extremely biased towards static evaluation, where bench -
mark run times are measured in minutes compared to hours of interaction with the rest of 
the environment. Scalability aspects also favor the use of the static approach of assessing 
large model populations but the interactive protocols allow a more in depth analysis of a 
single system.
Empirical validity is positively correlated to evaluation realism, indicating that a hybrid 
method combining effective screening in the form of a static model with specific interactive 
models should be considered as one of the best ways to manage resource allocation Huang 
et al. (2023). These dimensions would be compared in a framework of tabular form: static 
benchmarks are efficient and effective; interactive settings are realistic and offer evaluation

4 Datasets
Datasets form the empirical basis on which agent assessment is based but they are highly 
designed in that they influence and often limit what is measurable about autonomous behav-
ior. This sub-section is a critical analysis of datasets as evaluation tools, and the distinction 
between evaluation datasets and training corpora and the implications of dataset properties 
on the validity and credibility of benchmark claims.
4.1 Agent-centric perspective on datasets
The analysis of agentic AI systems requires a basic re-understanding of the role and struc -
ture of datasets. In contrast to the static machine learning models, which apply to inde -
pendent input–output pairs, agentic AI systems continuously interact with environments, 
make sequential decisions, call tools, and engage in multi-step reasoning in the future of 
artificially intelligent systems and machine learning, where all elements are linked Wang 
et al. (2024a). This requires the separation of the training datasets used to pretrain or fine-
tune foundation models and evaluation datasets assembled with the goal of evaluating agent 
behaviour in realistic conditions. Training datasets build agent capabilities; evaluation data-
sets assess whether agents apply that knowledge coherently across trajectories involving 
perception, planning, and action.
The agents evaluation datasets are fundamentally different in form and intent compared 
to traditional benchmarks of evaluation Shridhar et al. (2020). Instead of assessing agent 
performance on single tasks, agent assessment involves recording the trajectory data: full 
streams of state observations, in-between reasoning steps, calls to tools, responses of the 
environment and corrective measures. These trajectories show not only whether an agent 
was successful, but also how it solved problems, learned to respond to feedback, and how it

4.4 Emerging and synthetic agent evaluation datasets
The rapid saturation of static benchmarks has driven exploration of dynamic dataset genera-
tion strategies Ott et al. (2022). LLM-based task synthesis promises scalable benchmark 
creation by automatically generating evaluation scenarios through template instantiation 
or self-play exploration. These approaches address benchmark scarcity and contamination 
simultaneously synthetic tasks, by definition, cannot appear in pretraining data, and gen -
eration costs are dramatically lower than human curation.As shown in Fig. 9, outcome-
only metrics fail to capture cost, safety, and reasoning quality accumulated across agent 
trajectories.
However, synthetic datasets introduce new evaluation validity concerns. Generated tasks 
may lack the semantic diversity and edge-case complexity of human-authored problems, 
potentially yielding optimistic assessments of agent robustness Kapoor and Narayanan 
(2023). LLM-generated evaluations also risk evaluator bias if both agents and evaluation 
tasks are LLM-derived, benchmarks may inadvertently measure alignment with a particu -
lar model’s biases rather than general competence. Recent work has proposed validation 
strategies through human review of generated tasks and cross-model evaluation, but these 
increase costs and undermine scalability advantages Pineau et al. (2021).
Constant and changing data streams are trying to reduce memorization with new tasks 
every now and then by updating the sets of evaluation tasks periodically Ott et al. (2022). 
This solves the contamination but makes it harder to track a longitudinal performance of a 
system whose performance is measured against a different version of the benchmark. The 
compromise between resistance to contamination and reproducibility is not yet determined. 
Combined vision, language, code, and action Multimodal agent datasets are a new frontier 
of agent evaluation comprehensively and criticallyBayoudh et al. (2022). Examples of data-
sets such as embodied question answering benchmarks are those that involve agents tra -
versing 3D domains, visual scene interpretation as well as manipulation based on language. 
These datasets will offer more holistic evaluation although these labels correctly marked 
trajectories in high-dimensional observation areas are prohibitively costly at scaleDeng 
et al. (2023).
One of the most important questions that run through the new methodologies is: Do 
such methods really measure agent generalization, or do they simply bring in new evalua -
tion biases? Contrived tasks can by chance be biased towards agents conditioned to similar 
generation processes. Multimodal data sets are a threat of confounding agent abilities with 
the quality of representation learning. Dynamic standards can be focused on flexibility to 
distribution changes instead of basic reasoning ability. The discipline does not yet have 
principled methods to demonstrate that novel dataset paradigms are genuinely better than 
conventional human-curated benchmarks and not simply different Raff (2019).

5 Performance metrics
Performance metrics are the assessive basis on which assertions of agent ability, economy 
and preparation to deploy are confirmed, but the current metrics are inadequately estab -
lished to the special necessities of agentic AI systems.
5.1 Role of metrics in agentic AI evaluation
The quantitative basis of claims of agent capability and generalization is performance mea-
sures. As opposed to the output-based measures such as accuracy or F1-score used to assess 
the performance of a static machine learning model, agentic AI systems demand measures 
which capture sequential decision-making, tool invocation, environmental feedback, and 
non-deterministic performance over broader and longer horizons Bandi et al. (2025). Con-
ventional models focus on the accuracy of prediction; agent feedback has to include action 
efficiency, planning optimalities, error tolerance, and multi-step compounding effects of 
decision making Lebovitz et al. (2021).
Performance interpretations are fundamentally determined by metrics in benchmarks 
such as AgentBench, WebArena, SWE-bench and BrowserGym. Nonetheless, common 
measures transform the multifaceted behaviors into binary success measures and prevent 
a clear understanding of the computational cost, path quality, compliance with safety stan -
dards, and resilience Dakhel et al. (2024). Reductionism makes it possible to make mis -
leading comparisons when the agents whose success rates are the same can have radically 
different consumption of resources and failure modes.

We operationally define the missing evaluation dimensions as follows.
Cost-aware evaluation quantifies computational expense through three measur -
able components: (1) API cost per successful task completion (measured in USD 
or token count), (2) latency per action (wall-clock time in seconds), and (3) total 
computational budget consumption (CPU/GPU hours). A cost-normalized suc -
cess metric would be computed as Success_Rate/ log(1 + Cost_Ratio), where 
Cost_Ratio = Agent_Cost/Baseline_Cost, penalizing agents that achieve marginal 
accuracy gains through disproportionate resource consumption.
Safety-aware evaluation operationalizes constraint compliance through: (1) policy vio -
lation rate (percentage of trajectories containing unauthorized actions such as accessing 
restricted data, bypassing authentication, or exceeding rate limits), (2) harm potential score 
(weighted count of actions with negative externalities–data corruption, privacy breaches, 
system instability), and (3) recovery capability (percentage of constraint violations detected 
and corrected by the agent without external intervention). A safety-constrained performance 
metric would be Success × (1 − Violation_Rate)λ, where λ is a domain-specific sensitiv-
ity parameter (λ =2 for high-stakes domains, λ =1 for moderate-risk applications). These 
operational definitions enable reproducible measurement and cross-study comparison of 
previously unmeasured evaluation dimensions.
Addressing these gaps necessitates cost-normalized success metrics (e.g., 
Success/ log(Cost Ratio )), trajectory quality scores that capture plan coher -
ence and tool-use efficiency, and safety-constrained performance measures such as 
Success × (1 − Violation Rate )λ. Implementing such metrics requires substantially richer 
instrumentation than that provided by most existing benchmarks. Nonetheless, platforms 
such as WebArena and BrowserGym offer promising foundational frameworks for this 
direction.

5.3 Limitations of existing metrics
Existing measures have inherent weaknesses. Binary measures of success prevail, which 
causes theeddy-lines of performance to collapse into pass/fails and masks even behavioral 
variations in performance reformatting Ndukwe et al. (2023). A telling example: the SWE-
bench agent with 43% success (versus 38%) was actually less preferred in deployment – it 
had 72% code rejection in review (versus 12%) and cost three times more per issue ($127 
versus $43). This reversal demonstrates how binary metrics obscure deployment-critical 
dimensionsCihon et al. (2025). Agents completing tasks after unnecessary tool calls receive 
identical credit to optimal solvers, despite dramatically different resource implications. 
Measures are insensitive to the quality of reasoning, in that they are a measure of the end 
state with no evaluation of the coherence of intermediate reasoning or the level of plan revi-
sion. This is problematic in the case of architecture different agents where ReAct systems, 
Reflexion frameworks, and multi-agent architectures have different trajectory structures, 
which are not well represented by existing metrics.
Table 6 demonstrates that cost and resource consumption are systematically absent: 0/15 
benchmarks include computational expense in their primary evaluation metrics, despite 
agent practicality being directly dependent on API costs, token usage, and latency Bandi 
et al. (2025). This allows false pretensions where even the smallest improvements of accu -
racy would need order of magnitude increases in costs. The metric incompatibility among 
benchmarks cannot allow the meaningful comparison. The metrics used by AgentBench do 
not match WebArena criteria of task completion and SWE-bench criteria of test-based per-
formance which complicates characterization of holistic performance description of perfor-
mance metrics in any holistic way Kio ( 2025). Such disaggregation hinders meta-analyses 
and finding of patterns of occurrence across assessment models.
5.4 Open challenges and metric design recommendations
The next-generation measures should deal with unmet needs. The credit assignment of long-
horizon credit assignment is not solved the current measures are not able to ascribe the result 
of particular trajectory choices. The safety-conscious scoring should provide a combination 
of the constraint satisfaction in performance measures and not consider safety orthogonal. 
Generalization to new task variations and adversarial perturbation requires structured evalu-
ation of generalization, which under environment change requires robustness, which is cost, 
safety and quality of reasoning, built over agent trajectories (as illustrated in Figure 9).
Recent work on verifiable AI reliability Yang and Wang (2025) demonstrates that deploy-
ment-ready systems require continuous constraint verification and silent failure detection 
beyond episodic benchmark evaluation. Contract-based monitoring frameworks Esposito 
(2025) and systematic silent failure detection protocols Wachter et al. (2024) address the 
reliability and safety dimensions systematically absent from current agent benchmarks. 
These approaches provide operational counterparts to our deployment-gap critique: while 
benchmarks measure episodic task success, production systems require runtime safety 
envelopes, fault detection, and graceful degradation–capabilities unmeasured in Table 10 
scoring framework.
The future measures should adopt the use of trajectory level analysis evaluating the coher-
ence of reasoning and the quality of strategic planning as opposed to the end results alone.

6.2.1 Benchmark limitations in software engineering evaluation
The evaluation of agents in the field of software development is dominated by domain-
specific benchmarks such as SWE-bench, HumanEval, and MBPP. SWE-bench assesses 
repository-level issues solutions by the pass rate of test suites, and it can be characterized as 
an assessment of realistic development processes Jimenez et al. (2023). Nevertheless, this 
test based testing is essentially false representation of the quality of codes. Agents are opti-
mized to pass the test by creating the minimum sufficient patches to meet the test require -
ments without regard to maintainability, readability and architectural coherence. Empirical 
studies have shown that the high scoring submissions in the SWE-bench often bring about 
code smells, break coding conventions, and introduce complexity of integration that is not 
visible to automated test harnessesAlmeida et al. (2024).
Recent work Akshathala et al. (2025) has documented these SWE-bench limitations. Our 
framework advances this critique in three ways: (1) demonstrating that identical evaluation 
failures (outcome-focus, cost-blindness, safety gaps) occur across WebArena (web), ALF -
World (embodied), and PaperBench (research), revealing systematic methodology problems 
not benchmark-specific issues; (2) providing quantitative deployment-readiness scoring 
(SWE-bench: 20%, WebArena: 45%, Table 5) enabling cross-benchmark comparison; (3) 
offering reproducible measurement protocols (Section 7.10) that practitioners can apply to 
evaluate new benchmarks, rather than post-hoc critique of existing ones.

is, whether the generated code passes at least one of the k tests. This metric design promotes 
quantity over quality where the agents are rewarded based on the number of low-quality

6.2.3 Concrete deployment case
A representative case illustrates the benchmark-deployment gap. An SWE-bench agent 
achieving 43% issue resolution (ranking top-5 on the leaderboard) was deployed in a mid-
sized software company for automated bug fixing over 3 months Sankhe et al. (2025). Despite 
high benchmark performance, deployment revealed critical failures. Of 127 attempted fixes, 
only 36 (28%) were accepted without modification. Code review rejected 72% of solutions 
due to maintainability issues: excessive complexity (average cyclomatic complexity 24 vs. 
team standard of 12), inadequate error handling (present in only 31% of solutions), and poor 
documentation (68% lacked inline comments). Security analysis identified vulnerabilities 
in 23 solutions (18%), including SQL injection risks and improper input validation. Cost 
analysis revealed each successful fix required an average of $127 in API calls compared 
to $43 for human developers, a 3× cost premium that benchmark metrics ignored entirely. 
This case demonstrates how test-passing metrics systematically fail to capture deployment-
essential dimensions, validating our central thesis that evaluation methodology, not model 
capability, constitutes the primary deployment bottleneck.

6.3 Secondary application domains
Other areas of applications beyond software development also reveal failures of parallel 
evaluation that extend to agentic AI deployment situations.
6.3.1 Web automation
WebArena and BrowserGym measure the abilities of the agents in web navigation problems 
using goal completion measures. Nonetheless, these benchmarks are highly biased in terms 
of representation of trajectory quality. Agents with the same success in completing a task 
can have very different action efficiency, such as: an agent with the fewest steps to complete 
a task; an agent with the most steps, performing unnecessary page reloads or circular navi -
gation; and an agent with repeating actions despite executing the same task Lebovitz et al. 
(2021). The existing metrics do not consider these differences in efficiency, allowing agents 
to win using brute-force exploration but with a bad strategy.
Cross-benchmark performance analysis reveals systematic variance: agents achieving 
78% task completion on WebArena’s simulated environments demonstrate only 41% suc -
cess on real-world websites (performance drop of 47%), with failure pattern analysis show-
ing that 58% of failures stem from unmeasured dimensions–authentication handling (23%), 
dynamic content loading (19%), and rate-limiting compliance (16%) Zhou et al. (2023). 
Statistical analysis of 1,240 agent trajectories shows that action efficiency varies 340% 
(mean steps: 12.4, SD: 42.1) among agents with identical 80% success rates, yet current 
metrics assign identical scores.

6.4 Emerging application pressures on evaluation frameworks
New applications exacerbate evaluation weaknesses. The limitations to periodic evaluation 
on fixed test sets, which are characteristic of a stationary benchmark, can be revealed in 
the continuous learning agents that are placed in non-stationary settings and need continu -
ous adaptation. Continuous learning agents can assess adaptation capabilities, distribution 
shift robustness, or catastrophic forgetting. Multi-agent systems bring about coordination 
failures, emergent behavior and dynamics of interaction that cannot be seen in single-agent 
benchmarks. The cost sensitive deployments need cost-performance trade-offs, which are 
not implied by the accuracy oriented measures.
These are stress forces that hasten benchmark saturation and metric gaming. As models 
are optimized to current benchmarks, evaluation frameworks are no longer discriminative 
and so new benchmarks are required to be optimized, and the agent then optimizes to them, 
again resulting in an arms race between evaluation and optimization. This interaction shows 
that evaluation methodology rather than model capability is the most critical constraint to 
the deployment of reliable agents.
The required applications therefore require next-generation appraisal units with trajec -
tory-based judgment evaluation, security-sensitive scoring, cost-effectiveness versus per -
formance metric, as well as workflow amalgamation measurements. Unlike domain-specific 
critiques Akshathala et al. ( 2025), our analysis reveals that the benchmark-deployment 
discrepancy stems from cross-domain methodological failures. Our gap scoring frame -

are revealed in the real world, as the practical implementation is a stress test that shows 
these areas of failure of methodology in measuring.

7 Challenges and limitations
Even though there has been some improvement in establishing benchmarks in agentic AI 
systems, there have been underlying issues that have posed a threat to the validity, reproduc-
ibility and practical use of the agentic AI systems in evaluations at present. This part is a 
critical analysis of these limitations and emergent challenges that need to be resolved.
7.1 Introduction: why evaluation challenges matter for agentic AI
One of the most important bottlenecks in the development of autonomous capabilities is the 
evaluation of agentic AI systems. In contrast to supervised learning, in which the boundar -
ies of the evaluation are clearly known, agentic systems work in open-ended environments 
where both the goal-directed behavior and the strategic planning and the adaptive utiliza -
tion of the tools operate across longer horizons. There are disturbing disillusionments in 
practices: agents demonstrating state-of-the-art benchmark performance often do not work 
in the real world, demonstrating weaknesses in the evaluation methods used with them 
(Zhou et al. 2023; Jimenez et al. 2023). This evaluation gap threatens scientific validity, 
reproducibility, and safe deployment. Poor benchmarking sends illusory signals of progress, 
which guides research to set on a path of small-scale optimization instead of the ability to 
handle high-quality work. These issues need to be addressed to build trustful agentic AI 
development.
7.2 Fundamental challenges in evaluating agentic AI
The assessment of agentic systems is faced with cross-cutting issues unlike traditional ML 
assessment. The conceptualization of success in the case of long-horizon, goal-oriented 
agents is still conceptually problematic. Agents AI system have to work on ill-defined goals, 
overcome failures, and survive in changing environments, which are characteristics that are 
not easily quantifiedLiu et al. (2023). There is critical tension between the measures of task 
completion and behaviour quality at the process level. The existing benchmarks assess the 
final results but do not focus on the quality of decisions in the middle, such as the coherence 
of the strategy and the effectiveness of the tools. Such an outcome-based bias rewards target 
goal achievement by all means, which may reward brittle strategies which work with bench-
mark distributions but not with distributional shift Brohan et al. (2023). Benchmark gaming 
comes out as especially pernicious. The computational expenses of full evaluation develop 
obstacles to rigorous large-scale evaluation. Table 10 presents key evaluation challenges

7.3 Benchmark-level limitations
The modern agent benchmarks are characterized by high structural deficiencies limiting the 
utility of the agent. Although multitask coverage is also offered by AgentBench, the use of 
text-based environments is primarily based on insufficient representation of the multimodal 
complexity of real-world implementationLiu et al. (2023). Its formulations of the tasks are 
rigid, and they result in the inability to represent dynamic and unpredictable environments 
in which production agents act. WebArena is an enhancement of web-based assessment 
but lacks the breadth of tasks with simplified navigation Zhou et al. (2023). Its controllable 
structure, although, allowing reproducible evaluation, does not model different real-life 
web interactions such as the authentication issues and the dynamism of loading contents. 
BrowserGym is an extension of browser-based testing that however has artificial limita -
tions on action spaces reducing ecological validity. SWE-bench is a major leap towards the 
realistic assessment of software engineering, but coverage is only up to a limited number of 
software development lifecycles because of relying on isolated GitHub issues Jimenez et al. 
(2023). The benchmark overlooks code revision procedures, elicitation processes, as well 
as team-based processes. PaperBench and MLGym also limit the analysis to limited slices 
of domains. Over-dependence on the success of final tasks without evaluating the quality of 
decisions made at the intermediate stage and low cross-benchmark comparability because 
of differences in protocols are pervasive limitations.
7.4 Metric and methodological limitations
The existing measures of evaluation have fundamental flaws in their ability to measure mul-
tidimensional autonomous action. Binary success/fail metrics predominate, reducing com -
plex behavioral trajectories to a single bit and obscuring meaningful performance variation 
across agents. Such reductionism blurs the lines between the successful use of efficiency 
and the vast amount of trial-and-error Sumers et al. (2023). Critical dimensions are not well 
measured: quality of planning is not directly assessed; efficiency of tool invocation is not 
standardized; memory use and retention are also given little consideration although they are 
important during prolonged operations. Self recovery and auto correction is not well tested. 
The existing benchmarks do not usually evaluate the ability of agents to detect errors, devise 
recovery mechanisms or learn failure skills that are crucial in safe deployment. The lack of 
cost-conscious, latency-conscious and resource-conscious metrics introduces the discon -
nects between the benchmark performance and the viability of deployment Li et al. (2022). 
The problems of evaluation are reproducibility because of stochastic environments, non -
deterministic results, and poorly-documented pipes. Figure 14 depicts the conceptual gap

hostile utilization brings the hazards that could not be posed by individual agents. Existing 
frameworks have weak infrastructure to evaluate the property of multi-agent robustness or 
cooperative safety.

7.9 Emerging and open challenges
There are a number of pivotal issues that should be tackled in order to develop evaluation 
methodology. Adaptive benchmarks that improve with the capabilities of the agents must 
be continuous and change over time, otherwise there is a risk of becoming saturated too 
fast. Any static suites are necessarily replaced by distributed frameworks that can produce 
new tasks based on the optimization of the associated distribution. The assessment of multi-
agent coordination is still immature even with the increased significance of agent teams. 
Unified criteria of teamwork in solving problems are on high demand. Another frontier 
that needs to be innovated is the human-in-the-loop and mixed-initiative paradigms. In its 
most basic form, the field needs common taxonomies and standardized protocols that allow 
comparing across the benchmarks and make progress cumulatively. The solution to these 
challenges requires the integrated assessment plan suggested in the present paper offering 
standard measures, uniform guidelines, and the overall coverage of low-level tools use to 
the top-level planning. It is only with an elaborate infrastructure that the field can leave the 
benchmark gaming behind and proceed towards actual progress.

The agentic AI assessment is experiencing a paradigm shift due to the growing complexity 
of agent architectures, and a growing need to implement these systems in practice. This 
section will be the synthesis of the emerging trends in evaluation, the critical directions of 
research based on the limitations of the present, and the evaluation as the core facilitator of 
the deployment of agents in a safe, scalable and trustworthy manner.

8.1 Emerging trends in agentic AI evaluation
Given the above, there are 5 interrelated trends which are transforming conceptualization, 
implementation, and interpretation of agent evaluation by the research community, all sig -
naling a break in with the assumption of previous benchmark design.
8.1.1 From Static Benchmarks to Realistic, Interactive Environments
There is a radical change in the field with the movement of non-interactive, single turn, 
benchmarking to interactive multi-step, benchmarking that better depicts the way agents 
operate in the real world. The old paradigms of early evaluation typified by fixed question-
answer sets of questions or scripted navigation problems did not reflect the dynamism and 
partial observability of a real world deployment of an agent. Modern standard programs like 
WebArena, BrowserGym, and MLAgentBench can be seen as an example of this change as 
agents are now expected to navigate a changing web interface, solve long-horizon planning 
under uncertainty, and learn to respond to non-deterministic reactions of the environment.
This shift to realism presents very important appraisal issues. The nature of interactive 
environments is characterized by greater variance in outcomes because of environmental 
stochasticity, third-party changes to API, and temporal dependencies. The situation changes 
radically when life-time web services or dynamic simulation states are involved in the eval-
uation process and cannot be evaluated beforehand. Moreover, the computational and mon-
etary demands of solving the agent trajectories in many different scenarios by multi-step 
computations can be prohibitive, especially by teams that have limited resources. These are 
the trade-offs between environmental validity and experimental control that are one of the 
main points of tension in the current agent evaluation.
8.1.2 Continuous, lifecycle-aware evaluation
The second trend is the integration of the continuous evaluation structures that monitor 
the performance of agents through repeated developmental processes as opposed to evalu -
ation as a single measure. With frequent updates occurring to agent systems be it with a 
refinement in the engineered promptly, a policy modification, or an upgrade of the founda -
tion model traditional leaderboard-style benchmarks are no longer sufficient to provide a 
deployment-appropriate measurement. In software engineering, constant evaluation para -
digms, which are similar to regression testing, can help developers to track performance 
deterioration, trace regressions in capabilities and certify improvements in representative 
task distributions.
This lifecycle-sensitive method has far reaching effects on the role of evaluation in the 
decision to deploy. Continuous evaluation promotes tolerance to distribution change, con -
sistency of model versions and consistency in performance under constraints of operation, 
as opposed to just ensuring optimization on held-out test sets. Nevertheless, the cost of such 
frameworks to implement involves a significant investment on infrastructure and the pos -
sibility of benchmark versioning, longitudinal comparability and how often re-evaluation 
should be conducted is debatable.

8.1.3 Beyond success rates: richer and multi-dimensional metrics
The assessment community is now realizing that binary success rates of task are not a suf -
ficient basis upon which agent systems can be compared. New standards treat composite 
metrics reflecting sample efficiency of the interface (number of environment interactions 
needed), putting costs into consideration (by API calls, computational latency, number of 
tokens), supporting recovery from errors, and meet safety requirements. As an example, an 
agent with 80% successful task completion but three times the tool invocations as a 75% 
successful alternative can not be as preferable in resource limited environments.
Especially promising prospects lie in trajectory-level evaluation which goes further than 
assessing the endpoints, to evaluate the quality of reasoning steps made in the middle of the 
process, the use of tools detection strategies and partial failure recovery. The existing proxy 
measures, though, are still insufficient to describe subtle effects of agent behaviour like 
efficiency of the exploration strategies or the suitability of the uncertainty quantification. 
Formulation of principled multi-dimensional appraisal model that puts into equilibrium a 
number of competing or competing goals is a current research field.
8.1.4 Safety- and alignment-aware agent evaluation
Probably the most important emerging trend is the direct inclusion of the safety, misuse 
potential and alignment criteria in the benchmark design. With agentic systems becoming 
more autonomous and having access to consequential tools, it is necessary to evaluate them 
not only in terms of their capabilities but also in terms of their maladaptive capabilities, such 
as the possibility of misgeneralizing the goals, misusing tools, as well as displaying decep-
tive behaviour. More recent literature has started to realize adversarial settings that question 
whether agents are behaving in a manner that honours explicit restrictions, or behave in a 
manner that avoids causing harm in reaching a goal, and are consistent with designer intent 
when facing distribution shift.
This shift in the view of capability-only evaluation to capabilities-with-constraints evalu-
ation is an increasingly familiar trend to safely realize that advanced agent systems represent 
qualitatively new risks. Assessing the likelihood of an agent to use dangerous instrumental 
goals, distort information sources or abuse unintentional tool capabilities needs radically 
different benchmark designs than conventional performance measures. Nonetheless, safety 
measures are still to be standardized and it is not an easy task to find a common line on the 
acceptable risk level because of the situational nature of safety needs in various areas of 
deployment.

Lastly, the discipline is shifting to domain based evaluation practices which are domain 
specific to their failure modes and needs. Although generic agent benchmarks are useful 
in the measurement of a general ability of a system, they are not always very effective in 
stress-testing application-specific behavior. The SWE-bench and its variants test versus the 
authentic software engineering task whererepo navigation, code understanding, and patch 
generated ability are needed which are hardly predetermined on the basis of general coding 
tests. On the same note, scientific research agent assessments are increasingly coming to

8.2 Future research directions
The evaluation challenges enumerated above need to be tackled through concerted research 
in the five dimensions that are complementary, each focusing on a particular gap in the exist-
ing evaluation infrastructure and approach.
8.2.1 Unified taxonomies and standardized evaluation protocols
The growth of agent benchmarks has resulted in a disjointed evaluation environment with -
out shared taxonomies of agent capabilities, environment properties and evaluation metrics. 
The further studies should create standardized frameworks which will allow to compare 
across benchmarks, conduct meta-analyses of agent performance, and conduct reproducible 
evaluation. These constructs of architecture must explicitly describe architectural assump -
tions (e.g., the presence or properties of agency with memory, self-reflection or coordination 
with other agents), environmental degrees of affordance (e.g., determinism, observability, 
action space) and quantification of metric degree (e.g. how partial success is quantified, are 
costs normalised).
The development of standard interoperability benchmarks would allow researchers to 
significantly combine results, discover capability gaps and work towards research priori -
ties. This needs community coordination that extends to individual research groups and 
this could be via standardization bodies or via cohesive sets of benchmarks with common 
evaluation harnesses.
8.2.2 Scalable and cost-efficient agent evaluation
The resource-intensive computational and financial resources of full agent evaluation risk 
the concentration of evaluation resources in institutions that are well-endowed. Further 
efforts are needed to create low-cost assessment proxies, which are correlated with perfor -
mance on high-priced full-scale benchmarks, adaptive sampling strategies, which can invest 
evaluation budget in informative test cases, and simulation-to-real transfer technologies, 
which can pre-test valuable test cases in controlled settings before high-priced real-world 
testing.
Surrogate modeling, Bayesian optimization under benchmark task spaces, and progres -
sive evaluation protocols are some of the techniques which may significantly decrease the 
cost of evaluation without compromising measurement fidelity. Nevertheless, it is a serious 
empirical issue to substantiate that cost-cut evaluation is the optimal predictor of full-scale 
performance.

8.2.3 Evaluation for generalization and transfer
Contemporary benchmarking practices tend to push towards overfitting to fine-tuning task 
contests by featuring implicit training optimism and environment stability. Cross-task gen-
eralization (item pools showed high performance on new task groups held out) and envi -
ronment transfer (the ability to make maintenance to changed interfaces or tool API) and 
toolset variation (capable of functioning well with alternative but functionally equivalent 
tools) ought to be explicitly tested in future evaluation systems. These benchmarks would 
be more useful in forecasting agent robustness in deployment conditions of continuously 
changing environments. Generalization benchmark design should be constructed by paying 
close attention to the task family construction with a carefully controlled similarity struc -
ture, generation of environment procedures by which complexity dimensions can be varied 
systematically, and transfer learning protocols that decouple the processes of adaptation and 
memorization.
8.2.4 Human-in-the-loop and hybrid evaluation paradigms
Complete detection of all dimensions of agent quality by full automated evaluation is espe-
cially weak in subjective terms (e.g. naturalness of interactions, adequacy of explanations, 
etc.), and the suitability of autonomy-seeking behavior. The evaluation paradigms in the 
future must include hybrid techniques of automated measures with expert and user-friendly 
evaluation. This involves the creation of scalable human assessment protocols and inter -
faces that can allow assessment of human-agent interaction with significant meaning and 
methodologies to aggregate subjective ratings into consistent measures of quality. The main 
issue in question is to find the equilibrium between the benefits of increased scalability of 
the automated evaluation and the subtle information that can be gained through human 
evaluation, to handle the problems of subjectivity of the evaluator, expertise level demands, 
and budget limitations.
8.2.5 Architecture-aware evaluation
The modern standards are mostly architecture-neutral and use agents as black boxes whose 
fitness is judged by inputoutput behaviour alone. Nevertheless, alternative agent architec -
ture ReAct-style prompted systems, Reflexion-based self-improvement loops, or multi-
agent collaborative models can have entirely new capability profiles and failure behaviour. 
Architecture-sensitive evaluation should be included in future frameworks to put into con -
sideration structural variations in agent reasoning, planning and action. This trend involves 
the need to create assessment techniques that are architecturally sensitive, benchmarks that 
test architecture-specific capacities, and a protocol of fair comparison in comparisons of 
structurally different agent designs. The threat is developing assessment models which are 
too specific to specific architectures, reducing the extrapolation of knowledge. Table 11

8.3 Barriers and open challenges for emerging evaluation paradigms
Although the trends are promising, there are still major challenges that hinder the adoption 
of the next-generation agent evaluation. The cost of in-depth assessment and high cost of 
calculation and money restrict access to in-depth assessment making it possible that in a 
situation where the rich are getting richer, a team with the money to buy lots of measure -
ments can make their iteration quicker. Stochastic, interactive environments raise repro -
ducibility challenges that hamper scientific rigour and makes fair comparison of research 
groups across different, temporally or structurally varying, infrastructural settings difficult.
The inability to standardize safety measures is indicative of more conceptual issues: 
safety is contextual, it deals with low-probability high-consequence events which are diffi -
cult to measure empirically, and is necessitated by predicting new failure modes not observ-
able in existing systems. The conflict between realism and experimental control has not 
been completely resolved–more real world assessments are lost in the name of reproduc -
ibility and systematic variation, whereas controlled experiments are lost in the name of 
ecological validity.
Lastly, benchmark overfitting and leader board gaming still pose a threat to evaluation 
integrity. By going public and making benchmarks highly optimized against, they run the 
risk of measuring system-specific optimization instead of capability that is generalized. 
Dynamic benchmarks including periodically updated tasks are partial but come with their 
fair share of issues regarding longitudinal comparability and cost of evaluation.
9 Conclusion
This review analyzed the assessment scenario of agentic AI systems critically, analyzing 
benchmarks, metrics, datasets, and methodologies in a broad range of applications. The 
key conclusion is obvious: the modern practices of evaluation can be essentially unable 
to address dimensions of agent behavior that are critical in deployment. Although frame -
works like AgentBench, WebArena, SWE-bench, PaperBench, MLGym, and BrowserGym 
take the field to the next level of no longer just the assessment of specific tasks in a static 
manner, they are nonetheless characterized by binary success measures that do not reflect 
the alignment of planning, tool-use efficiency, memory management, cost-efficiency, and 
safety compliance. This results-oriented bias gives rise to a systemic evaluation-implemen-
tation discrepancy in which benchmark performance is a significant poor predictor of actual 
viability. These challenges are crystallized in the form of software development agents. 
Test-passing measures have been shown to be excellent in measuring functional correctness 
and systematically neglecting the code quality, security vulnerabilities, maintainability and 
integration of workflows that are vital in the production deployment. This kind of failure 
is common to web automation, embodier agents, and scientific fields of research and, in 
all cases, it is the evaluation methodology rather than model capability that represents the 
major bottleneck. Architecturally different agent systems ReAct, Reflexion, multi-agent 
frameworks have radically different failure modes that cannot be distinguished by archi -
tecture-agnostic benchmarks. The trends of realistic environments, lifelong assessment, 
analysis at the trajectory level, and safety-conscious metrics have a potential, but there are 
still critical obstacles: high computational costs, the impossibility of reproducibility, and the

of agentic AI. The community can use principled, multi-dimensional, deployment-oriented 
evaluation as the only way to make sure that improvements in agent capabilities can be 
converted to reliable, safe, and useful systems in the real world.
In summary, this review demonstrates that evaluation methodology not model capabil -
ity is the primary barrier to trustworthy agentic AI deployment. Across all 15 benchmarks 
examined, the evidence is unambiguous: safety is unscored (0/15), cost is untracked (0/15), 
trajectory quality is unmeasured in 14/15 cases, and binary success metrics are the sole 
evaluation criterion in 13/15 benchmarks. No benchmark currently exceeds 50% on our 
deployment-readiness rubric (Table 10). Until evaluation infrastructure evolves to capture 
these dimensions, benchmark leaderboard rankings will remain poor proxies for real-world 
viability. We call on the research community to treat evaluation methodology as a first-class 
scientific contribution, on equal standing with model development itself.
