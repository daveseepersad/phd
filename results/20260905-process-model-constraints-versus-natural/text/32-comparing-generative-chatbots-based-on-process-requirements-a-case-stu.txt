Comparing Generative Chatbots Based on Process
Requirements: A Case Study
Luis Fernando Lins, Nathalia Nascimento, Paulo Alencar, Toacy Oliveira, Donald Cowan
David R. Cheriton School of Computer Science
University of Waterloo (UW)
Waterloo, Canada
{lflinsdossantos, nmoraesd, palencar, toliveira, dcowan}@uwaterloo.ca
Abstract—Business processes are commonly represented by
modelling languages, such as Event-driven Process Chain (EPC),
Yet Another Workflow Language (YA WL), and the most popular
standard notation for modelling business processes, the Business
Process Model and Notation (BPMN). Most recently, chatbots,
programs that allow users to interact with a machine using
natural language, have been increasingly used for business
process execution support. A recent category of chatbots worth
mentioning is generative-based chatbots, powered by Large Lan-
guage Models (LLMs) such as OpenAI’s Generative Pre-Trained
Transformer (GPT) model and Google’s Pathways Language
Model (PaLM), which are trained on billions of parameters
and support conversational intelligence. However, it is not clear
whether generative-based chatbots are able to understand and
meet the requirements of constructs such as those provided by
BPMN for process execution support. This paper presents a
case study to compare the performance of prominent generative
models, GPT and PaLM, in the context of process execution
support. The research sheds light into the challenging problem
of using conversational approaches supported by generative
chatbots as a means to understand process-aware modelling
notations and support users to execute their tasks.
Index Terms—business processes, generative chatbots, large
language models, process modelling languages, process-aware
support.
I. I NTRODUCTION
Processes are pervasive in personal and organizational set-
tings. They can support a wide variety of tasks, ranging from
scheduling a trip [1] to hiring new employees or admitting
students into a university program [2]. For this reason, orga-
nizations are increasingly relying on automated processes to
improve efficiency and accuracy in the execution of their tasks.
In many cases, processes require people to perform tasks
manually. These processes are known as human workflows
[3]. Although mechanisms have been proposed to visualize,
control, and manage processes, performing tasks in a process
involving numerous human decisions can be more complicated
than it seems. First, tasks may have either a strict or a
loose order, and this might leave participants confused in
complex scenarios. Second, dependencies between tasks can
be challenging to track, in which case, not being aware of
the information that is required a priori, people may start
executing tasks they are not able to finish properly. Third,
processes that rely heavily on humans may differ based on
their context or require access to ancillary documentation so
that errors are avoided [4]. Owing to these challenges, process
participants should have a solution to guide them through
process instances.
One possible solution to help guide users through business
processes are chatbots, also known as conversational agents.
Chatbots are an emerging technology that has become ever so
present in various domains, from healthcare to customer ser-
vice [5]. This technology’s main selling point is that chatbots
are able to talk to humans, understand their needs, and respond
in a coherent way, while also being available 24/7. Many
businesses and academic projects have been using chatbots
as a valuable tool, but there are still some challenges in the
chatbot field that remain to be solved in an effective way. Some
of these challenges include personalized responses based on
user context, analysis of customer behaviour from interaction
history, sentiment and emotion analysis, and negotiation with-
out human intervention [6] [7].
On the other hand, chatbots can be very unpredictable,
deciding conversation paths by itself that not always corre-
spond to the business’s intention, especially when the chatbot
is machine-learning-based [8]. Therefore, another challenge
in the development of chatbots is creating one that closely
follows the business requirements. It is not clear whether
generative-based chatbots are able to understand and meet
the requirements of constructs such as those provided by
BMPM for process execution support. For this reason, this
paper presents a case study to compare the performance of
prominent generative models, GPT and PaLM, in the context
of process execution support. The research sheds light into
the challenging problem of using conversational approaches
supported by generative chatbots as a means to understand
process modelling notations and support users to execute their
tasks.
Our strategy involves two steps. First we produce the
Extensible Markup Language (XML) file related to a specific
case study (e.g., travel planning) using a BPMN-modelling
application, in this case, Camunda Modeler. Second, we pro-
vide the chatbot with the entire XML file related to a BPMN
model, and let it interpret it on its own. We will send the XML
file related to one BPMN process model to two different LLM
chatbots (GPT-3.5 and PaLM 2), in an attempt to see how well

II. R ELATED WORK
There are multiple works that have been previously pub-
lished that cover the use of chatbots for helping in the
execution of business processes. Kecht et al. introduced a
study on quantifying a chatbot’s ability to learn and adhere
to organizations’ business processes [9]. Their chatbots were
trained with a dataset of customer support conversations with
human support agents on Twitter, and their work quantified
the ability of these chatbots to learn the process from the
dataset and adhere to the discovered process. von Wolff et al.
conducted a research study to understand users’ perspectives
in terms of design and acceptance of a process-based chatbot
[10]. Their results showed that users can execute business
processes in a similar or faster time when using process-based
chatbots compared to existing solutions, and that this kind of
chatbot also reduces the risk of users making critical errors or
experiencing interruptions when using process-based chatbots
to execute a business process.
However, both of the previous studies only analyzed ad-
hoc processes, not ones described in a systematic notation.
Meanwhile, there are also works that use the BPMN notation
with chatbots, such as Amantea et al.’s study, that presents a
framework that leverages both BPMN modelling and chatbot
technology to enhance healthcare processes [11]. Meanwhile,
Rooein et al. investigate the use of chatbots to teach business
models written in BPMN to different actors in a company [12].
Although there have been advances in the use of differ-
ent kinds of chatbots to support conversational interactions
with users in terms of BPMN process execution, there is a
need to understand to what extent generative-based chatbots,
specifically, such as GPT and PaLM can understand modeling
notations for business processes and provide users with process
execution support.
III. G ENERATING THE XML OF THE BPMN P ROCESS
REPRESENTATION
The XML file for a specific case study, that is, trip planning,
was generated using Camunda Modeler. The Trip Planning
model is shown in Figure 1. We omit details about how the
process is modelled using Camunda Modeler, because our
focus is on how well generative chatbots understand the XML
file related to the BPMN model of the process.
BPMN uses a set of symbols and graphical notations to
represent different elements of a business process. Some
common symbols used in BPMN include:
1) Activities, which can represent tasks or actions per-
formed within a process;
2) Events, which represent something that occurs during
the course of a process, such as the start or end of a
process;
3) Gateways, which indicate points in a process where the
flow can diverge or converge based on certain conditions.
There are multiple types of gateways in the BPMN
notation, such as parallel gateways, exclusive gateways,
inclusive gateways, and more;
4) Flows, which depict the sequence and direction of ac-
tivities and events within a process;
The approach involves directly providing the BPMN XML
file to the chatbot and allowing it to autonomously parse and
comprehend all the elements within the BPMN model. This
idea was based on the chatbot’s ability to effectively extract
the relevant process information from the BPMN XML file
without the need for prior in-depth explanation about the
process in natural language. The only message that we sent
before the XML file, to give the chatbot some context about
what it was expected to do, was: “Given the BPMN XML
below, act as an agent to help me execute this process, given
BPMN restrictions. You should only list the tasks that are
available at that particular moment.”
IV. E VALUATIONS QUESTIONS USED IN COMPARING
GENERATIVE CHATBOTS
In this section, we discuss the evaluation questions (EQs)
that we will use to compare whether the chosen generative-
based chatbots can understand and follow the BPMN require-
ments as specified by the official BPMN specification [13].
These evaluation questions were thoughtfully designed and
are categorized into six distinct categories, each addressing
specific aspects of the chatbots’ performance and adherence
to BPMN standards. The six categories include:
1) Start Event : These questions focus on assessing the
chatbots’ ability to initiate process instances in line with
BPMN’s Start Event requirements.
2) Forward Flow : The evaluation questions in this cat-
egory delve into the chatbots’ capability to maintain
the sequential flow of activities and decisions within a
process.
3) Previous History: This category of questions examines
the chatbots’ handling of historical data and their ability
to integrate past information into the ongoing processes.
4) End Event : Here, we assess the chatbots’ proficiency
in finishing process instances appropriately, following
BPMN’s End Event guidelines.
5) Process Variables : This category of evaluation ques-
tions analyzes how well the chatbots manage and ma-
nipulate process variables.
6) Unintended paths : The final category of evaluation
questions checks if the chatbot allows the user to per-
form actions that are not supposed to be executed at a
given moment.

By categorizing the evaluation questions into these six
distinct areas, we aim to provide a comprehensive assessment
of the generative-based chatbots’ compliance with BPMN,
covering critical aspects of process modelling and execution.
A. Category 1: Start Event
EQ1: Has the process instance been initiated successfully
after triggering the start event?
Represented by sending the chatbot the following message:
“Let’s start a new process instance and name it X1.”
EQ2: Does the chatbot correctly mention the first task
that follows the start event after the process instance is
initiated?
Represented by asking the chatbot: “What tasks are avail-
able to me at the beginning?” and verifying if only the first
task is mentioned.
B. Category 2: F orward Flow
EQ3: After completing a task, if it is followed by another
task directly connected by a direct sequence flow, does the
chatbot correctly mention the next task?
Represented by analyzing the chatbot’s response after in-
forming the chatbot that a task was completed.
EQ4: After completing a task, does the chatbot avoid
mentioning tasks that are not yet available in the process
flow?
Represented by analyzing the chatbot’s response after in-
forming the chatbot that a task was completed.
EQ5: After completing a task and encountering an
exclusive gateway, does the chatbot correctly evaluate the
gateway based on the conditions of its outgoing sequence
flows and present only one valid flow to the user?
Represented by analyzing the chatbot’s response after in-
forming the chatbot that a task was completed.
C. Category 3: Previous History
EQ6: When presenting a task to the user, if it was
immediately preceded by another task through a sequence
flow, does the chatbot correctly identify the preceding
task?
Represented by asking the chatbot the following question:
“What was the previous task that was executed?”
EQ7: When presenting a task to the user, if it was pre-
ceded by an exclusive gateway, does the chatbot accurately
convey the decision represented by the preceding gateway?
Represented by asking the chatbot the following question:
“Why is this the only available task?”
D. Category 4: End Event
EQ8: Upon reaching an end event, does the chatbot
accurately identify the task that immediately preceded it
in the process flow?
Represented by asking the chatbot the following question:
“What was the last task before the process instance was
ended?”
EQ9: When reaching an end event, does the chatbot
ensure that the process instance is terminated only when
all start events have been triggered and all pending flows
have been completed?
Represented by asking the chatbot the following question:
“Had all the start events been triggered, and had all pending
flows been finished?”
E. Category 5: Process Decision Points
EQ10: At the end of the process, does the chatbot retain
knowledge of all the decisions that had to be made at
gateways?
Represented by asking the chatbot the following question:
“What were the decisions made at each gateway?”
EQ11: At the end of the process, does the chatbot retain
knowledge of the user’s choice at each gateway?
Represented by asking the chatbot the following question:
“What was the user’s choice at each gateway for this particular
process instance, X1?”
F . Category 6: Unintended paths
EQ12: Does the chatbot allow the user to perform a task
that is not yet available in the process?
Represented by having the user tell the chatbot that they
want to execute a task that is not on the list of available tasks
that the chatbot sent.
EQ13: Does the chatbot allow the user to end the process
when it is not supposed to?
Represented by asking the chatbot the following question:
“Can I end the process now?”, before all the required tasks
are done.

proceed with the “Book hotel” task, and then we

Overall, GPT-3.5 meets most of the criteria used in our
evaluation. However, EQ12, which prevents the user from
executing unavailable tasks, was not met, and EQ5, related

due to an unexpected behaviour when reaching an exclusive
gateway. That suggests that GPT-3.5 might have a hard time

B. PaLM: Trip Planning Use Case
After analyzing how well GPT adheres to the Trip Planning
process model using its BPMN XML file, we now move on to
the evaluation of Google’s PaLM model, so as to compare its
execution to GPT, again with the same process model. In this
section, we will answer the same evaluation questions as the
previous subsection, to be able to compare PaLM and GPT
with a structured methodology.
For PaLM, we used the same XML document for the Trip
Planning model as the one we used for GPT. Throughout this
section, we will be analyzing each of the six main categories
that are related to the adherence of PaLM to the BPMN
constructs utilized in our process model.
1) Category 1: Start Event: After creating a new process
instance named X1 and asking for the available tasks, PaLM
correctly initiates the process instance and presents the only
available task, as shown by the conversation below:
User: Let’s start a new process instance and name it
X1. What tasks are available to me at the beginning?
Chatbot: The following tasks are available to you
at the beginning of the process instance X1:
- book flight
Therefore, both EQ1 (Has the process instance been initiated
successfully after triggering the start event?) and EQ2 (Does
the chatbot correctly mention the first task that follows the
start event after the process instance is initiated?) are met for
this particular use case.
2) Category 2: F orward Flow: After booking the flight,
the next task, “book hotel” which is directly connected to
“book flight” through a sequence flow, is presented to the
user. However, PaLM also presents another task that should
not have been presented, which was the “book transfer”.
User: I’ve booked the flight
Chatbot: The following tasks are available to you
now:
- book hotel
- book transfer
Because the “book hotel” task has been correctly presented,
we argue that EQ3 (After completing a task, if it is followed by
another task directly connected by a direct sequence flow, does
the chatbot correctly mention the next task?) is met. However,
because it also mentions a task that is not yet available, EQ4
(After completing a task, does the chatbot avoid mentioning
tasks that are not yet available in the process flow?) is not
met.
Furthermore, when we challenge the chatbot asking if it is
sure that the “book transfer” task is available, it also lists its
incorrect assumption about the decision at the gateway.
User: Are you sure the book transfer task is avail-
able?
Chatbot: Yes, the book transfer task is available.
Since you did not select the “If user does not want
to book a transfer” path in the Gateway 01f6asu
gateway, the book transfer task is available.
Therefore, we argue that EQ5 (After completing a task and
encountering an exclusive gateway, does the chatbot correctly
evaluate the gateway based on the conditions of its outgoing
sequence flows and present only one valid flow to the user?) is
partially met, because the chatbot does present only one valid
flow but it did not correctly evaluate the gateway based on the
conditions of its outgoing sequence flow, as it did not ask the
user for their explicit choice.
3) Category 3: Previous History: For EQ6, (When pre-
senting a task to the user, if it was immediately preceded
by another task through a sequence flow, does the chatbot
correctly identify the preceding task?), we ask the chatbot
about the previous task executed, after completing the “Book
flight” task, and it responds appropriately. Therefore, EQ6 is
met, as shown in the excerpt below:
User: What was the previous task that was executed?
Chatbot: The previous task that was executed was
book flight.
Later in the process, after the user completes the
“book hotel” task, the chatbot fails to ask the user’s trans-
fer preference. Instead, it lists both the “book transfer” and
“book tour” tasks:
User: I’ve booked the hotel
Chatbot: The following tasks are available to you
now:
- book transfer
- book tour
Even though for EQ7 (When presenting a task to the user,
if it was preceded by an exclusive gateway, does the chatbot
accurately convey the decision represented by the preceding
gateway?), the question that should be asked was “Why is
this the only available task?”, in the current conversation, this
question does not make sense because the chatbot presented
two tasks, and not only one, as it should have. Therefore, EQ7
is not met.

“book tour” task before mentioning that the “book transfer”
task had been completed, to see how the chatbot would react.

VI. C ONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK
This research underscores the potential of chatbots as valu-
able tools in business process execution. It illuminates avenues
for future research and development, offering guidance on how
to harness generative models for more effective support. As
organizations continue to seek efficient and adaptable solutions
for their processes, this work provides a substantial step toward
a better integration of chatbots in the domain of business
process execution support.
Future work involves extending the evaluation criteria, and
including other process modelling constructs and generative
chatbots. The first future research avenue would be to extend
the evaluation criteria to include other BPMN constructs
and assess the applicability of generative-based process-aware
chatbots for different process notations. Second, the opportu-
nity of using generative-based chatbots to assist in the initial
modelling of BPMN processes (instead of only in their exe-
cution) could be explored. These chatbots could take a list of
tasks, decisions, events, and other BPMN constructs detailed in
a natural language prompt, and generate the final BPMN XML
documents. This approach has the potential of streamlining
the process modelling phase and simplifying the work of
process designers, potentially enhancing the overall efficiency
of BPMN modelling. Third, future work could investigate the
application of alternative generative chatbot models beyond
GPT and PaLM. Analyzing how different models, such as
Meta’s OPT and NVIDIA’s Megatron-Turing, perform in the
context of BPMN model understanding and process execution
support could be a promising research direction.
Overall, these proposed directions for future work can
contribute to the development of generative-based chatbots in
the context of BPMN processes, ultimately advancing the field
of process automation and support.
