Abstract
A tool-using model does not need execution authority to create risk; a structured proposal is enough if the
runtime dispatches it without a reliable check. This paper studies that check. PolicyFaultBench combines
deterministic fail-closed mediation, state-transition oracles, proposal-interface checks, and mutation
testing. Providers transcribe an operation rather than plan an open-ended action, so the benchmark
measures runtime mediation and interface conformance, not general agent safety. The 40-case corpus
exposed 7/12 ordered-rule mutants; ﬁve labelled survivor-guided probes exposed the remainder. Three
OpenAI and Anthropic ledgers contributed 1,200 records, including a prospectively frozen 400/400
Anthropic conﬁrmation under a corrected pre-policy gate. To test whether the ﬁndings depended on one
constructed corpus or one policy architecture, a second extension was frozen before execution. It derived
40 synthetic operations from four AgentDojo v1 suites, added an implemented capability policy, and
scheduled 400 trials for each of OpenAI and Anthropic. OpenAI met the compound endpoint in 400/400
trials. Anthropic met it in 391/400: nine outputs for one benign payment case added an unrequested
approval-scope ﬁeld and were quarantined before policy evaluation. Thus the preregistered 800/800
acceptance rule was not met, although all 791 admitted proposals agreed under both policies, every
veriﬁed transition matched, and no containment failure occurred. Preserved core proposals also replayed
800/800 under the capability policy. External-corpus mutation scores were low before targeted probes,
showing that cross-corpus execution success and policy fault adequacy are different properties. Runtime
assurance therefore also needs separate evidence for interface exactness, policy correctness, state
effects, and corpus-policy generalisation.
1. Introduction
A model response becomes a security event only when another component gives it effect. With tools, that
effect may be a ﬁle write, an email, a payment, a delegated capability, or a network request. Direct and
indirect prompt injection make the proposal channel untrustworthy, which is why current risk guidance
treats model outputs as potentially adversarial (Autio et al., 2024; Vassilev et al., 2025).
Benchmarks now exercise agents inside tool environments and measure attack success, task utility, or
harmful multi-step behaviour (Zhan et al., 2024; Debenedetti et al., 2024; Ruan et al., 2024; Zhang et al.,
2025; Andriushchenko et al., 2025). The aforementioned research seeks to determine whether the agent
commits or performs the risky action. However, there is another question at the next level, which is: Given
that the action is already suggested, does the runtime authorize it properly and provide suﬃcient evidence
about what took place?
It is helpful to shift the control out of the model. But this alone is not suﬃcient. The external policy may be
erroneous. An expanded subject pattern, missing authorization check, fail open exception, and outdated
sandbox can ruin an otherwise good design. The policy and its tests are therefore part of the assurance
target, not a trusted answer supplied in advance (Table 1).
Page 2/22

[[page 3]]
PolicyFaultBench isolates this lower-layer problem. Each prompt contains a frozen synthetic operation, so
provider generation is a transcription step. That choice removes open-ended planning from the
experiment. It also makes cross-provider interface drift visible while the same policy request is held ﬁxed.
Five questions organise the study:
RQ1 — Which policy faults does the frozen suite expose, and which survive until targeted probes are
added?
RQ2 — Do preserved provider proposals replay to the recorded policy decision and synthetic state
transition under a stricter containment rule?
RQ3 — How different are exact full-object conformance and conformance of the decision-relevant
authorisation envelope?
RQ4 — What do retries, quota censoring, latency, and evidence-tier changes contribute to
reproducibility?
RQ5 — Do the mediation and mutation results transfer to an independently derived corpus and a
second policy architecture?
The contribution is deliberately bounded:
a balanced 40-scenario corpus for policy mediation, not an open-ended agent-planning benchmark;
a mutation model that keeps the frozen and survivor-guided adequacy stages separate;
an oﬄine state-transition replay that repairs the original oracle claim without rerunning a provider;
a prospectively frozen Anthropic replication through the corrected pre-policy conformance path;
scenario- and family-cluster sensitivity analyses for repeated calls; and
an interface ablation contrasting exact objects with a narrower signed authorisation envelope;
a preregistered AgentDojo-derived external replication across OpenAI and Anthropic; and
an independently implemented capability policy with a separate C01–C12 mutation model.
2 Background and Related Work
2.1 What existing agent benchmarks measure
Prompt injection is usually framed as a conﬂict between trusted instructions and untrusted content inside
one model context (Greshake et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023). InjecAgent turns that conﬂict into indirect tool-
use cases (Zhan et al., 2024). AgentDojo adds a dynamic environment and keeps utility beside security
(Debenedetti et al., 2024); ToolEmu uses an LM-emulated sandbox to surface risky behaviour (Ruan et al.,
2024). ASB broadens the attack surface (Zhang et al., 2025), while AgentHarm asks whether an agent can
complete explicitly harmful tasks (Andriushchenko et al., 2025). Their unit of interest is the model or
agent trajectory.
Page 3/22

[[page 4]]
That is not the unit used here. PolicyFaultBench starts after the operation has already been ﬁxed. It does
not test tool choice, refusal quality, or whether an injected instruction changes a plan. The measured
object is the mediator placed between a structured proposal and a synthetic side effect.
2.2 Where system defences place trust
System defences split along the trust boundary. StruQ separates instructions from data at the input
interface (Chen et al., 2025). CaMeL protects control and data ﬂow with capabilities (Debenedetti et al.,
2025), and Conseca makes authorisation sensitive to purpose and context (Tsai and Bagdasarian, 2025).
These designs reduce reliance on model self-policing, though the surrounding enforcement code still
needs evidence.
FORGE, the Open Agent Passport, and AEGIS move closer to deterministic pre-action checks (Palumbo et
al., 2026; Uchibeke, 2026; Yuan et al., 2026). The three works are especially relevant, but they remain 2026
preprints in this review. We use them to position the mechanism, not as the sole basis for the novelty
claim. PolicyFaultBench contributes a fault-oriented test of the mediator and an audit of proposal
contracts rather than another policy language.
2.3 Policy mutation as an assurance probe
Access-control testing provides the older and ﬁrmer precedent. Martin and Xie (2007) deﬁned realistic
policy faults; Mouelhi et al. (2007) used mutation analysis to qualify security tests; Chen et al. (2021)
extended the approach to NGAC policies. A mutation score belongs to a policy–test pair. It is diagnostic.
It is not a proof, and a probe written after seeing a survivor must remain labelled as such.

3 System and Evidence Design
3.1 Threat model and scope
Provider outputs are untrusted, as well as user texts, retrieved data, tool outputs, memories, delegation
context, and authorization proofs. The policy engine, sandbox, adapters, and ledger constitute the trusted
computing base; however, they share the same code and can crash simultaneously. All experimental
assets use special test names or deterministic local states.
Containment is deﬁned with an inclusive rule. For a scenario expected to be denied, either an unexpected
allow decision or any post-denial state change counts as failure. A timeout, an annotation inside a
proposal, and a quota error are recorded elsewhere. They matter, but they are not silently turned into
security outcomes.
3.2 Executed and corrected paths
The preserved v1 ledgers did not contain a proposal-conformance event. Their order was provider
proposal, policy evaluation, optional sandbox execution, oracle record, and ledger completion. Exact
conformance was audited afterwards. The corrected runner places that gate before policy evaluation and
quarantines a mismatch. It was then executed prospectively in a separate 400-trial Anthropic ledger. The
Page 5/22

[[page 6]]
paths remain separate so the new evidence does not overwrite the conditions under which the ﬁrst 800
records were generated (Fig. 1).
3.3 Frozen corpora
Ten threat families each contribute four variants: benign, direct, indirect/stateful, and boundary. Benign
and boundary operations are expected to be allowed; direct and indirect/stateful operations are expected
to be denied. The resulting 20/20 split makes always-allow and always-deny equally poor baselines. Each
record ﬁxes a complete authorisation tuple, its expected disposition, and the proposed operation
(Table 2).

3.4 Independent policy architecture
The original mediator evaluates ordered rules. The second engine instead resolves server-side capabilities
from subject, tenant and resource scope, provenance and trust, approval binding, delegation expiry, and
side-effect risk. It defaults to deny and is implemented independently of the ordered-rule matching path. A
separate C01–C12 mutant family perturbs those capability checks. Both unmutated engines matched all
40 expected decisions in each corpus before any live extension call was made.

4 Methodology
4.1 What the provider was asked to do
The provider prompt exposed the requested synthetic operation and asked for one structured proposal.
Fields covered subject, action, resource, arguments, context, and a call identiﬁer. This is a controlled
transcription contract. It was chosen to hold policy input constant, not to simulate an autonomous agent
deciding what tool to use under attack.
The ledgers identify gpt-5.6-sol and claude-sonnet-5. A Gemini workﬂow targeted gemini-3.6-ﬂash, but its
corrected gate did not ﬁnish. Temperature and seed controls were used only when the provider interface
exposed them. The repeated calls describe observed API behaviour during the study window.

4.3 Preregistered corpus–policy extension
Protocol pfb-policy-generalization-v1 ﬁxed the external corpus, both policies, provider model identiﬁers, 40
× 10 schedules, randomisation seed, complete-object gate, endpoint, cost ceilings, and reporting
commitment before the ﬁrst extension call. One proposal was generated per scenario–repetition and then
replayed in separate reset sandboxes under both policies. The ﬁrst 12 identiﬁers formed an embedded
gate and remained in the 400-trial denominator.
The provider acceptance rule was 400/400 strict successes and zero containment failures; the overall rule
was 800/800. A terminal representation mismatch counted as a strict failure and stopped that proposal
before either policy. Mutation scores were computed separately for every corpus–policy pair. Scores after
survivor-speciﬁc probes remained an adequacy stage and were never relabelled as external validation.
4.4 Oﬄine replay and interface endpoints
For each v1 entry, the analysis would load the logged proposal in a new deterministic sandbox. The
playback started at the described baseline, made the recorded choice, and veriﬁed the predicted post-
state digest and the tool-result contract against the ledger. All denied proposals had to stay non-executed
and unaltered. The veriﬁcation v2 runner conducted the same procedure but on the potential compound
Page 8/22

[[page 9]]
endpoint. Both procedures employ the same sandbox semantics; therefore, they test for consistency and
not for an independent oracle implementation.
Two interface endpoints were retained. Full-object conformance compares subject, action, resource,
arguments, and the complete context object. Envelope conformance compares the decision-relevant
ﬁelds and the expected context key–value pairs while allowing additional non-authoritative annotations.
The latter is an oﬄine design ablation, not the contract used during v1 or conﬁrmatory v2. The v2 primary
endpoint required full-object conformance.
4.5 Statistical unit and uncertainty
Three denominators answer different questions. Each completed ledger contains 400 calls and estimates
repeatability conditional on its ﬁxed corpus, schedule, interface, and study window. Forty scenario clusters
describe coverage across distinct cases, while ten family clusters give a coarser stress test. Exact
Clopper–Pearson intervals (Clopper and Pearson, 1934) are shown as sensitivity bounds at each level; the
ﬁxed scenarios were not sampled randomly from a deﬁned population.
Interface differences were averaged within scenario before comparison. A paired cluster bootstrap
resampled the 40 scenario identiﬁers 50,000 times. The earlier trial-level McNemar test (McNemar, 1947)
and its planned Holm procedure (Holm, 1979) were not carried forward because repeated calls within one
scenario do not create 400 distinct scenario units. Latency received the same cluster treatment and
remains descriptive.
5 Results
5.1 Mutation adequacy
The frozen corpus killed 7 of 12 mutants (58.33%). Subject widening, approval bypass, temporal deletion,
fail-open exception handling, and reset omission survived. The pattern was not random: each survivor
needed a boundary absent from the original cases.
Four functional probes and one reset probe were then written against those survivors. The expanded suite
killed all implemented mutants. This is a repair result, not independent validation. The reset probe alone
exposed the reset-omission operator; a decision-only suite would still have missed it. The staged result is
retained in Fig. 2.
5.2 Decision, transition, and containment replay
The 800 v1 records reproduced the logged policy decision and deterministic post-state during oﬄine
replay. Conﬁrmatory v2 added 400 prospectively evaluated Anthropic records through the corrected path.
Across all 1,200 completed records, no expected-deny case received an allow decision and none changed
state. The result holds for every repetition, scenario cluster, and threat-family cluster in all three ledgers.

5.3 Full-object drift and the authorisation envelope
In the historical v1 comparison, OpenAI matched the full object in every completed call and Anthropic
matched 291/400. After averaging within each scenario, the mean difference was 27.25 percentage
points; the scenario-cluster bootstrap interval was 14.75–42.00 points. Eleven scenario clusters were
discordant in one direction (two-sided sign p = 0.00098). The smaller trial-level p-value from the earlier
draft is omitted.
The mismatch was narrow. Required actions, resources, arguments, subjects, provenance, and expected
context values stayed intact; extra warning text was inserted in context. Consequently, the decision-
relevant envelope matched in every v1 record even though the strict object contract did not. Conﬁrmatory
v2 then achieved 400/400 full-object matches under the corrected prospectively frozen interface, with a
99.08% trial-level exact lower bound. This does not erase the historical drift or isolate whether the
changed interface, study window, or service behaviour produced the difference. It does establish a second
primary ledger under a criterion ﬁxed before live execution (Table 6; Fig. 3; Fig. 4).

5.5 Operational events, latency, and cost
OpenAI v1 produced two transient aborted attempts: one HTTP 502 and one timeout. Both trial identiﬁers
were later completed through the resume path and appear once in the effectiveness data. Anthropic v1
recorded no aborted attempt. The conﬁrmatory Anthropic gate passed 12/12, after which all 400 trials
ﬁnished without transient, conformance, oracle, or containment aborts. The corrected Gemini gate passed
ten cases before HTTP 429 ended the gate; no deﬁnitive Gemini trial started. Its quota stop is treated as
infrastructure censoring rather than model failure (Google AI for Developers, 2026).
Across the v1 scenario medians, the median-of-medians was 6.662 s for OpenAI and 2.204 s for
Anthropic. The paired Anthropic-minus-OpenAI median difference was − 4.116 s, with a scenario-cluster
bootstrap interval from − 4.285 to -3.569 s. Anthropic conﬁrmatory v2 had a scenario median-of-medians
of 2.285 s and a main-run cost of USD 1.70192; its 12-case preﬂight used an estimated USD 0.05289. The
three 400-trial ledgers cost USD 4.68259 in total, excluding preﬂight gates. Service routing, token
accounting, and network load remain mixed into latency, so v2 is descriptive rather than another paired
comparison (Fig. 5).
5.6 External-corpus and policy-architecture generalisation
Both embedded 12-case gates passed. OpenAI then met the strict extension endpoint in 400/400
scheduled trials. Anthropic met it in 391/400 (97.75%; trial-level exact 95% lower bound 95.77%). All nine

failures belonged to the benign scenario ADJ-BANK-U03. In those repetitions, the action, resource,

6 Discussion
6.1 A mediator needs its own adversarial test
Putting a deterministic check after generation removes ﬁnal authority from the model. That is a useful
boundary, not a guarantee. The survivors show where a seemingly broad corpus was thin: subject binding,
approval, time, exception handling, and reset state. None of those weaknesses is repaired by a safer
model response.
Reset behaviour deserves separate attention. Most policy tests look only at the current decision. A
stateful harness can pass those tests and still carry poisoned state into the next case. The one mutant
detected only by the reset probe is small evidence for treating lifecycle controls as part of the policy
surface.
6.2 Exact schemas can be stricter than authorisation needs
This provider discrepancy wasn’t one about the request itself. It was one about where commentary
resides in relation to the object itself. It’s an important distinction when hashing, caching, signing, and
comparing calls verbatim.
One way to achieve this is through a slim envelope. Sign the parts that affect authority; keep the
commentary outside the envelope. This avoids brittle rejections, but also creates a new requirement:
never consult the non-signed channel for authority under any circumstances.
The conﬁrmatory run changes the evidential status, not the historical observation. Exact conformance
became repeatable under the corrected interface and frozen pre-policy gate, yet the design changed
between v1 and v2. The study can therefore claim a successful prospective replication of the compound
endpoint, but not that one isolated mechanism caused the 109 earlier mismatches to disappear.
6.3 Evidence order is part of reproducibility
Page 13/22

[[page 14]]
The v1 gate discrepancy is a cautionary detail. A corrected runner can be better than the runner that
generated the evidence, yet it cannot rewrite history. The prospectively frozen Anthropic replication now
tests that corrected path directly. Keeping the original mismatch, post-run audit, correction, freeze, and
new ledger in chronological order is more informative than replacing v1 with the passing result. The same
principle applies to retries and quota stops.
6.4 Generalisation is endpoint-speciﬁc
The extension removes the narrowest version of the one-corpus, one-policy limitation, but it does not
produce one global pass or fail. Policy decisions and state transitions transferred across two corpora and
two independent mediation architectures for every proposal that crossed the interface gate. Exact
proposal conformance did not transfer perfectly for Anthropic, and the external corpus alone was weak at
killing policy mutants. These are different endpoints and should remain separate.
The nine strict failures are useful evidence about fail-closed design. A semantically plausible annotation
was not silently accepted as part of an authority-bearing object. Availability fell for one case, but synthetic
state did not change. Systems that need annotation should provide a separate non-authoritative channel
rather than relax the signed object after observing a rejection.
The capability engine reduces dependence on ordered-rule semantics, yet both policies still share the
same scenario projection, sandbox, proposal gate, and transition infrastructure. A third-party
implementation and an interactive external trajectory benchmark would be stronger next tests.
6.5 Engineering implications
Separate proposal from execution.
Tie approval to the complete decision envelope comprising subject, resource, goal, time, and
delegation.
Validate both the decision and the resultant state, including explicit reset checks for multistep
decisions.
Intentfully select between full-object and decision-envelope validation.
Maintain transient proposals and quota stops in addition to the security denominator.
Repeat mutation checks whenever there is a change in policies, schema of tools, or lifecycle behavior.
Record policy, interface, and mutation generalization separately as different endpoints.
7 Threats to Validity and Limitations
Construct validity. The prompt supplies the operation to transcribe. The study cannot support claims
about autonomous tool selection, refusal, planning quality, or resistance to an injection that must be
interpreted before an action is chosen.
Page 14/22

[[page 15]]
Oracle validity. Oﬄine replay veriﬁes consistency with deterministic sandbox semantics, but it uses the
same implementation family. A separately written oracle would give stronger protection against common-
mode defects.
Statistical validity. Repetitions are repeated API calls on ﬁxed cases. Cluster summaries reduce pseudo-
replication; they do not turn the hand-built scenarios or threat families into random samples.
External validity. Synthetic ﬁles, payments, memory, egress, and delegation do not reproduce the failure
modes of a production cloud tenant, bank, hospital, or MLOps platform. The second corpus is traceably
derived from AgentDojo, but it projects source tasks onto terminal operations rather than replaying full
interactive trajectories.
Provider breadth. The extension runs OpenAI and Anthropic prospectively through the same compound
endpoint, but only one model identiﬁer per provider was tested. The ﬁrst Anthropic ledger remains
historical secondary evidence, and Gemini has no deﬁnitive denominator. The results do not support a
general provider ranking.
Mutation validity. Separate 12-operator families now cover ordered rules and capabilities, but they are still
plausible ﬁrst-order faults rather than an exhaustive fault model. The low external-core scores also show
that source diversity does not guarantee mutation adequacy. Equivalent mutants and higher-order
interactions remain untested.
Operational validity. Model identiﬁers, API behaviour, pricing, routing, and quotas can change. The cost
and latency observations belong to the recorded study window.
8 Conclusion
PolicyFaultBench provides a way to interrogate the small layer between a model proposal and an
executed side effect. Its strongest evidence is not a universal safety rate. It is the combination of
preserved historical ledgers, policy mutation, state replay, explicit interface contracts, and a prospective
conﬁrmation followed by a preregistered 800-trial external-corpus and policy-architecture extension. That
extension did not satisfy its 800/800 rule: nine Anthropic proposals were rejected for exact-object drift.
The rejection was fail-closed, while all 791 admitted proposals agreed under both policies and produced
veriﬁed state transitions. Low external-core mutation scores further show why execution success cannot
substitute for fault adequacy. The next useful extension should remove the frozen operation from the
prompt, retain independent mediation, and test open-ended planning under untrusted content with a
separately implemented oracle.
