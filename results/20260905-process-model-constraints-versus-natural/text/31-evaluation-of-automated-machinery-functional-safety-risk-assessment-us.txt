ABSTRACT Accurate classification of required Performance Level (PLr ) for hazard scenarios in machinery
functional safety risk assessment demands expert-level interpretation of technical standards (e.g. ISO
12100 and ISO 13849-1) and functional safety domain knowledge. To address this, a prompt-layered
architecture is proposed, introducing a structured, multi-stage prompting design that operationalizes
the ISO 13849-1 risk graph through deterministic Natural Language Processing and Natural Language
Understanding (NLP–NLU) logic. Building on this architecture, this study develops and evaluates a novel,
structured, multi-stage, standards-aligned Large Language Model (LLM) prompting framework engineered
to explicitly map the sequential, conditional decision logic of the ISO 13849-1 risk graph. It represents
a novel application of deterministic NLP–NLU prompting to non-reasoning LLMs, implementing a
standards-aligned functional safety workflow that systematically evaluates rule-constrained and retrieval-
augmented prompts for reproducible PL r classification. The evaluation pipeline systematically tests LLMs
for PL r determination across modular prompts (zero-shot, rule-based, retrieval-augmented, and hybrid),
leveraging a 7,800-scenario dataset and three complementary test regimes namely, baseline, robustness,
and cross-architecture comparison, to characterize model accuracy and stability. Results show that explicit
ISO rule-based prompting lifts PL r accuracy from less than 50% (zero-shot) to about 99% with mid-scale,
instruction-tuned models. Retrieval augmentation adds little beyond rules and can increase latency, while
hybrid prompts do not consistently improve stability. These findings provide clear deployment guidance:
use rule-based prompting with mid-scale instruction-tuned LLMs for reliable, low-latency industrial safety
assessments, and consider on-premise or domain-adapted open-source models to maintain data sovereignty
and regulatory compliance.

II. BACKGROUND AND RELATED WORK
Risk assessment in industrial contexts, particularly in func-
tional safety, has long relied on expert-led procedures guided
by standards such as ISO 12100 [2] and ISO 13849 [3].
However, the emergence of LLMs has opened new opportu-
nities to automate and augment these tasks through advanced
natural language understanding and reasoning. This section
first presents a background on machinery functional safety
risk assessment in section II-A. Following this, a review of
prior research and related work on the application of LLMs in
safety-critical domains, covering their use in hazard analysis,
prompt engineering, retrieval augmentation, and their role in
functional safety risk classification is presented.
A. MACHINERY FUNCTIONAL SAFETY RISK ASSESSMENT
Machinery safety is a concern for suppliers and manu-
facturing businesses throughout the world, and there are
a wide variety of regulations [1] which help to protect
people, assets and property from harm. Most industrial
manufacturing environments involve complex assemblies of
heterogeneous machinery, each requiring compliance with
applicable safety regulations [9], [10]. The EU Machinery

Directive (2006/42/EC) [1] mandates that such equipment
meet essential health and safety requirements as a prereq-
uisite for CE marking a legal obligation for market access
within the European Union. A core component of this
compliance process is a structured hazard analysis and risk
assessment for each machine covered by the Directive.
ISO 12100 [2] is an international standard which specifies
basic terminology, principles and methodology for achieving
safety in the design of machinery. It specifies principles
of risk assessment and risk reduction to help designers
in achieving this objective. Procedures are described for
identifying hazards, estimating and evaluating risks during
relevant phases of the machine life cycle. For instance,
a hazard refers to something that potentially causes harm and
the risk is a combination of the probability and severity of the
harm. For example, a sharp part is a hazard. When it is in a
prominent position, it creates a risk.
1) RISK ASSESSMENT
The iterative process of risk assessment and reduction is
shown in Fig. 1. A risk assessment follows a series of logical
steps to identify and examine any potential hazards associated
with machinery. The process starts with hazard identification
in terms of machine space, time, and usage limits. The hazard
is then estimated by risk elements such as harm severity
(S), occurrence frequency (F), and avoidance or limitation
probability (P). Based on the information obtained, this risk
will be finally evaluated whether it is acceptable. If not, risk
reduction measures are required. The whole process is called

risk assessment. Iteration of this process can be necessary
to eliminate hazards as far as practicable and to adequately
reduce risks by the implementation of protective measures.
Protective measures play an important role in risk reduction.
Such measures include protection devices and safety controls,
the combination of which is called the Safety-Related Part of
the Control System (SRP/CS) [3], [11].
2) SAFETY FUNCTION
Safety functions (SFs) are the machine functions that cause
an immediate increase in risk upon failure [3]. A single SF
can be implemented by multiple SRP/CSs. A single SRP/CS
may also implement multiple SFs, such as prevention of
accidental start-up and limits regarding safety parameters in
temperature and pressure. For example, the control system
shuts down the furnace fire when the boiler pressure reaches a
dangerous value. If this function fails, excessive pressure will
lead to an explosion. In this scenario, safety depends on the
SRP/CS performing the correct function. Each SF is tasked
with reducing the risk of one or more hazardous events. It is
necessary to consider each hazard and its corresponding SF
in the design. The risk assessment results influence the PL r
value for the SF.
B. REQUIRED PERFORMANCE LEVEL-PL R
PLr is the risk reduction expectation required for the
implementation of an SF and can be determined by the
risk graph (cf. Fig. 2). A risk graph is a grading-based risk
estimation method with parameters S, F and P corresponding
to the severity of harm, the duration or frequency of operator
exposure to the hazard area, and the probability of avoiding
the hazard respectively [11]. The result represents the level of
risk without protection from the safety system. It is also used
to determine the performance level of the safety function that
is needed to reduce the risk to a permissible level. Figure 2
shows the structure of the risk graph. The severity (S) of
harm is divided into S1-slight and S2-serious. Only slight
reversible harm, serious irreversible harm, and fatalities are
considered when estimating the levels of harms [11]. The
normal recovery process is generally used as the basis for

evaluating the severity of harm to people. Slight harm is
usually recoverable, while serious is not. For example, fatigue
and slipping are categorized as S1, and amputation and death
are categorized as S2.
The frequency (F) or time of exposure to the hazard is
classified as F1-seldom or short time and F2-frequent or long
time. This parameter is a measure of the time spent in the
danger zone. As per [3], when the operator is present in
the area more frequently than once every 15 minutes, it is
considered as F2 level. If for automated processing machines,
the operator needs to intervene only once a month, F1 is the
obvious choice.
The possibility (P) of avoiding hazards is divided into
categories P1 and P2, which are determined based on whether
the hazard can be identified or prevented. If it is possible to
avoid an accident under certain circumstances, P1 is chosen,
but if it is almost impossible to avoid, then P2 is chosen.
Factors that affect parameter P include the speed of the
hazardous situation leading to harm, any awareness of risk,
and the human ability to escape. For example, the speed
of machine operation is limited so that potential accidents
are delayed, and the operator has the opportunity to react
and leave the zone. As can be seen from the graph (Fig. 2),
combining these parameters increases the risks from low to
high (i.e., PL r -a to PL r -e, where PL r -e is the highest level
required for SF and the most expensive to implement).
C. DESIGN RELEASE
After determining that the risk is unacceptable, the SRP/CS
is designed as a safety guard. All SFs required to prevent
the risk are first identified. The properties of each SF are
clearly defined. For example, the machine is stopped when
the safety door is opened and automatic restarts are prevented
during this period. Then the PL r is determined for every SF.
After implementation and evaluation (e.g. in [12]), the match
between the actual PL and PL r is verified. The former must
be higher or equal to the latter. This is the condition under
which the design can be ‘‘released’’. Please note that the
entire process of risk assessment and determination of PL and
PLr are discussed so far.
However, in the study presented in this paper, the LLM is
presented with natural language descriptions of machinery
hazard scenarios that implicitly or explicitly indicate the
three risk parameters defined in ISO 13849, namely severity
(S), frequency (F), and possibility of avoidance (P). The
model is evaluated for its ability to interpret these factors and
accurately classify the corresponding required Performance
Level (PL r ), thereby demonstrating its potential to support
functional safety risk assessment in accordance with ISO
12100 and ISO 13849-1. This evaluation is essential to deter-
mine whether general-purpose LLMs can reliably replicate
expert-level judgment in PLr classification, a prerequisite for
integrating AI into scalable, standards-compliant functional
safety workflows where manual assessments are often
inconsistent, resource-intensive, and difficult to reproduce.
Building on this background, the following section reviews
related work on AI-assisted safety assessments, rule-based
prompt engineering, and retrieval-augmented reasoning in
the context of structured decision-making and industrial risk
classification.
D. LLM APPLICATIONS IN SAFETY-CRITICAL HAZARD
ANAL YSIS
LLMs, especially those like GPT-4 [13], have demonstrated
promising capabilities in automating hazard identification
and initial risk estimation. Several recent studies illustrate
how LLMs can accelerate tasks that were previously depen-
dent on expert input.
Nouri et al. [14] developed a GPT-4 pipeline to generate
Hazard Analysis and Risk Assessment (HARA) tables
for automotive systems. Their approach decomposed the
analysis into subtasks, hazard identification, scenario gen-
eration, severity classification, each guided by task-specific
prompts. While effective at producing draft assessments,
the approach emphasized the continued necessity of expert
validation. Qi et al. [15] assessed ChatGPT’s capacity
for System-Theoretic Process Analysis (STPA) in automo-
tive braking. Their findings showed that although naive
prompting yielded poor results, domain-specific prompts and
human collaboration allowed LLMs to identify hazards more
effectively than some experts. Similarly, Collier et al. [16]
found that ChatGPT could enumerate a broad range of failure
scenarios in consumer product safety but often delivered
weak or unsupported risk judgments. The study in [8]
demonstrates that integrating LLMs like ChatGPT within
a Human-In-The-Loop (HITL) framework can enhance
functional safety risk assessment under ISO 12100, achieving
high accuracy and efficiency through expert validation.
However, it lacks a systematic experimental evaluation using
a large, standardized dataset on LLMs to independently
quantify model performance.
These findings underscore the limits of generic LLM use
in safety-critical domains and motivate structured prompt
design and additional context-aware techniques, explored
next. Effective interaction with LLMs in risk analysis tasks
often hinges on structured, rule-based prompting. Without
domain-specific guidance, LLMs may produce inconsistent
or misleading outputs.
Nouri et al. [14] employed format-constrained subtasks
and predefined templates to improve output consistency.
Diemert and Weber [17] introduced a co-hazard analysis
(CoHA) framework that combined iterative Q&A with
ChatGPT and domain rules, enhancing the coverage and
creativity of hazard identification. Sammour et al. [18]
further demonstrated that structured prompts significantly
influenced LLM accuracy and interpretability on safety
certification questions. However, they also noted that no
single prompt structure performs optimally across all task
types. These insights reveal that while prompt engineering
improves LLM reliability, it does not fully solve the problem

E. RETRIEVAL AUGMENTED GENERATION (RAG) FOR
DECISION SUPPORT
RAG enhances LLM outputs by supplying relevant, external
knowledge, improving both accuracy and domain alignment.
Xue et al. [19] integrated Qwen-2.5 with a curated fire
safety regulation database to improve factuality in fire
engineering queries. Meng et al. [20] applied advanced
RAG pipelines to toxicology assessments, achieving higher
scientific fidelity through query rewriting and evidence
grounding. In the automotive domain, Hillen et al. [21]
developed the LASAR system, which combines scenario
generation with catalog-based retrieval to guide LLMs in
Hazard and Risk Assessment (HARA).
These RAG-based approaches reinforce the need for
interpretability and traceability in LLM outputs, topics
that prompt engineering strategies increasingly attempt to
address.
F. PROMPT ENGINEERING AND MODEL
INTERPRETABILITY
In safety-critical applications, interpretability is as vital
as accuracy. Well-designed prompts can elicit stepwise
reasoning, uncertainty estimates, and justifications from
LLMs, aiding human validation.
Hillen et al. [21] and Nouri et al. [14] found that instructing
LLMs to explain their severity ratings improved reviewer
confidence and auditability. Sammour et al. [18] showed that
prompt-induced variations could swing model performance
by over 13%. Y et, the benefits of added prompt complexity
often depend on the task structure, indicating a need for both
prompt-task matching and systematic evaluation.
Prompt strategies, however, are only one component.
In functional safety, risk classification frameworks such
as PL r demand end-to-end integration of structured logic,
thereby necessitating AI systems tailored to such regulatory
schemes.
G. AI-BASED RISK ASSESSMENT IN FUNCTIONAL SAFETY
Functional safety risk assessment in machinery domains is
typically governed by ISO 12100 and ISO 13849, which
define a logic-driven method for estimating required Perfor-
mance Level (PL r ) based on hazard severity, exposure, and
avoidance. Traditional assessments rely on expert judgment,
limiting scalability and reproducibility. The work in [22]
proposed an early AI assistant for PL r estimation using a
TextCNN-LSTM network. While achieving∼ 80% accuracy
on internal datasets, the model lacked robustness to diverse
language formulations. To address benchmarking gaps, the
work presented in [7] introduced a structured, open-access
dataset of 7,800 annotated machinery hazard scenarios
aligned with ISO 12100 and ISO 13849. This enables
reproducible PL r prediction experiments with state-of-the-
art LLMs.
Follow-up studies, such as a short paper in [23] applied
zero-shot, role-based, and retrieval-augmented rules to the
dataset. Results showed that rules with retrieval augmentation
outperformed Zero-shot and role-based prompts, but also
exposed variability across prompt styles. This motivates
a deeper investigation of LLM robustness, prompting
techniques, and hybrid architectures, as undertaken in the
present work. Earlier work on model-driven automation in
automotive safety, such as the synthesis and timing analysis
of AUTOSAR-based embedded systems, demonstrated how
standardized representations enable deterministic reasoning
and tool-supported verification across system layers [24],
[25]. These principles of structured modeling and formal-
ized logic directly parallel the rule-constrained prompting
approach explored in this study.
In summary, despite these advances, current research
suffers from three key limitations: lack of systematic
benchmarking on PL r -aligned tasks on general purpose
LLMs, minimal integration of ISO standard risk classification
rules (with or without) retrieval augmentation and structured
prompting and poor generalizability. To address these gaps,
this paper introduces the first reproducible benchmark for
LLM-based PL r estimation using a publicly available,
standards-aligned dataset [7]. Multiple LLMs, spanning
proprietary, open-access, and open-source architectures, are
evaluated under diverse prompting and retrieval strategies to
assess their accuracy, robustness, and sensitivity. In doing
so, the study aims to inform the development of scalable,
standards-aligned AI tools for functional safety. Its findings
are expected to guide the practical integration of LLMs into
industrial workflows, including machinery risk assessment
and automated PL r determination.
III. METHODOLOGICAL FRAMEWORK OVERVIEW
This study presents the first modular, standards-aligned
Natural Language Processing (NLP) and Natural Language
Understanding (NLU) evaluation pipeline designed to bench-
mark the performance of LLMs in classifying the required
Performance Level (PL r ) for machinery hazard scenarios
in accordance with ISO 12100 and ISO 13849-1. The
framework integrates structured linguistic input (hazard
descriptions) with domain-specific semantic targets (PL r
classes), positioning the task as a deterministic text classi-
fication and comprehension problem rather than a reasoning
exercise. The proposed configuration constitutes a prompt-
layered, rule-encoded architecture explicitly optimized for
deterministic safety classification, transforming conventional
retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) into a scene-specific,
standards-constrained evaluation workflow that emphasizes
syntactic regularity, rule grounding, and reproducibility
within safety-critical NLP workflows.

the reliability and scalability of LLMs in safety-critical
PLr classification, representing, to our knowledge, the first
standards-aligned, quantitatively validated methodology for
this domain.
A. RAG IMPLEMENTATION
Building upon the general pipeline shown in Figure 3, this
subsection details the retrieval-augmentation mechanism that
operationalizes the RAG path. The overall RAG pipeline and
the default retrieval parameters are illustrated in Figure 4.
RAG is integrated into the PLr evaluation pipeline to examine
whether augmenting ISO-rule prompts with precedent hazard
examples improves deterministic classification. The method
adopts a hybrid retrieval design coupling dense semantic
search with symbolic domain filtering, thereby ensuring
both topical relevance and compliance with ISO 13849-1
semantics. A persistent ChromaDB collection of ≈1,020
curated hazards, drawn from the 7,800-scenario corpus,
serves as the retrieval index. For each query scenario, the
system over-retrieves top-k candidates by vector similarity
and applies a semantic threshold ssem ≥ 0.70, followed
by lexical Jaccard filtering (J ≥ 0.30) and optional S/F/P-
triplet gating to ensure consistency with ISO 13849-1 risk
parameters. After deduplication and ranking, the top-M
exemplars (M=3 by default) are compacted into structured
snippets ({rag_examples}) injected into the prompt
(cf. Figure 3).
The parameter settings used throughout the RAG pipeline
reflect a deliberate balance between over-retrieval and
precision filtering. The over-retrieval size k=20 ensures a
sufficiently diverse candidate pool for downstream filtering,
avoiding early exclusion of semantically relevant but lex-
ically variant examples. The semantic similarity threshold
ssem ≥ 0.70 (where ssem=1−d, derived from embedding
distance) filters candidates that are only loosely related to
the query scenario. Lexical Jaccard filtering with a threshold
J (A, B) ≥ 0.30 further removes spurious candidates that
share minimal token overlap with the input scenario, thereby

B. TASK-SPECIFIC PROMPT LA YERING AND SCENE
OPTIMIZATION
The methodological core of this study lies in the struc-
tured prompt-layering architecture developed specifically for
deterministic PL r classification in machinery safety scenes.
Unlike generic prompt-based evaluations, this framework
explicitly encodes ISO 13849-1 risk-graph rules, integrates
scene-relevant contextual factors (e.g., hazard type, task,
and user role), and supports controlled retrieval-augmented
prompting through structured precedent examples. Optional
task-decomposition instructions further guide model com-
prehension toward rule-consistent, context-aware classifi-
cation. Each prompt configuration builds incrementally,
from unstructured zero-shot to fully structured rule-based,
retrieval-augmented, and instruction-guided variants, allow-
ing systematic ablation and attribution of performance
gains. This scene-optimized layering enables evaluation
under both surface-form shift (e.g., paraphrased exposure
conditions) and rule-conflict conditions, ensuring robustness,
interpretability, and reproducibility. To the best of our knowl-
edge, this represents the first prompt-layered NLP–NLU
architecture specifically engineered for deterministic safety
classification under ISO 13849-1 compliance.
IV. EXPERIMENTAL SETUP AND SYSTEM DESIGN
The evaluation pipeline developed in this study is opera-
tionalized through a modular system architecture designed
for reproducibility, real-time assessment, and prompt exper-
imentation. The core orchestration engine is built using
LangGraph [30], which enables dynamic flow control for
prompt routing and evaluation logic. Modular prompting
strategies such as rule-based, RAG, and hybrid approaches
are implemented using LangChain chains [31], allowing
seamless reconfiguration across tests. A vector database
(ChromaDB) [32] enables high-throughput semantic retrieval
of domain-specific hazard precedents for RAG-based scenar-
ios. Retrieved contexts are injected via prompt placeholders
to test reasoning performance under analogical guidance.
This modular setup mirrors realistic deployment conditions
while enabling systematic control over prompting strategies,
model selection, and input variants.

3) DATASET ANNOTATION, QUALITY ASSURANCE, AND
RETRIEVAL PREPARATION
The dataset was constructed using a systematic annotation
and validation workflow to ensure physical, contextual,
and semantic consistency of all hazard scenarios. Each
origin–consequence pair was evaluated according to a
three-step plausibility protocol. First, combinations had to
comply with fundamental physical laws and mechanical
feasibility (e.g., eliminating impossible interactions such
as acoustic noise causing loss of balance). Second, every
hazard origin required a traceable mechanism of injury
linking it causally to the stated consequence, ensuring
that the described energy transfer or exposure process was
realistic. Third, expert judgment was applied to enforce
domain-specific common sense, eliminating highly improb-
able or contextually implausible scenarios even if they were
not physically impossible [7].
Each accepted origin–consequence pairing was then
automatically expanded into multiple contextualized hazard
scenarios by varying the parameters User Type, Task, and
Environment. These contextual factors were further combined
with the standardized ISO 13849-1 risk graph parameters,
namely Severity (S), Frequency (F), and Possibility of Avoid-
ance (P). The corresponding Required Performance Level
(PLr ) was deterministically computed from the qualitative
ISO 13849-1 risk graph, ensuring that identical S/F/P triples
yield identical PL r values. This mapping was embedded
directly in the dataset generation script and cross-validated
through automated schema and consistency checks.
Annotation and quality control were performed in two
complementary phases. During the automated generation
phase, a validation script verified schema compliance,
uniqueness of hazard identifiers, and legal S/F/P com-
binations. During manual review, two domain experts
independently conducted spot checks across all ten ISO
12100 hazard categories to verify plausibility and PL r
consistency. Disagreements were discussed and resolved by
consensus with a third reviewer. This hybrid process ensured
that the resulting dataset adhered to both the formal logic of

F. TYPES OF EVALUATION TESTS
To comprehensively assess the capabilities and understand
the limitations of general-purpose LLMs in automating
PLr determination for industrial machinery safety, a robust
and multifaceted evaluation framework was developed. This
framework facilitated a series of targeted tests, each designed
to explore distinct aspects of model performance, general-
ization, and robustness within both realistic and controlled
environments.
1) TEST 1: COMPREHENSIVE BASELINE EVALUATION
This test establishes a foundational performance benchmark
by applying all experimental configurations across a 567-
case dataset of hazard scenarios. To ensure representativeness
and mitigate potential sampling bias, these scenarios were
selected using a randomized proportional method from the
entire dataset of 7800 scenarios [7], [26], reflecting the
distribution of different hazard types within the comprehen-
sive dataset. The evaluation utilizes three OpenAI models
namely gpt-4.1-nano, gpt-4.1-mini, and gpt-4o-mini under
all four prompting strategies. This large sample size and
comprehensive inclusion of hazard types provide a robust
statistical basis, reflecting typical real-world deployment
conditions.
2) TEST 2: FOCUSED EVALUATION ON ORIGINAL DATASET
This test evaluates each model-strategy configuration over
100 hazard scenarios, repeated across three independent runs.
Each run yields a summary metric (e.g., mean accuracy),
resulting in three values per configuration. The standard
deviation across these run-level metrics quantifies the vari-
ability in model performance due to randomness in sampling
or inference. While the small number of repetitions limits
statistical power, this approach supports basic robustness
assessment.
3) TEST 3: CROSS-MODEL GENERALIZATION
This test benchmarks alternative LLMs, specifically
Llama3.2-8B-Instruct (deployed via Ollama) and DeepSeek-
Chat, against OpenAI’s models (gpt-4.1-mini) to assess
the generalizability of observed performance trends across
diverse model architectures. This evaluation, conducted
using 100 samples from the original dataset under identical
experimental setups, aims to probe architectural strengths and
limitations beyond a single model family.
Together, these evaluations collectively establish a com-
prehensive testing matrix that systematically investigates the
impact of varying model architectures, prompt engineering
strategies, and input formulations. This integrated framework
facilitates a principled analysis of LLM performance in struc-
tured, safety-critical classification tasks, thereby yielding
critical insights for the development of robust AI-assisted risk
assessment pipelines.
4) METRICS AND STATISTICAL PROTOCOL
The primary evaluation metric is exact-match accuracy on
PLr ∈ {a,..., e}, computed against ground-truth labels
derived from ISO 13849-1 Annex A. Accuracy is defined
as the proportion of correctly predicted PL r classifications
across all test scenarios. To assess robustness, each exper-
imental condition (prompt type × model configuration)
is repeated three times on independent random draws of
n=100 scenarios, reporting the mean and standard deviation
across repetitions. For the full stratified test (n=567), 95%
binomial confidence intervals are also computed to quantify
the statistical reliability of observed accuracies.
Runtime performance is reported as both average
per-sample time and cumulative batch processing time
to enable comparative efficiency analysis. All evaluations
are performed under fixed decoding parameters (temper-
ature = 0, top-p = 1.0, constant max tokens), ensuring
fully deterministic outputs and isolating the effects of
prompt design rather than stochastic sampling. This statistical
protocol provides reproducible and interpretable performance
estimates across prompt configurations while maintaining
direct comparability between LLM variants and RAG
settings.

rules+ RAG with instructions), likely due to increased
sensitivity to semantic drift introduced by the retrieved
hazard cases. Since RAG involves injecting contextually
similar examples to guide PL r inference, subtle mis-
matches in risk parameters (S, F, P) can mislead model
predictions. This highlights the importance of prompt
consistency and relevance, particularly for lower-capacity
architectures.
The heatmap in Figure 6 further emphasizes model-
prompting interactions. Larger models tend to benefit more
from retrieval and multi-component prompting strategies,
whereas the smallest model, gpt-4.1-nano, remains less
responsive to RAG enhancements and shows its best per-
formance with static rule-based input (i.e., experiment 2 in

2) PROCESSING AND RUNTIME ANALYSIS
Figure 7 illustrates the computational trade-offs associated
with each configuration. Average processing time per sce-
nario ranged from 2.42 seconds (gpt-4.1-nano with rules)
to 4.30 seconds (gpt-4o-mini with rules combined with
RAG and instructions, i.e., experiment 4 in section IV -B4).
Total execution time over 567 scenarios followed simi-
lar trends, with rule-based configurations generally yield-
ing lower runtime than RAG-enhanced ones (i.e., from
experiment 3 and 4).
While gpt-4.1-nano consistently exhibited the shortest
inference time, its accuracy remained significantly below the
other models. In contrast, gpt-4o-mini achieved competitive
accuracy with rules (0.910) but incurred the highest total run-
time under RAG-enhanced conditions (up to 111 minutes).
The high total runtime for gpt-4o-mini under RAG-enhanced
conditions results from longer token sequences introduced
by retrieval-augmented prompts, which include both ISO
rules and multiple retrieved hazard examples. As a larger
model with more complex decoding operations, gpt-4o-
mini requires more computation per token, making it
more sensitive to prompt length and structure than smaller
counterparts.
3) SUMMARY AND IMPLICATIONS
The baseline evaluation demonstrates that model scale and
prompting strategy jointly influence classification perfor-
mance and efficiency. Rule-based prompting emerged as a
robust, interpretable method that offers strong performance
with lower computational overhead. Retrieval-augmented
prompting was particularly effective for larger models but
introduced considerable latency.
Among all configurations, gpt-4.1-mini with either rules
prompting only (i.e., experiment 2 in section IV -B2) or
RAG plus rules prompting strategy (i.e., experiment 3 in
section IV -B3) achieved near-expert accuracy (≥ 0.990)
with moderate runtime, offering the most favorable balance
between precision and throughput. These findings identify
rule-based prompting with a mid-scale LLM as a strong
candidate for integration into real-world PL r assessment
pipelines where both accuracy and latency are mission-
critical.

E. THREATS TO VALIDITY
Despite the rigor of the experimental design, several potential
threats to validity are recognized. These do not undermine the
study’s conclusions but define the scope of their applicability.
• Internal Validity:The dataset was generated through an
ISO 12100/13849-1–compliant process and validated by
domain experts, yet subjective decisions in plausibility
filtering may persist. Deterministic schema validation
and rule-based PL r assignment reduce inconsistencies,
though templated natural-language descriptions could
limit lexical diversity and induce minor recognition bias.
Repeated stratified evaluations confirm stable results
across hazard types.
• External Validity: The dataset represents industrial
machinery hazards aligned with ISO 12100 Annex B;
hence, results generalize primarily within this domain.
Broader applicability to non-industrial or hybrid systems
remains to be validated. Nevertheless, consistent model
rankings across architectures indicate robustness of the
underlying reasoning patterns.
• Construct Validity: PLr classification accuracy serves
as an operational proxy for expert safety reasoning.
While objective and standard-aligned, it abstracts away
contextual nuances such as control-system design or
human–machine interaction. Misclassifications may
thus reflect incomplete contextual cues rather than
reasoning errors.
• Statistical Conclusion Validity: The stratified subset
(n=567) preserves the distribution of hazard categories
and PL r levels from the full 7 800-scenario corpus.
Confidence-interval analysis (MoE≤ 2.5 pp) confirms
equivalence of results within statistical uncertainty.
Remaining variance may arise from retrieval quality or
prompt–model coupling, warranting future sensitivity
analysis.
• Reliability and Reproducibility: Experiments were
executed with deterministic decoding (temperature=0),
fixed random seeds, and uniform API configurations.
Minor reproducibility differences may occur due to
version drift in proprietary LLMs. All data, prompts, and
scripts are released to support independent replication.
• Dataset Representation and Augmentation: Canon-
ical, standards-based phrasing was used to isolate
reasoning performance from linguistic variability. While
ensuring comparability, this limits lexical and semantic
diversity. Controlled paraphrasing or generative aug-
mentation can extend this benchmark in future work
to assess generalization under varied linguistic and
contextual conditions.
These considerations delineate the empirical scope of the
presented results and establish a foundation for future studies
on larger, linguistically diversified, and uncertainty-aware
safety datasets. Future work will extend this benchmark
through lexical and semantic augmentation to test linguistic
generalization, complemented by independent expert valida-
tion to externally confirm reasoning fidelity.
F. IMPLICATIONS FOR INDUSTRIAL SAFETY PIPELINES
For embedded or production-grade deployment, rule-based
prompts used with a mid-scale, instruction-tuned model like
gpt-4.1-mini are the most viable. They ensure near-expert
accuracy (0.990–0.993), low variance, and fast inference.
However, models like gpt-4.1-mini and deepseek-chat are
typically accessed via commercial APIs, which poses a
risk to data confidentiality and violates many industrial
data protection policies especially in regulated sectors (e.g.,
manufacturing, rail, or medical devices).
To address this, organizations must ensure that safety-related
data is never transmitted to third-party servers. This requires
the deployment of models locally or within air-gapped
environments. In this context, open-source LLMs such
as llama3.2 offer a privacy-preserving alternative but fall
short of safety-grade accuracy thresholds (≤ 0.410) unless
extensively fine-tuned on domain-specific corpora.
A hybrid strategy may be viable: using commercial
models like deepseek-chat for non-sensitive offline audits
and internal evaluations, while investing in fine-tuning open
models (e.g., llama3.2 or DeepSeek variants, if available for
local deployment) for real-time safety inference. DeepSeek’s

VI. CONCLUSION
This study presents the first systematic and benchmark-driven
evaluation of LLMs for automating PL r classification in the
context of industrial machinery functional safety, applying
deterministic NLP–NLU prompting to non-reasoning LLMs
for standards-compliant risk interpretation. It establishes a
reproducible, standards-aligned evaluation framework that
integrates empirical AI benchmarking with the formal rigor
of ISO 12100 and ISO 13849-1 functional safety processes.
The results demonstrate that model scale, architectural
alignment, and prompt engineering are critical determinants
of classification accuracy, inference latency, and deployment
viability. Together, these contributions establish a founda-
tional pathway toward certifiable and explainable AI systems
for industrial functional safety.
Rule-based prompting consistently achieved the highest
accuracy with minimal variance, especially for instruction-
tuned, mid-scale models like gpt-4.1-mini. Compared to zero-
shot prompting, rule-based prompts improved PL r classifi-
cation accuracy by over 50 percentage points, highlighting
the essential role of structured input for deterministic safety
tasks. RAG provided marginal gains but incurred increased
latency, reducing suitability for real-time deployments. Zero-
shot prompting proved consistently inadequate and should
not be used in safety-critical assessments.
gpt-4.1-mini offered the best trade-off between predictive
accuracy and inference time, making it a strong candidate
for embedded safety applications. deepseek-chat achieved
perfect accuracy with rule-based prompts but suffered from
extreme latency under RAG limiting its practical utility.
In contrast, llama3.2 offered faster runtime but consistently
underperformed, underscoring the limitations of small,
unaligned open-source models in regulated domains.
Reliance on API-based commercial models poses sig-
nificant barriers in sectors governed by strict data protec-
tion regulations, such as manufacturing, rail, and medical
devices. Compliance with standards such as IEC 61508
[37], ISO 12100 [2], ISO 13849-1, and emerging EU
regulations like the Cyber Resilience Act [38] requires that
safety-related data remain within controlled infrastructures.
This mandates the use of on-premise deployments. Although
current open-source models fall short, instruction-aligned
fine-tuning and architectural refinement (e.g., DeepSeek
variants, if made available for local deployment) could enable
privacy-preserving alternatives without sacrificing accuracy.
Future research should prioritize: (i) instruction-tuned
fine-tuning of lightweight open models specifically for func-
tional safety domains; and (ii) explainability enhancements
to support traceability and certifiability of LLM decisions in
safety audits.

PADMA IYENGHAR received the B.E. degree
in computer science in India, in 2003, the M.S.
degree in computer science from Technische
Universität Hamburg (TUHH), Germany, in 2006,
and the Ph.D. degree in computer science from
Osnabrück University (UOS), Germany, in 2012.
In October 2020, she was a Postdoctoral
Researcher at UOS, where she led and contributed
to several publicly funded research and innovation
projects in collaboration with industry partners.
Since 2020, she has been a Senior Functional Safety and Cybersecurity Con-
sultant at innotec GmbH–TÜV Austria Group. From 2021 to 2024, she also
held a Development Professorship (Entwicklungsprofessur) at the University
of Applied Sciences Osnabrück, supported by German Federal Ministry of
Education and Research (BMBF) through the ‘‘Professorinnenprogramm
III.’’ During this period, she held a 50% academic role in teaching and
research alongside her continuing industry position at innotec, where she
now works full-time. She has over 14 years of research and development
experience in model-based software development and embedded systems
engineering, and more than eight years of teaching experience across
various computer science subjects. She has supervised numerous theses
and has published in leading conferences and journals. At innotec GmbH,
she has contributed across all phases of the functional safety lifecycle in
sectors, including machinery, industrial automation, railway systems, and
automotive. She leads projects at the intersection of AI, functional safety,
and cybersecurity.
Dr. Iyenghar is an Active Member of the DKE/AK technical committees
on AI and functional safety (e.g., DKE 914.11: TS 22440). Her academic
honors include the Best Outgoing Student Award and the University Medal
for Excellence in Academics during her undergraduate studies. She is
actively engaged in academic and professional communities, serving as a
guest editor, organizing special sessions at international conferences, and
contributing as a reviewer and editorial board member for several journals.
