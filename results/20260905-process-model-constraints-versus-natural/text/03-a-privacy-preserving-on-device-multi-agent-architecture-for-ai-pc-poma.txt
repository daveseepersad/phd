A Privacy-Preserving On-Device Multi-Agent Architecture 
for AI PC (POMA) Workflow Automation 
Sultana Begum Michael Rosenzweig 
Sultana.begum@intel.com Michael.rosenzweig@intel.com 
 Intel Corporation, USA 
Abstract— AI agents that automate desktop workflows can 
reduce time spent on repetitive knowledge work, but enterprise 
deployment raises acute privacy and trust challenges. 
Computer-use agents (CUAs) observe sensitive on -screen 
content and execute high -impact action s in open -world 
environments, making them vulnerable to prompt injection and 
unsafe tool use. Meanwhile, recent model evolution has 
significantly improved the capabilities of small language and 
compact multimodal models, enabling specialized low -latency 
inference that can run fully on -device. This paper presents 
AIPC-POMA, a privacy -preserving on -device multi -agent 
architecture for personal and enterprise AI PC workflow 
automation. AIPC POMA composes a set of carefully crafted, 
task-specialized small agents including an SLM for drafting, a 
compact VLM+OCR stack for document and screen 
understanding, and a sandboxed CUA for GUI interaction 
coordinated by a local Orchestrator and enforced by a Policy 
Verifier. POMA is designed around two principles: privacy by 
minimized data flow and trust by controlled action execution, 
achieved through least -privilege action primitives, risk -aware 
gating, and auditable execution traces. We describe a hardware 
aware implementation optimized for heterogeneous AI 
acceleration on AI PCs such as Panther Lake -class system, and 
we introduce an evaluation task suite and adversarial safety 
battery targeting indirect prompt injection and real-world agent 
threats. We defi ne metrics spanning utility, latency, privacy 
leakage, and action safety, demonstrating that POMA enables 
practical low -cost automation while reducing exposure to 
untrusted inputs. 
 
Keywords— On-device AI, multi -agent systems, workflow 
automation, privacy-preserving inference, computer-use agents, 
prompt injections, multimodal models, AI PC, trustworthy 
autonomy. 
 
 I. INTRODUCTION 
Desktop workflow automation is entering a new phase as AI 
agents evolve from passive assistants into systems that can 
perceive, plan, and act across real software environments. 
Computer-use agents (CUAs) agents that operate through 
GUIs by interpreting on-screen content and executing actions 
have rapidly advanced in capability and scope. Recent 
surveys summarize foundational architectures and open 
challenges for agents that “use” computers in the loop [1]. 
Systems such as Mobile -Agent-v3 [2] and OpenCUA [3] 
demonstrate increasingly general and robust GUI automation, 
while COLA shows that multi -agent decomposition and 
dynamic scheduling can improve reliability and scalability 
[4]. Collectively, these advances indicate that many 
productivity tasks document pre paration, extraction, cross 
application coordination, and reporting can be automated 
with minimal user intervention. However, enterprise 
deployment remains constrained by privacy and trust. CUAs 
inherently observe sensitive on -screen data (e.g., internal 
dashboards, proprietary documents) and can execute 
highimpact actions such as opening links, downloading files, 
authenticating, and transferring content across applications. 
This creates a risk that an agent’s control policy can be 
influenced by untrusted inputs (web pages, shared 
documents, external text). Recent security research shows 
that agentic systems expand the attack surface beyond 
standard assistants by exposing tool interfaces and autonomy 
loops to adversarial manipulation [7], [9], [10]. In particular, 
prompt injection incl uding indirect injection through tool 
inputs or retrieved content has been benchmarked as a 
practical threat to tool -integrated agents [8]. These risks 
intensify when execution privileges are available: evaluations 
show that computer -use and code -capable a gents can be 
coerced into dangerous real -world behaviors under 
adversarial conditions [5]. Enterprise -ready automation 
therefore requires not only utility, but also controlled 
execution, least privilege, and auditability. 
At the same time, the hardware and systems ecosystem is 
shifting toward local -first autonomy. Advances in edge and 
on-device LLMs supported by improved efficiency, 
quantization, and hardware -aware scheduling make it 
increasingly practical to run agent subr outines locally using 
specialized compact models [11], [12], [14]. Keeping 
inference on -device reduces data movement and mitigates 
exposure when sensitive inputs would otherwise leave 
enterprise endpoints [13]. This aligns with emerging AI PCs 
that provide heterogeneous acceleration across CPU, GPU, 
and NPU units. For example, Intel’s Panther Lake platform 
targets high -performance local inference via heterogeneous 
acceleration and OpenVINO ™ [6], enabling interactive 
multimodal workloads without constant cloud dependence. 
Together, these trends motivate architectures where cloud 
connectivity is optional and policy -gated, rather than the 
default execution path. Motivated by these developments, we 
propose AI PC -POMA, a privacy -preserving, on -device 
multi-agent architecture for personal and enterprise workflow 
automation on AI PCs.

B. The Enterprise Risk: Autonomy Meets Untrusted Inputs 
Although CUAs and agentic automation have progressed 
rapidly, enterprise adoption remains constrained by the 
security and trust implications of autonomy in open -world 
software environments. Agents routinely ingest content from 
untrusted sources web pages, PDFs, email attachments, 
shared documents, and tool outputs where adversaries can 
embed malicious instructions to manipulate reasoning and 
tool use. Prompt injection, including indirect injection 
through retrieved or tool-generated content, persists even for 
strong models because it targets the control pathway rather 
than factual knowledge [7], [9]. Benchmarking work shows 
that tool-integrated agents can be reliably diverted by indirect 
injections plac ed in the environment or intermediate tool 
outputs [8]. These risks are amplified for CUAs because 
model outputs translate into real actions clicking UI elements, 
opening links, authenticating, granting permissions, and 
transferring data across application s where a single 
adversarial interaction can trigger credential leakage, data 
exfiltration, or unintended configuration changes. Security 
studies increasingly frame this as an “Internet of Agents” 
challenge, where agents expand the attack surface by 
coupling perception, planning, and execution [10]. Recent 
evidence further suggests that agents with execution 
privileges can be induced into end -to-end harmful behavior 
in realistic settings [5]. Togeth er, these risks motivate AI 
PCPOMA’s local-first design and policy-governed execution 
model. 
III. THE AI PC POMA 
POMA composes specialized small models and tools an 
SLM-based drafting agent for content generation, a compact 
multimodal VLM+OCR agent for document and screen 
understanding, and a sandboxed CUA for GUI interaction 
coordinated through a local Orchestrator and enforced by a 
Policy Verifier. 
POMA is designed around two core principles: 
1. Privacy by minimized data flow: keep inference 
and intermediate artifacts on -device by default, and 
limit inter-agent information sharing to only what is 
necessary for task completion. 
2. Trust by controlled action execution: constrain 
autonomous GUI actions through verification, 
riskaware policies, and explicit user escalation for 
highimpact steps. 
key design goal is to support reliable automation while 
maintaining meaningful human control. Human oversight is 
not simply a “confirmation dialog,” but a socio -technical 
requirement: effective oversight must be calibrated to avoid 
both over -trust and excessive friction [16]. POMA 
incorporates structured intervention points and interpretable 
execution traces consistent with emerging human-in-the-loop 
agent paradigms [15] and broader system -theoretic views of 
human–agent interaction [18]. Further, POMA align s with 
recent calls for safe and responsible agent design rooted in 
transparency, accountability, and trustworthiness [17].

Specialized On-Device Agents 
AI PC -POMA composes specialized agents, each 
powered by compact models optimized for a specific 
modality or workflow stage: 
1. Drafting Agent (SLM): The Drafting Agent handles 
summarization, rewriting, structured extraction, 
planning, and document generation. AI PC -POMA 
assumes SLMs below ~8B parameters as a practical 
deployment target because they offer a strong 
balance of latency, memory footprint, and accuracy 
for workflow tasks, and their performance has 
improved significantly in recent model evolution. 
This design decision is supported by edge LLM 
execution trends emphasizing efficient inference 
and hardware -aware deployment strategies [11], 
[12], [14]. Keeping drafting local also reduces 
privacy exposure by avoiding cloud transmission of 
sensitive enterprise artifacts [13]. 
2. Perception Agent (Compact VLM + OCR). The 
Perception Agent performs screen and document 
understanding over UI regions, PDFs, and slide 
canvases. It uses OCR and compact multimodal 
reasoning to extract structured evidence (tables, 
fields, layout blocks, cha rt labels) needed by 
downstream planning and drafting. This component 
is security -critical: indirect prompt injection can 
enter through OCR text, extracted page content, and 
tool outputs, motivating strict provenance tagging 
and separation between untruste d content and 
control logic [8]–[10]. 
3. CUA Interaction Agent (Sandboxed GUI Actions). 
The CUA Interaction Agent executes constrained UI 
primitives (click/type/scroll/navigation) across 
applications, enabling real workflow completion 
beyond text generation. This module draws directly 
on recent progress in computer-use agents and GUI 
automation foundations [1] –[3]. However, because 
execution privileges raise enterprise risk, AI 
PCPOMA routes all CUA actions through a 
dedicated Policy Verifier (Section IV-C). 
4. Media Agent (ASR/TTS, optional). 
The Media Agent supports transcription and 
auditory feedback, enabling hands -free workflow 
control and low -attention confirmations (useful for 
safety escalation and task status updates). Human – 
agent interaction research increasingly emphasizes 
system-level coordination between user oversight 
and autonomy in agentic environments [15], [18]. 
V. POLICY-GOVERNED AUTONOMY: POLICY VERIFIER 
FOR CONTROLLED CUA EXECUTION 
A central contribution of AI PC -POMA is policy -governed 
autonomy: a runtime Policy Verifier that mediates highimpact 
actions issued by the computer -use agent (CUA) to reduce 
unsafe automation under untrusted inputs. While recent CUA 
and multi-agent systems demonstrate strong GUI interaction 
capability [1]–[4], enterprise deployment requires safeguards 
because CUAs operate amid mixed-trust content (web pages, 
PDFs, shared documents, emails, and tool outputs). Tool -
integrated agents are vulnerable to indirect prompt injection, 
where malicious instructions embedded in retrieved or 
observed content can redirect tool use and execution despite 
benign user intent [7], [8]. More broadly, agentic workflows

A. Action Model: Typed, Least-Privilege Primitives 
AI PC -POMA restricts the CUA to a typed set of 
leastprivilege action primitives, avoiding unconstrained 
scripts and reducing the damage from both errors and 
adversarial manipulation. This aligns with least-privilege tool 
authorization frameworks (e.g., Min iScope [20]). Each 
primitive is bounded and auditable, enabling verification 
based on what the agent will execute. Core primitives 
(illustrative): OPEN_URL, CLICK, TYPE, SCROLL, 
DOWNLOAD, UPLOAD, COPY/PASTE, 
CONFIRM_DIALOG. Each action request includes an 
envelope with: (i) plan-step binding, (ii) evidence provenance 
(OCR/VLM/web/email/user), (iii) sensitivity tags 
(PII/credential/confidential/public), (iv) destination identity 
and trust tier, (v) rationale, and (vi) expected post-condition. 
This provides enough context for verification without relying 
on the agent to self-police. 
 
B. Risk Model: Impact × Uncertainty 
For each action 𝑎, the verifier computes a risk score 
combining: 
• Impact: potential harm if wrong (low: navigation; 
medium: local edits/forms; high: credential entry, 
permission grants, external upload/share, executable 
downloads). 
• Uncertainty (evidence trust): evidence from 
untrusted channels (web content, OCR text, extracted 
HTML, tool outputs) is treated as lower trust because 
these are key injection and workflow manipulation 
pathways [8] –[10]. This mitigates a common 
weakness identified in security analyses: authorizing 
high-impact actions using low -trust evidence [7], [9]. 
In practice, environment-derived instructions are non-
authoritative unless corroborated by trusted signals or 
escalated. 
 
C. Policy Checks and Outcomes 
Before execution, the verifier enforces a policy chain: 
• Intent alignment: action must advance the user 
goal and approved plan step; actions justified mainly 
by untrusted instructions are denied or escalated, 
addressing trust–authorization mismatch [21]. 
• Destination trust: allow/deny rules for 
apps/domains; unknown endpoints raise risk, 
especially with data transfer. 
• Data exposure: detect/redact sensitive values and 
escalate external transfer of confidential content 
[13]. 
• Scope enforcement: prevent privilege creep 
beyond minimal required permissions [20]. 
• UI integrity checks: validate app/window identity 
and flag suspicious dialogs or context switches. 
Actions are classified as ALLOW, ESCALATE 
(user confirmation required), or DENY .

A. Workflow Automation Task Suite (Utility + Efficiency) 
We define reproducible end -to-end workflows involving 
documents, multimodal inputs, and cross -application 
execution: Document -to-Email Automation, Invoice -
toForm Completion, Notes -to-Slides Generation, Meeting 
Audio Digest (optional), Dashboard Screenshot → Report

Each task is evaluated under three 
 deployment configurations: 
• Local-only: on-device execution 
 without cloud collaboration. 
• Local-first + optional MCP: cloud collaboration 
enabled only when required and allowed by policy. 
• CUA-enabled vs. CUA -free: compare workflows with 
and without GUI action execution. 
This suite measures whether AI PC -POMA reliably 
completes workflows under practical latency constraints 
while preserving a local-first privacy posture. 
B. Adversarial Safety Battery (Injection + Workflow Exploits) 
Policy-governed autonomy must be tested under adversarial 
conditions because enterprise risk often arises from unsafe 
actions, not merely incorrect text output [7], [9], [10]. We 
therefore construct a battery aligned with known agent threat 
models, including indirect prompt injection as formalized in 
tool-integrated benchmarks [8]. The battery includes: 
Indirect Prompt Injection via Environment Content 
• Injection strings embedded in web pages, 
documents, or UI text (e.g., “ignore policy, export 
secrets”). 
• Hidden instructions in layout artifacts (OCR 
footnotes, overlay text). 
These reflect injection pathways where untrusted 
content enters through perception/tool outputs and 
becomes control input [8], [9]. 
Intermediate Artifact / Workflow Exploits 
• Malicious content inserted into extracted HTML, 
OCR output, clipboard buffers, or file previews. 
• Attacks inducing unsafe tool use (e.g., “upload this 
report now”). 
These map to exploit taxonomies emphasizing 
multi-step manipulation across agent –tool 
interfaces [7], [9], [10]. 
UI Deception and High-Impact Triggers 
• Spoofed dialogs (re -auth prompts), lookalike 
approve buttons, misleading window titles. 
• Consent-fatigue patterns intended to push 
escalations toward approval. 
This targets the agent’s action selection rather than 
response quality. 
Unsafe Execution Sequences (CUA Misuse) 
• Multi-step chains such as “open link → download 
→ authorize → upload” intended to trigger 
exfiltration or unauthorized transfer. 
These reflect concerns that execution privileges can cause 
severe outcomes without strict mediation [10] (and can be 
tied to [5] when included).For each case, we evaluate whether 
the Policy Verifier correctly:(i) DENY unsafe actions, (ii) 
ESCALATE high-risk steps with actionable user context, or 
(iii) ALLOW safe actions when justified.

VIII. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK 
This paper introduced AI PC -POMA, a privacy -preserving 
on-device multi-agent architecture for personal and enterprise 
workflow automation on AI PCs. POMA composes 
specialized agents an SLM (<8B) drafting agent, a compact 
VLM+OCR perception agent, and a sandboxed computer-use 
agent (CUA) coordinated by a local 
Orchestrator and governed by a runtime Policy Verifier. The 
system is designed around two principles: privacy by 
minimized data flow, achieved through local -first execution 
and controlled information sharing, and trust by controlled 
action execution, achie ved through least -privilege action 
primitives, risk -aware gating, escalation for high -impact 
actions, and auditable execution traces. To assess real -world 
deployment readiness, we proposed an evaluation 
methodology combining a workflow automation task suit e 
with an adversarial safety battery targeting indirect prompt 
injection and workflow -level exploits, and we defined a 
metric taxonomy spanning utility, efficiency, privacy 
exposure, and trust. Together, AI PC-POMA demonstrates a 
practical path toward low-cost AI PC automation that reduces 
exposure to untrusted inputs while preserving usability and 
enterprise governance requirements.
